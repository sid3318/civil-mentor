## 1. Monetary Policy Framework and Flexible Inflation Targeting

Every modern economy operates on a delicate balance between two macroeconomic imperatives: sustaining rapid output and employment expansion on the one hand, and preserving the purchasing power of its currency on the other. Whereas fiscal policy—conducted by the elected Union and State Governments through taxation, public expenditure, and sovereign borrowing—operates through annual budgets and legislative approvals, **monetary policy** is the process by which a country's central bank (**the Reserve Bank of India [RBI]**) regulates the cost, availability, and direction of money and credit to achieve macroeconomic stability. If an economy overheats with excessive aggregate demand chasing limited productive capacity, monetary policy applies the brakes by raising interest rates and absorbing surplus liquidity (**contractionary or hawkish monetary policy**). Conversely, when private investment stalls, inflation softens, and idle capacity opens up, monetary policy releases the accelerator by lowering interest rates and injecting durable liquidity (**expansionary or dovish monetary policy**).

For the UPSC Civil Services Examination, monetary policy and the Indian banking system together constitute arguably the single highest-yield pillar of Indian Economy across both stages. **Prelims** routinely dissects the statutory architecture of the **Monetary Policy Committee (`MPC`)**, the mechanics of the **Liquidity Adjustment Facility (`LAF`) corridor** (`Repo`, `SDF`, `MSF`, `CRR`, `SLR`, `OMO`, `MSS`), subtle institutional distinctions across commercial banks, cooperative banks, Regional Rural Banks, differentiated banks, and Non-Banking Financial Companies (`NBFCs`), and the prudential rules governing Non-Performing Assets (`NPAs`), Basel III, and insolvency resolution. **Mains GS Paper III**, in parallel, probes deeper structural questions: whether **Flexible Inflation Targeting (`FIT`)**—renewed for a third five-year term in **March 2026**—adequately navigates supply-side food shocks; why monetary transmission lagged for decades before the **External Benchmark Linked Lending Rate (`EBLR`)**; how India engineered a historic turnaround from the **Twin Balance Sheet crisis** (`11.2%` Gross NPA peak in March 2018) to multi-decadal lows (`1.8%` GNPA in March 2026); and what institutional reforms are still required to finance **Viksit Bharat @2047** without igniting shadow-banking fragility.

### Evolution of India's Monetary Policy Framework (1935–2026)

India's monetary policy framework did not emerge fully formed in 2016; it evolved through four distinct historical regimes reflecting the shifting structure of the Indian economy, from state-controlled credit planning to globalised market integration. Understanding *why* each predecessor framework was abandoned explains the exact institutional design of Flexible Inflation Targeting today.

#### 1. Credit Planning and Regulated Interest Rates (1935–1985)

In the first three decades after Independence, the RBI functioned primarily as a developmental financier of India's Five-Year Plans. Interest rates on both bank deposits and loans were **administered directly by the RBI** rather than discovered in markets, statutory preemption of bank resources through high `CRR` and `SLR` financed government deficits, and the fiscal deficit was automatically monetised via **ad hoc Treasury Bills** issued by the Central Government to the RBI. Monetary policy had virtually no independent nominal anchor: credit allocation was directed through selective controls and the **Credit Authorisation Scheme (1965)**, while inflation was treated largely as a structural and supply-side phenomenon.

#### 2. Monetary Targeting with Feedback (1985–1998)

By the early 1980s, chronic deficit monetisation was driving persistent double-digit inflation. In **1985**, the **Sukhamoy Chakravarty Committee** (*Committee to Review the Working of the Monetary System*) recommended replacing ad hoc credit regulation with **Monetary Targeting with Feedback**. Under this framework, the RBI used **broad money (`M3`)** as its primary intermediate target—setting an annual projected growth range for `M3` consistent with expected real GDP growth and a tolerable inflation rate, while using **reserve money (`M0`)** as its operating instrument.

Crucially, however, Monetary Targeting relied on a theoretical assumption from Milton Friedman's monetarism: that the **demand for money is stable and predictable** (meaning a fixed velocity of circulation links `M3` directly to inflation). After the **1991 LPG reforms** opened India's capital account, deregulated interest rates, abolished automatic monetisation of deficits (1994–1997 agreement replacing ad hoc T-Bills with Ways and Means Advances), and spurred financial innovation, the empirical link between `M3` growth and inflation broke down. Capital inflows swelled reserve money unpredictably, rendering single-variable `M3` targeting unreliable.

#### 3. The Multiple Indicator Approach (1998–2014)

Under RBI Governor **Bimal Jalan** in **April 1998**, the RBI formally abandoned pure monetary targeting in favour of the **Multiple Indicator Approach (`MIA`)**. Rather than focusing on a single intermediate variable like `M3`, the RBI tracked a wide dashboard of high-frequency macroeconomic and financial indicators—interest rates across the yield curve, credit growth, fiscal position, trade and balance of payments, exchange rate movements, capital flows, output trends, and inflation (primarily measured then by the **Wholesale Price Index [`WPI`]**). Simultaneously, the RBI operationalised the **Liquidity Adjustment Facility (`LAF`)** in **June 2000** (following Narasimham Committee II recommendations), shifting from direct quantity controls (`CRR`/`SLR`) toward indirect short-term interest-rate management.

While the Multiple Indicator Approach brought welcome flexibility during the high-growth 2000s, it suffered from three fatal flaws exposed sharply after the **2008 Global Financial Crisis**:

- **Absence of a Clear Nominal Anchor:** Tracking dozens of indicators simultaneously meant no single metric held the central bank accountable. When CPI inflation remained stuck near or above **9%–11% between 2009 and 2013**, markets could not judge whether the RBI prioritised growth, exchange-rate stability, or price stability.
- **Wrong Inflation Gauge (`WPI` instead of `CPI`):** Relying on WPI—which excludes services altogether and under-weights household food consumption—masked severe retail cost-of-living erosion faced by Indian households, de-anchoring inflation expectations and driving household savings out of financial banks into physical gold.
- **Excessive Discretion and Fiscal Dominance:** Monetary decisions rested solely with the RBI Governor (advised only by a non-binding Technical Advisory Committee constituted in 2005), creating transparency gaps and vulnerability to informal government pressure for lower interest rates.

#### 4. Transition to Flexible Inflation Targeting (2014–2026)

To establish institutional credibility and anchor inflation expectations permanently, RBI Governor **Raghuram Rajan** constituted the **Expert Committee to Revise and Strengthen the Monetary Policy Framework** in September 2013 under Deputy Governor **Dr. Urjit R. Patel**. Submitting its seminal report in **January 2014**, the **Urjit Patel Committee** recommended four transformative reforms:

1. Adopt **Headline Consumer Price Index – Combined (`CPI-C`)** as the sole nominal anchor of monetary policy (replacing WPI), because households make wage, savings, and consumption decisions based on retail prices.
2. Establish a **Flexible Inflation Targeting (`FIT`)** mandate centered on **`4%` CPI-C inflation with a symmetrical tolerance band of `± 2%` (`2%` to `6%`)**.
3. Shift rate-setting authority from the individual RBI Governor to a statutory, collective **Monetary Policy Committee (`MPC`)** bound by transparent voting records and published minutes.
4. Define explicit **accountability and failure triggers** (three consecutive quarters outside the tolerance band), requiring a formal written explanation to the Government.

Following the committee's roadmap, the Government of India and the RBI first signed a bilateral **Monetary Policy Framework Agreement (`MPFA`)** on **20 February 2015**. Parliament then elevated the framework to full statutory force through the **Finance Act, 2016** (effective **27 June 2016**), which amended the **`Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934`**—revising its **Preamble** and inserting **Chapter IIIF (`Sections 45ZA to 45ZO`)**.

### Statutory Architecture of Flexible Inflation Targeting and the MPC

Every single statutory clause in **Chapter IIIF (`Sections 45ZA–45ZO`)** of the `RBI Act, 1934` is prime Prelims territory because UPSC routinely swaps who notifies the target, who appoints the external members, how ties are broken, and what constitutes failure.

#### 1. Amended Preamble and Dual Hierarchy of Objectives

The amended Preamble of the `RBI Act, 1934` explicitly establishes a **hierarchical mandate**—not an equal-weight dual mandate like the US Federal Reserve:

> "The primary objective of the monetary policy is to maintain price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth." — Amended Preamble, Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 (Finance Act, 2016)

Notice the deliberate adjective **"Flexible"** in Flexible Inflation Targeting. A *strict* inflation targeter would hike rates ruthlessly to keep inflation at `4.0%` every single month regardless of output collapse. India's **Flexible** framework allows the MPC to absorb temporary supply shocks (such as an erratic monsoon spike in tomato or onion prices) within the **`2%–6%`** band and guide inflation back toward `4%` over a medium-term horizon without needlessly crushing GDP growth and employment. Yet price stability remains lexicographically *primary*: when inflation threatens to become entrenched or generalised outside the band, growth considerations must yield to re-anchoring inflation.

#### 2. Inflation Target and Five-Year Statutory Reviews (`Section 45ZA`)

Under **`Section 45ZA`** of the `RBI Act, 1934`, the **Central Government, in consultation with the RBI**, determines the inflation target in terms of the **Consumer Price Index (`CPI`)** once in every **five years** and notifies it in the Official Gazette. Crucially, UPSC frequently tests who sets the target versus who sets the policy rate: **the Central Government (in consultation with RBI) sets the inflation target; the MPC sets the policy repo rate to achieve that target.**

- **Target Metric:** Headline **CPI–Combined (`CPI-C`)** (Rural + Urban, compiled monthly by the National Statistical Office [`NSO`], MoSPI).
- **Point Target & Tolerance Band:** **`4.0%`** with an upper tolerance limit of **`6.0%`** and a lower tolerance limit of **`2.0%`** (`4% ± 2%`).
- **Three Consecutive Five-Year Terms:**
  1. **First Term (`5 August 2016 – 31 March 2021`):** Inaugural notification establishing `4% ± 2%`.
  2. **Second Term (`1 April 2021 – 31 March 2026`):** Retained unchanged via Gazette notification dated **31 March 2021** following the first statutory review.
  3. **Third Term (`1 April 2026 – 31 March 2031`):** Following an extensive second statutory review evaluating a decade of FIT experience, the Central Government issued a Gazette notification on **25 March 2026** retaining **all features unchanged**—preserving headline `CPI-C` at `4%` with the `±2%` band through March 2031 despite intense policy debate over excluding volatile food items.

#### 3. Composition, Tenure and Appointment of the MPC (`Sections 45ZB–45ZF`)

**`Section 45ZB`** empowers the Central Government to constitute a **six-member Monetary Policy Committee (`MPC`)** split evenly between internal central-bank leadership and independent external economists:

1. **Three RBI (Internal) Members:**
   - **Governor of the RBI** — *Chairperson, ex officio* (currently **Shri Sanjay Malhotra**, 26th RBI Governor, appointed December 2024).
   - **Deputy Governor of the RBI in charge of Monetary Policy** — *Member, ex officio* (currently **Dr. Poonam Gupta**).
   - **One Officer of the RBI** nominated by the **Central Board of the RBI** — *Member, ex officio* (typically an Executive Director; currently **Shri Indranil Bhattacharyya**).
2. **Three Central Government-Appointed (External) Members:**
   - Three persons of ability, integrity, and standing with knowledge and experience in economics, banking, finance, or monetary policy, appointed by the Central Government on the recommendations of a **Search-cum-Selection Committee (`Section 45ZC`)**.
   - **Composition of the Search-cum-Selection Committee (`Sec. 45ZC`):** Chaired by the **Cabinet Secretary**, with the **RBI Governor**, the **Secretary, Department of Economic Affairs (`DEA`), Ministry of Finance**, and **three experts** in economics/banking/finance/monetary policy nominated by the Central Government.
   - **Current External Members (appointed October 2024):** **Dr. Nagesh Kumar** (Director & CEO, ISID), **Shri Saugata Bhattacharya** (economist), and **Prof. Ram Singh** (Director, Delhi School of Economics).
   - **Term & Reappointment (`Sec. 45ZD`):** External members hold office for a fixed term of **four years** and are **NOT eligible for re-appointment** (a critical safeguard against political capture or currying favour for a second term!).
   - **Ineligibility Criteria (`Sec. 45ZC`):** No Member of Parliament or State Legislature, no public servant (except university professors/teachers who are eligible), no employee/director of any bank or financial institution regulated by RBI, and no person over **70 years of age** can be appointed.
   - **Removal (`Sec. 45ZE`):** The Central Government may remove an external member only on specified grounds—insolvency, physical/mental incapacity, conviction for moral turpitude, conflict of interest, abuse of position, or absence from three consecutive meetings without permission—and after affording a reasonable opportunity of being heard.

#### 4. Functioning, Voting, Quorum and Transparency (`Sections 45ZI–45ZM`)

- **Meeting Frequency (`Sec. 45ZI`):** The MPC is statutorily required to meet **at least four times a year** (at least once per quarter). In practice, the RBI schedules **six bi-monthly meetings** every financial year (April, June, August, October, December, February), plus emergency off-cycle meetings when warranted (e.g., May 2022).
- **Quorum:** **`4 members`** (including the Governor—or in the Governor's absence, the Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy).
- **Voting and Casting Vote:** Each member has **one vote**. Decisions are taken by **majority vote** of members present and voting. In the event of an **equality of votes (`3–3` tie)**, the **RBI Governor has a second or casting vote**.
  - *Crucial Prelims Distinction:* The RBI Governor does **NOT** possess a **veto power**! If all 3 external members plus 1 internal RBI member vote one way (`4–2`), the Governor is outvoted and bound by the MPC resolution. The Governor's casting vote applies *only* to break an exact `3–3` tie.
- **Binding Resolution & RBI Operational Autonomy (`Sec. 45ZB(4)` & `45ZJ`):** The MPC's decision is **binding on the RBI** exclusively with respect to the **policy repo rate** (and accompanying policy stance). Operational liquidity instruments (`CRR`, `SLR`, `OMO`, `VRRR`, forex intervention, regulatory risk weights) remain exclusively within the **RBI's institutional domain**, outside the MPC's vote!
- **Statutory Transparency (`Secs. 45ZK–45ZM`):**
  - The RBI must publish the **MPC Resolution** immediately after each meeting.
  - Under **`Section 45ZL`**, the RBI must publish the **Minutes of MPC proceedings on the 14th day** after every meeting, disclosing each member's individual vote and written rationale (creating unprecedented public accountability).
  - Under **`Section 45ZM`**, the RBI must publish a **Monetary Policy Report (`MPR`)** once every **six months** (April and October), detailing inflation sources and forecasts for the next 6 to 18 months.
  - **Silent Period (`Sec. 45ZI`):** To prevent market leaks and speculative arbitrage, MPC members observe a strict **7-day silent period** before and after every rate-setting meeting.

#### 5. Failure to Maintain the Inflation Target (`Section 45ZN`)

Under **`Section 45ZN`** and the Central Government's Gazette rules, the RBI is deemed to have **failed** to meet the inflation target if:

1. Average headline `CPI-C` inflation **exceeds the upper tolerance limit (`> 6%`) for any three consecutive quarters**; **OR**
2. Average headline `CPI-C` inflation **falls below the lower tolerance limit (`< 2%`) for any three consecutive quarters**.

When failure occurs, the RBI is statutorily obligated to submit a **written report to the Central Government** within one month specifying three things: **(a) the reasons for failure**, **(b) the remedial actions proposed to be taken by the Bank**, and **(c) an estimate of the time-period within which the inflation target will be achieved**. Notably, the statute does **not** automatically sack the Governor or MPC members, nor does it mandate immediate public disclosure of the report.

- **India's Sole Statutory Failure (2022):** Triggered by the Russia–Ukraine war's global food, fertiliser, and crude-oil shock layered atop post-COVID supply disruptions, India's quarterly average `CPI-C` exceeded `6%` across **three consecutive quarters—Q1 2022 (`6.34%`), Q2 2022 (`7.28%`), and Q3 2022 (`7.04%`)** (corresponding to Q4 FY22, Q1 FY23, and Q2 FY23). The MPC convened a special meeting on **3 November 2022** to finalise and send its confidential statutory report under `Sec. 45ZN` to the Central Government, simultaneously executing `250 bps` of cumulative repo hikes (`4.00%` to `6.50%` between May 2022 and February 2023) that successfully guided inflation back below `6%` without triggering recession.

> [!prelims]
> **Five High-Frequency UPSC Prelims Traps on the MPC (`RBI Act, Secs. 45ZA–45ZN`):**
> 1. **Statutory, NOT Constitutional:** The MPC was created by amending the `RBI Act, 1934` via the **Finance Act, 2016**—it has NO Article in the Constitution.
> 2. **Who Sets the Target?** The **Central Government (in consultation with RBI)** notifies the `4% ± 2%` target every 5 years (`Sec. 45ZA`)—NOT the MPC and NOT RBI unilaterally.
> 3. **Casting Vote ≠ Veto:** The RBI Governor chairs the 6-member MPC and holds a **second/casting vote ONLY in a `3–3` tie**—never a veto over a majority! (UPSC Prelims 2017 tested MPC composition and chairmanship directly.)
> 4. **External Members' Term:** Appointed by the Central Government (via Cabinet Secretary-chaired Search Committee) for **4 years, NON-RENEWABLE** (`Sec. 45ZD`), and **NOT** 5 years.
> 5. **Failure Window & Scope:** Failure requires **3 consecutive quarters** (NOT 3 consecutive months, NOT 2 quarters) above `6%` **OR** below `2%`. Moreover, the MPC votes ONLY on the **Policy Repo Rate** (and stance)—**NOT** on `CRR`, `SLR`, `OMO`, or forex intervention!

### Monetary Policy Stances and the 2025–2026 RBI Policy Cycle

Alongside the numerical repo rate, the MPC announces a **Monetary Policy Stance**—a forward-guidance signal indicating the likely direction of future policy rate moves so that bond markets, banks, and firms can price credit smoothly rather than facing sudden shocks.

1. **Accommodative Stance:** Signals that the central bank is prepared to **expand money supply and cut interest rates** (or keep them low) to stimulate growth; a rate hike is effectively off the table in the near term.
2. **Neutral Stance:** Gives the MPC maximum **two-way flexibility** to either hike or cut interest rates depending on incoming inflation and growth data, without any pre-committed directional bias.
3. **Withdrawal of Accommodation:** Used during post-crisis normalisation (employed by the RBI from April 2022 to August 2024) to signal progressive absorption of excess pandemic-era liquidity and containment of inflation without choking investment.
4. **Calibrated Tightening (Hawkish Stance):** Signals that **rate cuts are off the table** in the near term, and future actions will be restricted exclusively to either a **rate hike or a pause**—calibrated carefully to incoming data so tightening does not stall growth unnecessarily.

> [!debate]
> **Mains GS-3 Policy Debate: Should India Target Headline CPI or Core CPI (Excluding Food)?**
> Ahead of the March 2026 five-year FIT review, the **Economic Survey 2023–24** sparked a landmark debate by arguing that the RBI should target **core inflation (excluding food)** rather than headline CPI:
> - **Arguments for Excluding Food (Core CPI):** Food accounts for nearly **`45.86%`** of India's 2012-base CPI basket (still ~37–40% in modern consumption baskets), and food price spikes stem primarily from **supply-side shocks** (erratic monsoons, heatwaves, cold-chain deficits, perishable crop cycles). Hiking interest rates cannot grow onions or make it rain; instead, tightening policy in response to vegetable spikes needlessly depresses industrial investment, MSMEs, and job creation.
> - **Arguments for Retaining Headline CPI (RBI's Counter — Validated in March 2026 Renewal):** RBI empirical research (and Deputy Governor **Dr. Poonam Gupta's** May 2026 NCAER lecture) demonstrated that in a lower-middle-income economy where food absorbs a huge share of household budgets, persistent food inflation rapidly **spills over into household inflation expectations, rural/urban wage demands, and core services prices** (*second-round effects*). Ignoring food would destroy public trust in the central bank's credibility, since ordinary Indians live on headline prices—not core prices.
> - **Balanced Mains Synthesis:** Retaining **headline CPI (`4% ± 2%`)** in March 2026 was prudent because the **`±2%` tolerance band** already gives the MPC institutional space to "look through" *transient* first-round vegetable shocks while acting decisively whenever food pressures generalise into wages and core inflation—provided supply-side policy (buffer stocks, e-NAM, cold chains, climate-resilient pulses/oilseeds) bears the primary burden of taming food volatility.

## 2. Instruments of Monetary Policy and Policy Transmission

How does an announcement by six economists in Mumbai—moving the repo rate by a mere `0.25%`—ripple through bank branches, housing EMIs, corporate bond yields, and vegetable markets across 1.4 billion people? The answer lies in the **instruments of monetary policy** and the **channels of monetary transmission**. Economists traditionally classify RBI's policy toolkit into two broad categories:

1. **Quantitative (General / Indirect) Instruments:** Affect the **aggregate volume and cost of credit** across the entire economy indiscriminately—comprising the **Liquidity Adjustment Facility (`LAF`) corridor** (`Repo`, `SDF`, `MSF`, `VRRR`), **Reserve Ratios** (`CRR`, `SLR`), **Open Market Operations (`OMO`)**, **Market Stabilisation Scheme (`MSS`)**, **Bank Rate**, and **Long-Term Repo Operations (`LTRO`/`TLTRO`)**.
2. **Qualitative (Selective / Direct) Instruments:** Regulate the **direction and sectoral allocation of credit** without necessarily altering total money supply—comprising **Priority Sector Lending (`PSL`)**, **Margin Requirements**, **Consumer Credit Regulation**, **Credit Rationing**, **Moral Suasion**, and **Direct Action**.

---

### Quantitative Instruments: The LAF Interest Rate Corridor

Since 2000, India's primary operating framework for daily monetary policy has been the **Liquidity Adjustment Facility (`LAF`)**—a corridor system designed to keep the economy's benchmark overnight interbank interest rate, the **Weighted Average Call Money Rate (`WACR`)** (the **operating target** of monetary policy), tightly aligned with the **Policy Repo Rate**.

#### 1. Policy Repo Rate (The Anchor — `5.50%` as of Nov 2026)

**Repo** stands for **Repurchase Option** (or *Repurchase Agreement*). The **Repo Rate** is the interest rate at which the RBI **lends short-term funds (typically overnight, or 7–14 days via Variable Rate Repo [`VRR`]) to Scheduled Commercial Banks** against the pledge of **eligible government securities (`G-Secs` / Treasury Bills) held OVER AND ABOVE their mandatory SLR quota**, accompanied by a simultaneous agreement that the borrowing bank will repurchase those exact securities at a predetermined higher price on maturity.

- **Mechanics of a Repo Hike:** When the MPC **raises the Repo Rate**, borrowing from the RBI becomes costlier for commercial banks. Banks pass this higher funding cost onto borrowers via higher lending rates (immediately on repo-linked `EBLR` loans!), discouraging credit-financed consumption and corporate capital expenditure, curbing aggregate demand, and cooling **demand-pull inflation**.
- **Mechanics of a Repo Cut:** Lowering the Repo Rate cheapens short-term bank liquidity, nudges deposit and lending rates downward, stimulates borrowing, investment, and consumption, and revives GDP growth.
- **Collateral Rule (Prelims Trap!):** Repo borrowing is strictly **collateralised**—and banks **cannot** use securities already earmarked for their mandatory `SLR` quota to borrow under the regular LAF Repo window; they must pledge **excess SLR securities**.

#### 2. Standing Deposit Facility (`SDF`) — The Uncollateralised Floor (`5.25%` as of Nov 2026)

Introduced by the RBI on **8 April 2022** (following the **Urjit Patel Committee 2014** recommendation and a 2018 amendment inserting **`Section 17(1A)`** into the `RBI Act, 1934`), the **Standing Deposit Facility (`SDF`)** is an **overnight liquidity-absorption window** through which eligible LAF participants (commercial banks) can park their surplus funds with the RBI at their own discretion, earning the **SDF Rate** fixed symmetrically at **`25 basis points below the Policy Repo Rate`** (`5.25%` when Repo is `5.50%`).

- **Why SDF Replaced Fixed Reverse Repo as the Corridor Floor:** Prior to April 2022, whenever massive forex inflows or pandemic liquidity flooded Indian banks with ₹8–10 lakh crore of surplus cash, absorbing that money via **Reverse Repo** forced the RBI to hand over **G-Sec collateral** from its own balance sheet to commercial banks. If surplus liquidity exceeded the RBI's stock of available G-Secs, the RBI ran out of collateral!
- **The Game-Changing Feature of SDF:** **SDF is completely UNCOLLATERALISED!** The RBI absorbs unlimited overnight excess liquidity at the SDF rate **without transferring any government securities as collateral**—freeing the central bank's balance sheet during systemic liquidity gluts while serving as an automatic, binding **floor** of the LAF corridor (no bank will lend overnight in the interbank call market below `5.25%` when it can risklessly park unlimited funds with the RBI at `5.25%`!).
- **SLR Eligibility Bonus:** Balances parked by banks under the SDF are **NOT eligible for `CRR` maintenance**, but **ARE eligible as liquid assets for `SLR` compliance** under `Sec. 24` of the `Banking Regulation Act, 1949`!

#### 3. Marginal Standing Facility (`MSF`) — The Penal Ceiling (`5.75%` as of Nov 2026)

Introduced in **May 2011**, the **Marginal Standing Facility (`MSF`)** is an emergency overnight borrowing window allowing Scheduled Commercial Banks to borrow urgent overnight funds from the RBI at a **penal rate fixed symmetrically at `25 basis points above the Policy Repo Rate`** (`5.75%` when Repo is `5.50%`) whenever interbank liquidity dries up completely.

- **Two Crucial Differences from Repo:**
  1. **SLR Dip-In Allowed:** Unlike normal LAF Repo, banks borrowing under MSF are explicitly permitted to **dip into their mandatory `SLR` portfolio up to `2%` of their `NDTL`** to pledge G-Secs as collateral, without incurring the statutory SLR default penalty!
  2. **Corridor Ceiling:** MSF establishes the hard **upper ceiling** of the LAF corridor (no bank will pay more than `5.75%` in the overnight interbank call market when it can tap RBI's MSF window at `5.75%`). Thus, the **LAF corridor width** is strictly symmetric at **`50 bps`** (`Repo − 25 bps` SDF floor to `Repo + 25 bps` MSF ceiling).

#### 4. Reverse Repo Rate (Fixed `3.35%` & Variable Rate Reverse Repo [`VRRR`])

**Reverse Repo** is the exact mirror image of Repo: the interest rate at which the **RBI borrows (absorbs) surplus liquidity from banks** by temporarily selling government securities to them **against collateral** with an agreement to buy them back later.

- **Fixed Rate Reverse Repo (`FRRR`):** Currently frozen at **`3.35%`** since May 2020. Since **April 2022**, FRRR is **no longer the floor of the LAF corridor** (SDF holds that role) and is retained solely at RBI's discretion for specific liquidity operations.
- **Variable Rate Reverse Repo (`VRRR`) & Variable Rate Repo (`VRR`):** Today, the RBI conducts active fine-tuning liquidity management—typically 2-day, 7-day, or 14-day auctions—using **market-discovered interest rates** (`VRRR` to drain durable surplus liquidity; `VRR` to inject liquidity during advance-tax outflows or festival cash drains), keeping the overnight `WACR` anchored tightly around the `5.50%` Policy Repo Rate.

### Quantitative Instruments: Reserve Ratios, OMO, MSS, Bank Rate and LTRO

Beyond the daily LAF corridor, the RBI commands powerful **structural (durable) liquidity tools** that alter the banking system's core money-creating capacity.

#### 1. Cash Reserve Ratio (`CRR`) — `3.00%` of NDTL (`Section 42`, RBI Act, 1934)

Under **`Section 42(1)` of the `RBI Act, 1934`**, every Scheduled Commercial Bank (and under `Sec. 18` of the `BR Act` for non-scheduled banks) must maintain a stipulated fraction of its **Net Demand and Time Liabilities (`NDTL`)** exclusively in the form of **liquid cash balances deposited with the RBI**. (`NDTL` equals a bank's total public demand deposits [current + savings accounts] plus time deposits [fixed + recurring deposits], minus interbank deposits held with other banks.)

- **Key Statutory & Economic Features of CRR:**
  1. **Form:** Strictly **CASH ONLY**, held in the bank's current account with the RBI (never gold, never G-Secs, never vault cash for scheduled banks under `Sec. 42`!).
  2. **Zero Interest:** Following the **RBI (Amendment) Act, 2006**, the RBI pays **ZERO interest** on CRR balances—making CRR an implicit "reserve tax" on bank profitability. That is why cutting CRR from `4.00%` to `3.00%` in 2025 not only released **₹2.5 lakh crore** of durable liquidity but also lifted bank Net Interest Margins (`NIMs`)!
  3. **No Statutory Floor or Ceiling:** Prior to 2006, `Sec. 42` constrained CRR between `3%` and `20%`. The **RBI (Amendment) Act, 2006 removed BOTH the floor and the ceiling**, empowering the RBI to set CRR anywhere from `0%` to `100%` in theory.
  4. **Direct Money-Multiplier Lever:** Raising CRR immediately locks up high-powered base money with the RBI, shrinking the **money multiplier (`1 / CRR` in the simplified model)** and contracting aggregate credit rapidly; lowering CRR expands lendable reserves immediately.
  5. **Maintenance & Default Penalty:** Banks maintain CRR on an average basis over a **fortnight** (with a daily minimum floor of **`90%`** of the required CRR). Default attracts a penal interest of **Bank Rate + 3%** on the first default fortnight and **Bank Rate + 5%** on subsequent defaults.

#### 2. Statutory Liquidity Ratio (`SLR`) — `18.00%` of NDTL (`Section 24`, BR Act, 1949)

Under **`Section 24` of the `Banking Regulation Act, 1949`**, every commercial bank must maintain a specified minimum proportion of its `NDTL` **with itself** (not deposited with RBI) in specified **unencumbered liquid assets**—namely: **(a) Cash** (vault cash / excess over CRR), **(b) Gold**, and **(c) RBI-approved unencumbered securities** (predominantly Central Government `G-Secs`, Treasury Bills, and State Development Loans [`SDLs`]), plus `SDF` balances.

- **Key Statutory & Economic Features of SLR:**
  1. **Statutory Ceiling (`40%`), No Floor:** Originally, `Sec. 24` prescribed a floor of `25%` and a ceiling of `40%`. The **Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2007 abolished the `25%` statutory floor** while **retaining the `40%` statutory ceiling**—allowing the RBI to lower SLR progressively from its 1991 peak of `38.5%` down to **`18.00%`** today.
  2. **Earns Return:** Unlike sterile CRR cash, banks earn sovereign coupon yields (roughly `6.5%–7.2%`) on their SLR G-Sec holdings.
  3. **Dual Role — Prudential Liquidity + Captive Sovereign Borrowing:** Historically, high SLR forced Indian banks to finance fiscal deficits cheaply ("financial repression"). Today, at `18%`, SLR aligns largely with Basel III's **Liquidity Coverage Ratio (`LCR`)**—ensuring banks hold deep high-quality liquid assets (`HQLA`) to survive sudden depositor runs.
  4. **Default Penalty:** Identical structure to CRR—penal interest of **Bank Rate + 3%** (first default) and **Bank Rate + 5%** (subsequent defaults).

> [!remember]
> **Huge 2025 Prelims Update — Redefinition of "Fortnight" for CRR & SLR (`Banking Laws [Amendment] Act, 2025`):**
> For decades, UPSC textbooks taught that banks calculated and reported `NDTL`, `CRR`, and `SLR` on **alternate Fridays** ("Saturday to the second following Friday"). **Effective 15 December 2025**, the **`Banking Laws (Amendment) Act, 2025`** amended `Section 42` of the `RBI Act, 1934` and `Sections 18 & 24` of the `Banking Regulation Act, 1949` to redefine a **"fortnight"** cleanly as:
> - **1st to 15th day of each calendar month**, and
> - **16th to the last day of each calendar month** (both days inclusive)!
> Keep this update front-of-mind—any old MCQ claiming CRR/SLR reporting still follows "alternate Fridays" is now statutorily superseded!

#### 3. Open Market Operations (`OMO`) and Operation Twist

**Open Market Operations (`OMO`)** refer to the **outright (permanent) buying and selling of Government Securities (`G-Secs`)** by the RBI in the open secondary market (unlike Repo/Reverse Repo, which are temporary collateralised agreements reversed on maturity).

- **OMO Purchase (Expansionary / Injection):** The RBI buys G-Secs from commercial banks and pays them in freshly created central-bank reserves (`M0`). Bank liquidity swells permanently, bond prices rise, **bond yields fall**, and credit expansion accelerates.
- **OMO Sale (Contractionary / Absorption):** The RBI sells G-Secs out of its portfolio to banks and absorbs cash reserves permanently, shrinking durable liquidity, pushing bond yields upward, and dampening inflation.
- **Operation Twist (Simultaneous Buy-Long / Sell-Short):** First deployed by the US Fed in 1961 and adopted actively by the RBI in 2019–2021, **Operation Twist** involves the RBI **simultaneously purchasing long-term G-Secs (e.g., 10-year bonds) and selling an equal value of short-term Treasury Bills (e.g., 1-year T-Bills)**. It is **liquidity-neutral** (total reserve money stays unchanged), yet it flattens the sovereign yield curve—pushing **long-term yields down** to lower corporate borrowing and mortgage costs without flooding the overnight money market with inflationary cash!

#### 4. Market Stabilisation Scheme (`MSS`, 2004) — Sterilising Capital Inflows

Introduced via a Government–RBI MoU in **April 2004** when surging foreign portfolio and FDI inflows flooded India with dollars, the **Market Stabilisation Scheme (`MSS`)** is a specialised **liquidity-absorption (sterilisation)** instrument. Whenever the RBI buys excess US dollars from the forex market to prevent sharp rupee appreciation (protecting Indian exports), it necessarily releases equivalent rupees into the banking system—fuelling inflation. If the RBI lacks enough G-Secs of its own to conduct OMO sales, the **Central Government issues special `MSS` Treasury Bills and Dated Securities** on behalf of the RBI to soak up the excess rupee liquidity.

- **Three Crucial Prelims Traps on MSS:**
  1. **Ring-Fenced Cash Account:** Funds raised via MSS bonds are **impounded in a separate MSS Cash Account at the RBI**—the Government **CANNOT spend MSS proceeds** to finance its fiscal deficit!
  2. **Who Bears the Interest Cost?** Although MSS serves an RBI monetary-sterilisation objective, the **Central Government bears the interest cost** of MSS securities (creating a quasi-fiscal cost reflected in the Union Budget's interest payments, though MSS principal does not finance fiscal deficit).
  3. **Ceiling Fixed Annually:** Parliament/Central Government sets an annual cap on MSS issuances; famously, during **Demonetisation (Nov 2016)**, the MSS ceiling was raised sharply from ₹30,000 crore to **₹6 lakh crore** to absorb the tsunami of bank deposits.

#### 5. Bank Rate (`5.75%` as of Nov 2026) and LTRO / TLTRO

- **Bank Rate (`Section 49`, RBI Act, 1934):** Historically the oldest central-bank rate, the **Bank Rate** is the standard rate at which the RBI is prepared to buy or **rediscount bills of exchange or other commercial paper**, and to extend **long-term, uncollateralised** loans to banks. Before LAF (2000), Bank Rate was India's primary policy rate. Today, it is **automatically aligned with the MSF Rate (`Repo + 25 bps` = `5.75%`)** and serves principally as a **statutory penalty benchmark** (for CRR/SLR shortfalls).
  - *Prelims Contrast:* Repo is **short-term & collateralised** (active policy rate); Bank Rate is **long-term & requires no collateral** (now aligned with MSF as a penal benchmark).
- **Long-Term Repo Operations (`LTRO`) & Targeted LTRO (`TLTRO`):** Introduced by the RBI in **February 2020** (and TLTRO during COVID-19), `LTRO` provides **1-year to 3-year** durable liquidity to banks at the **Policy Repo Rate** against G-Sec collateral—bridging the gap between overnight LAF liquidity and multi-year corporate lending. Under `TLTRO`, banks had to deploy those cheap long-term funds specifically into **corporate bonds, commercial paper, and NBFC debt**, reviving frozen credit markets during systemic shocks.

### Qualitative (Selective) Monetary Policy Instruments and PSL

When inflationary pressures or credit shortages are concentrated in specific sectors—such as speculative commodity hoarding, frothy unsecured personal loans, or under-banked smallholder agriculture—blunt economy-wide repo hikes can cause collateral damage. Qualitative tools target credit flow directly:

1. **Margin Requirements (Loan-to-Value [`LTV`] Caps):** The margin is the difference between the market value of collateral pledged and the maximum loan sanctioned (`Margin = 100% − LTV`). Raising the margin on gold loans or speculative stock-market loans from `20%` to `35%` cuts borrowing capacity in that bubble sector without hurting industrial credit elsewhere.
2. **Consumer Credit Regulation:** Controlling minimum down-payments, maximum repayment tenures, or **risk weights** on consumer durables, personal loans, and credit cards (e.g., RBI's November 2023 `+25 percentage point` risk-weight hike on unsecured personal loans and credit-card receivables to cool retail froth).
3. **Credit Rationing:** Fixing upper ceilings on aggregate loans to particular industries or single corporate groups (**Large Exposure Framework [`LEF`]** caps single-borrower exposure at `20%` of a bank's Tier-1 capital, `25%` in exceptional cases, and connected-group exposure at `25%`).
4. **Moral Suasion:** Informal persuasion, speeches, bilateral meetings, and advisory letters by the RBI Governor urging banks to pass on rate cuts faster, curb predatory digital lending, or restrain irrational pricing—backed implicitly by the regulator's immense authority.
5. **Direct Action:** Formal statutory punitive measures under **`Section 35A`** of the `Banking Regulation Act, 1949` against non-compliant banks or NBFCs—including refusal of refinancing, business restrictions, superseding boards, or levying monetary penalties (as deployed against Paytm Payments Bank, Kotak Mahindra Bank's digital onboarding, and errant NBFCs in 2024).

#### 6. Priority Sector Lending (`PSL`) — Revised 2025 RBI Master Directions

Originated in the late 1960s and formalised after bank nationalisation (based on the **Gadgil Study Group, 1969** and **K.S. Krishnaswamy Committee**), **Priority Sector Lending (`PSL`)** mandates that commercial and cooperative banks channel a prescribed share of their **Adjusted Net Bank Credit (`ANBC`)** or **Credit Equivalent of Off-Balance Sheet Exposures (`CEOBE`)**, whichever is higher, into socially vital sectors that commercial markets systematically underserve.

There are **eight eligible PSL categories**: **(1) Agriculture**, **(2) Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (`MSMEs`)**, **(3) Export Credit**, **(4) Education**, **(5) Housing**, **(6) Social Infrastructure** (schools, drinking water, sanitation, healthcare), **(7) Renewable Energy**, and **(8) Others** (including self-help groups, distressed poor repaying informal moneylenders, and start-ups up to ₹50 crore).

On **24 March 2025** (effective **1 April 2025**), the RBI issued the overhauled ***Master Directions – Priority Sector Lending (Targets and Classification) Directions, 2025***, followed by a landmark **20 June 2025 circular for Small Finance Banks**:

> **Applied Illustration — How Sterilisation (`MSS`) and Operation Twist Work in Practice:**
> Suppose foreign investors pour `$10 billion` into Indian equities in a single month. If the RBI does nothing, the rupee surges sharply from `₹84/$` to `₹78/$`, hurting Indian IT, textile, and pharma exporters. So the RBI intervenes in the forex market to **buy `$10 billion`** and inject **`₹84,000 crore`** of domestic currency into the banking system. Left unaddressed, that ₹84,000 crore surge in reserve money (`M0`) would fuel inflation. To **"sterilise"** it, the RBI uses **MSS** (or OMO sales) to sell ₹84,000 crore of government securities, pulling the ₹84,000 crore right back out of banks and impounding it in the MSS account—preserving export competitiveness *without* unleashing domestic inflation! Similarly, when fiscal deficits kept 10-year G-Sec yields stubbornly high in 2020 despite low repo rates, the RBI used **Operation Twist**—buying ₹10,000 crore of 10-year bonds while simultaneously selling ₹10,000 crore of 1-year T-Bills—compressing long-term borrowing costs without adding a single rupee to aggregate liquidity.

### Monetary Policy Transmission and the Evolution of Loan Benchmarks

Even the sharpest monetary policy decision fails if changes in the RBI's Policy Repo Rate do not translate swiftly into the deposit and lending rates faced by households and businesses. This pass-through is called **Monetary Policy Transmission**.

#### Five Channels of Monetary Policy Transmission

1. **Interest Rate Channel (Primary):** A repo cut lowers short-term money market rates → lowers bank cost of funds → reduces floating retail/corporate loan EMIs → stimulates housing, auto, and industrial investment.
2. **Credit / Bank Lending Channel:** Lower CRR/OMO purchases expand bank reserves and credit supply, easing credit rationing for bank-dependent MSMEs and farmers who lack bond-market access.
3. **Asset Price Channel:** Lower interest rates boost the present discounted value of equities, bonds, and real estate, increasing household wealth (stimulating consumption via the *wealth effect*) and raising corporate collateral values (*balance-sheet channel*).
4. **Exchange Rate Channel:** Lower domestic interest rates relative to global benchmarks (like the US Fed rate) reduce foreign capital inflows and weaken the rupee, making Indian exports more price-competitive while making imports dearer (lifting net exports `X − M`, though adding imported inflation risk).
5. **Expectations Channel:** Credible forward guidance by an inflation-targeting MPC anchors long-term wage contracts and corporate pricing behaviour even before actual rate changes bite.

```flow
# Monetary Policy Transmission Mechanism (From MPC Vote to Real Economy)
RBI MPC Alters Policy Repo Rate | E.g., −25 bps cut (or +25 bps hike in Oct 2026) shifts the entire LAF corridor (SDF & MSF)
Overnight Money Market (WACR) | Instantaneous pass-through (Day 1) to Call Money, TREPS & 91-day T-Bill yields
Interest Rate & Credit Channel | EBLR retail/MSME loans reprice within 3 months; MCLR & deposit rates reprice gradually || Asset Price & Exchange Rate Channel | Bond yields, equity valuations & INR exchange rate adjust to rate differentials
Aggregate Demand, Output & CPI | Household consumption, housing & corporate CapEx respond over 2–4 quarters; CPI inflation aligns over 4–6 quarters
```

#### Why Did Transmission Lag Historically? The Four-Stage Evolution of Loan Pricing

For decades, Indian monetary policy suffered from notorious **asymmetric transmission**: banks hiked lending rates within days of an RBI rate hike, yet delayed passing on RBI rate cuts for 12–18 months! The root causes lay in **opaque internal bank pricing formulas** and **structural deposit-market rigidities**. In response, the RBI overhauled India's loan-pricing regime four times:

| Era | Benchmark Regime | Pricing Formula & Mechanism | Why It Failed / Current Status |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **2003–2010** | **Benchmark Prime Lending Rate (`BPLR`)** | Internal rate ostensibly for a bank's most creditworthy borrower. | Deeply opaque: banks routinely lent *below* BPLR (`sub-BPLR` loans accounted for >75% of credit) to large corporates while overcharging MSMEs and retail borrowers; virtually zero transmission of repo cuts. |
| **July 2010 – Mar 2016** | **Base Rate** | Minimum lending rate based on banks' **average cost of funds**; prohibited sub-Base Rate lending. | Backward-looking: because average cost of funds reflected legacy fixed deposits contracted 2–3 years earlier, RBI repo cuts took quarters to move the Base Rate. |
| **April 2016 – Sep 2019** | **Marginal Cost of Funds based Lending Rate (`MCLR`)** | Tenor-linked internal benchmark based on the **marginal (incremental) cost of fresh deposits**, negative carry on CRR, operating costs & tenor premium. | Improved speed over Base Rate, yet remained an *internal* bank formula with discretionary spreads and long 6–12 month reset clauses—still allowing sluggish pass-through during the 2019 easing cycle. |
| **October 2019 – Present** | **External Benchmark Linked Lending Rate (`EBLR`)** | Mandatory for **all new floating-rate personal/retail loans (housing, auto) & MSME loans** (recommended by **Janak Raj Internal Study Group, 2017**). Must link directly to an *external* market rate: **RBI Policy Repo Rate**, **3-month/6-month T-Bill yield**, or **FBIL market benchmark**—reset **at least once every 3 months**! | **Transformative Success:** By 2025–2026, over **60% of floating-rate rupee loans** of SCBs are linked to `EBLR` (predominantly the RBI Repo Rate). Consequently, during the 2025 `125 bps` easing cycle, fresh retail and MSME loan rates transmitted rapidly—while legacy corporate loans still largely reference MCLR! |

#### Persistent Structural Impediments to Full Transmission (Mains GS-3 Value-Add)

Even under `EBLR`, RBI Annual Reports highlight four continuing structural frictions:
1. **Administered Small Savings Rates:** Government small-savings schemes (PPF, Sukanya Samriddhi, NSC, Senior Citizens' Savings Scheme) often remain sticky above bank term-deposit rates for political reasons, preventing banks from cutting retail deposit rates when RBI cuts the repo rate without risking deposit flight.
2. **Elevated Credit-Deposit (`C-D`) Ratio:** As Indian households channelled savings rapidly into mutual funds, SIPs, and equities in 2023–2026, bank deposit growth lagged credit growth—pushing the SCB **Credit-Deposit ratio past `80%–82%`** (verified at **`82.06%`** on 31 October 2026 via RBI NSDP!). Fierce competition to retain deposits keeps banks' funding costs elevated, squeezing Net Interest Margins (`NIMs`) whenever `EBLR` loans reprice downward automatically.
3. **High Share of Fixed-Rate Term Deposits:** Unlike floating loans that reprice every 3 months under EBLR, existing retail fixed deposits (`FDs`) reprice only upon maturity (1–3 years), creating temporary asset-liability repricing asymmetry.
4. **Informal Credit & Cash Economy:** Small borrowers dependent on unregistered moneylenders or unorganised trade credit remain largely insulated from formal repo-rate shifts, though **UPI, Jan Dhan, PM SVANidhi, and Account Aggregators** are progressively shrinking this blind spot.

## 3. Structure of the Indian Banking System

A central bank cannot lend directly to a cotton farmer in Vidarbha, a textile exporter in Tiruppur, or an infrastructure developer building a coastal expressway. Instead, monetary policy and savings mobilisation operate through a multi-tiered **banking architecture** that mobilises dispersed household surpluses, transforms short-term deposits into long-term productive investment (**maturity transformation**), runs the national payment rails, and allocates credit across urban and rural India. Unlike many Western economies dominated by a handful of homogeneous universal banks, India deliberately cultivated a **plural, layered banking structure** combining commercial banks, rural grassroot lenders, member-owned cooperatives, differentiated niche banks, and apex developmental financial institutions.

For UPSC Prelims, examiners routinely construct elimination traps around **statutory schedules, ownership ratios, regulatory demarcation (`RBI` vs `NABARD` vs `State Registrars`), PSL ceilings, and recent consolidations**—including the historic **May 2025 `One State–One RRB` amalgamation (`43 → 28 RRBs`)**, the **Banking Laws (Amendment) Act, 2025**, **NUCFDC (2024)**, and **AU Small Finance Bank's August 2025 Universal Bank transition milestone**. For Mains GS-3, understanding this institutional mosaic is essential to evaluate **financial inclusion, public sector bank governance, privatisation trade-offs, and last-mile rural credit**.

### Scheduled versus Non-Scheduled Banks (RBI Act, 1934)

At the foundational statutory level, every bank operating in India is classified into one of two categories under the **`Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934`**:

#### 1. Scheduled Banks (`Second Schedule`, RBI Act, 1934)

A **Scheduled Bank** is any bank whose name is included in the **Second Schedule of the `RBI Act, 1934`**. Under **`Section 42(6)(a)`** of the `RBI Act`, the RBI may include a bank in the Second Schedule only if it satisfies three statutory criteria:

1. **Minimum Capital Threshold:** Its paid-up capital and reserves must have an aggregate value of **not less than `₹5 lakh`** (the original 1934 statutory floor, though actual RBI licensing guidelines today mandate vastly higher thresholds—`₹1,000 crore` for Universal Banks, `₹200 crore` for SFBs, `₹100 crore` for Payments Banks).
2. **Public Interest & Depositor Safety:** It must satisfy the RBI that its affairs are **not being conducted in a manner detrimental to the interests of its depositors**.
3. **Eligible Legal Form:** It must be a State Cooperative Bank, a company defined under the Companies Act, a statutory corporation (such as SBI or nationalised banks), or a foreign-incorporated banking company.

- **Obligations & Privileges of Scheduled Banks:**
  - Must maintain **`CRR` directly with the RBI** under `Section 42(1)` of the `RBI Act` (and `SLR` under `Sec. 24` of the `BR Act`).
  - Enjoy direct access to the **RBI's `LAF` window (`Repo`, `SDF`, `MSF`)**, refinancing at the **Bank Rate**, and **automatic membership in clearing houses** and national payment settlement systems (`RTGS`/`NEFT`).
  - Scheduled Banks comprise **two broad wings**: **(a) Scheduled Commercial Banks (`SCBs`)**—covering Public Sector Banks, Private Sector Banks, Foreign Banks, Regional Rural Banks, Small Finance Banks, and Scheduled Payments Banks—and **(b) Scheduled Cooperative Banks** (select large State Cooperative Banks and Scheduled Urban Cooperative Banks).

#### 2. Non-Scheduled Banks

**Non-Scheduled Banks** are banks **not listed in the Second Schedule** of the `RBI Act, 1934`. Crucially, they are **NOT illegal or unregulated**: they remain licensed and regulated under the **`Banking Regulation Act, 1949`**.

- **Key Statutory Distinctions (Prelims Trap!):**
  - They **do NOT deposit CRR with the RBI** under `Sec. 42` of the `RBI Act`; instead, they must maintain cash reserves **with themselves** (or in current accounts with RBI/SBI/notified banks) under **`Section 18` of the `Banking Regulation Act, 1949`**, alongside SLR under `Sec. 24`.
  - They are **normally ineligible to borrow from the RBI** for regular banking purposes (except under extreme emergency liquidity stress under `Sec. 18` of the `RBI Act`) and do not enjoy automatic clearing-house membership.
  - **Examples:** **Local Area Banks (`LABs`)** (introduced in 1996 to bridge rural credit gaps across 2–3 contiguous districts—only **two** survive today: **Coastal Local Area Bank** in Andhra Pradesh and **Krishna Bhima Samruddhi Local Area Bank** in Telangana/Karnataka, after Capital LAB converted into an SFB and Subhadra LAB's licence was cancelled in 2020) and the majority of smaller **Non-Scheduled Urban Cooperative Banks** and **District Central Cooperative Banks**.

### Commercial Banks: Public Sector, Private Sector and Foreign Banks

**Commercial Banks** are profit-oriented financial intermediaries regulated under the **`Banking Regulation Act, 1949`** that accept public demand and time deposits and extend commercial, industrial, agricultural, and retail credit. As of 2026, Scheduled Commercial Banks (`SCBs`) account for over **90% of total banking system assets** in India.

#### 1. Public Sector Banks (`PSBs`) — From Nationalisation to Mega-Consolidation

A **Public Sector Bank (`PSB`)** is a commercial bank in which the **Government of India holds a majority stake (`> 50%`, specifically `≥ 51%`)**. PSBs form the historical spine of Indian banking, still commanding roughly **55%–58% of total banking deposits and ~52%–54% of credit** while operating the vast rural branch network that powered Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (`PMJDY`).

- **Historical Milestones of Public Ownership:**
  1. **Imperial Bank of India (1921) → State Bank of India (1 July 1955):** The three colonial Presidency Banks (Bank of Bengal 1806, Bank of Bombay 1840, Bank of Madras 1843) merged in 1921 to form the **Imperial Bank of India**. Acting on the **All-India Rural Credit Survey Committee (A.D. Gorwala Committee, 1954)**, Parliament enacted the **`State Bank of India Act, 1955`**, partially nationalising the Imperial Bank (initially with RBI holding controlling stake, later transferred to the Central Government in **2007** following the **Narasimham Committee** recommendation to eliminate conflict of interest between RBI as regulator and owner!). Eight princely-state banks became SBI Associate Banks in 1959.
  2. **First Wave of Bank Nationalisation (19 July 1969):** Arguing that private commercial banks owned by industrial houses ignored agriculture, small industries, and rural branches (credit to agriculture stood below `2%` in 1968!), Prime Minister Indira Gandhi nationalised **14 major private commercial banks** with deposits of **`₹50 crore or more`** through an ordinance ratified as the **`Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1970`** (after the Supreme Court struck down the 1969 Act's compensation clause in the landmark ***R.C. Cooper v. Union of India*** [Bank Nationalisation Case, 1970]).
  3. **Second Wave of Bank Nationalisation (15 April 1980):** Six more private banks with deposits of **`₹200 crore or more`** were nationalised under the **`Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1980`**, taking the state-controlled banking share past `90%` of total deposits. (In 1993, New Bank of India was merged with Punjab National Bank—the first PSB merger.)
  4. **IDBI Bank Reclassification (January 2019):** Following Life Insurance Corporation (`LIC`) acquiring a `51%` controlling stake in IDBI Bank, the RBI formally reclassified **IDBI Bank as a Private Sector Bank** (currently undergoing strategic disinvestment by GoI and LIC).
- **The Mega-Consolidation Drive (2017–2020: `27 PSBs → 12 PSBs`):** To build globally competitive, well-capitalised lenders capable of funding multi-billion-dollar infrastructure projects and reaping economies of scale (echoing **Narasimham Committee I [1991] & II [1998]**), the Central Government executed three sweeping consolidation rounds:
  - **April 2017:** All **5 remaining SBI Associate Banks** (State Bank of Bikaner & Jaipur, Hyderabad, Mysore, Patiala, Travancore) plus **Bharatiya Mahila Bank** merged into **State Bank of India**, vaulting SBI into the global top-50 banks.
  - **April 2019:** **Vijaya Bank** and **Dena Bank** merged into **Bank of Baroda** (India's first three-way PSB amalgamation).
  - **1 April 2020 (Mega-Merger of 10 PSBs into 4):** **Oriental Bank of Commerce + United Bank of India → Punjab National Bank**; **Syndicate Bank → Canara Bank**; **Andhra Bank + Corporation Bank → Union Bank of India**; and **Allahabad Bank → Indian Bank**.

Consequently, India today has exactly **12 Public Sector Banks**—consisting of **State Bank of India** (`SBI Act, 1955`) plus **11 Nationalised Banks** (`1970 / 1980 Acts`):

#### 2. Private Sector Banks (`PVBs`) — Old, New and On-Tap Universal Licensing

In **Private Sector Banks**, the majority equity is owned by private individuals, institutional investors, and corporate/financial promoters. Since the 1991 reforms, private banks have steadily expanded their market share to roughly **40%–42% of total SCB advances** through superior digital onboarding, retail lending analytics, and higher operational efficiency.

- **Old Private Sector Banks:** Pre-1991 private banks that were too small to cross the ₹50 crore (1969) or ₹200 crore (1980) nationalisation thresholds—preserving deep regional loyalty (e.g., **Federal Bank**, **South Indian Bank**, **Karur Vysya Bank**, **City Union Bank**, **Tamilnad Mercantile Bank**, **Karnataka Bank**, **RBL Bank**, **CSB Bank**, and **Jammu & Kashmir Bank** [unique in being majority-owned by the UT Government of J&K yet classified historically as an old private-sector banking company]).
- **New Private Sector Banks:** Licensed post-1991 across three periodic licensing windows following Narasimham Committee I recommendations:
  - *1993 Guidelines:* Birthed **HDFC Bank** (now India's second-largest bank and Bucket 2 D-SIB after its historic **July 2023 reverse merger with parent HDFC Ltd**), **ICICI Bank** (Bucket 1 D-SIB), **Axis Bank** (originally UTI Bank), **IndusInd Bank**, and **IDBI Bank** (initially DFI-promoted).
  - *2001 Guidelines:* Licensed **Kotak Mahindra Bank** (converted from an NBFC) and **Yes Bank** (reconstructed in March 2020 under an RBI–SBI rescue scheme after acute governance and NPA failure).
  - *2013 Guidelines:* Licensed **IDFC First Bank** (converted from IDFC) and **Bandhan Bank** (India's first microfinance NBFC to convert directly into a universal bank in 2015!).
- **On-Tap Universal Banking Licensing (`2016`, Consolidated in `RBI Universal Banks – Licensing Guidelines, 2025` dated 28 Nov 2025):** Instead of issuing bank licences once a decade ("stop-and-go"), RBI moved to continuous **"on-tap" licensing** via its **PRAVAAH** portal:
  - **Minimum Paid-Up Voting Equity Capital / Net Worth:** **`₹1,000 crore`** (doubled from ₹500 crore in the 2016 norms, harmonised across new entrants, converting NBFCs, and transitioning SFBs!).
  - **Eligible Promoters:** Resident Indian individuals/professionals with **10 years of senior banking/finance experience**, private-sector owned/controlled entities (with ≥10-year track record and assets ≥₹5,000 crore *provided* non-financial business does **not** account for ≥40% of total assets/gross income—**excluding large industrial conglomerates!**), and eligible **Small Finance Banks** seeking voluntary transition.
  - **Mandatory `NOFHC` & Initial Capital Buffer:** New private banks must maintain a minimum **`13%` CRAR for the first 3 years**, open at least **`25%` of branches in unbanked rural centres**, and cap individual promoter shareholding progressively down to **`15%` (`26%` long-run cap revised by RBI in Nov 2021)** over 15 years.

#### 3. Foreign Banks

**Foreign Banks** are banking companies incorporated outside India (e.g., Citibank [retail business sold to Axis Bank in 2023; continues institutional banking], HSBC, Standard Chartered, Deutsche Bank, Barclays, JPMorgan Chase, DBS Bank India, SBM Bank India) operating in India under an RBI licence. Since **November 2013**, the RBI has actively nudged systemically relevant foreign banks to convert from **branch mode** into locally incorporated **Wholly Owned Subsidiaries (`WOS`)**—offering WOS entities near-national treatment in branch expansion and acquisition of domestic private banks in return for ring-fenced local capital and governance insulated from parent-country contagion (as demonstrated when **DBS Bank India [WOS]** rescued the stressed **Lakshmi Vilas Bank** in November 2020!).

> [!debate]
> **Mains GS-3 Debate: Should India Privatise Public Sector Banks & Allow Large Industrial Houses to Own Banks?**
> Two major structural debates shape India's banking horizon ahead of Viksit Bharat @2047:
> 1. **PSB Privatisation (Budget 2021–22 Announcement & Poonam Gupta–Arvind Panagariya 2022 Paper):** Proponents argue that state ownership creates political interference, rigid vigilance overhang ("3Cs" — CBI, CVC, CAG), weak HR pay competitiveness, and recurrent taxpayer recapitalisation (₹3.1 lakh crore during FY17–FY21); privatising all PSBs except SBI would boost allocative efficiency. Critics counter that PSBs shoulder unprofitable social mandates—**Jan Dhan zero-balance accounts (`>53 crore`), rural last-mile branches, Mudra/PM SVANidhi micro-credit, and counter-cyclical lending during crises**—and achieved a historic profitability and asset-quality renaissance (`1.9%` PSB GNPA in March 2026!) *without* privatisation, making **P.J. Nayak-style board autonomy & FSIB professionalisation** a safer path than hasty fire-sales.
> 2. **Large Corporate / Industrial Houses in Banking (RBI Internal Working Group, Nov 2020):** In 2020, an RBI Internal Working Group (chaired by P.K. Mohanty) suggested permitting large industrial houses as bank promoters subject to strict `BR Act` connected-lending amendments. Former RBI Governor **Raghuram Rajan** and Deputy Governor **Viral Acharya** vehemently opposed the proposal—warning of **connected lending, concentration of economic/political power, and regulatory capture** (reminding India *why* banks were nationalised in 1969!). Notably, in November 2021 (and reaffirmed in the **Nov 2025 Licensing Guidelines**), the **RBI wisely rejected** opening universal banking licences to large industrial conglomerates, maintaining the strict wall between commerce and banking.

### Regional Rural Banks (RRBs) and the May 2025 One State–One RRB Consolidation

Conceived to bridge the yawning gap between commercial banks (which had capital and professionalism but shunned remote villages) and rural cooperatives (which had local grassroots reach but suffered from weak capital and elite capture), **Regional Rural Banks (`RRBs`)** were established on the recommendation of the **M. Narasimham Working Group (1975)**.

#### 1. Statutory Origin, Hybrid Mandate and Ownership Formula

Following an ordinance on 26 September 1975, the **first five RRBs** were launched on **2 October 1975** (Gandhi Jayanti)—headlined by **Prathama Bank** in Moradabad, Uttar Pradesh (sponsored by Syndicate Bank). Parliament soon enacted the **`Regional Rural Banks Act, 1976`** to govern them permanently.

- **Exact Tripartite Ownership Formula (All-Time Prelims Favourite!):**
  - **Central Government:** **`50%`**
  - **State Government:** **`15%`**
  - **Sponsor Bank** (a designated Public Sector Bank): **`35%`**
  - *(Note: Under the **RRB [Amendment] Act, 2015**, RRBs were permitted to raise capital from outside sources provided the combined shareholding of the Central Government and the Sponsor Bank never falls below **`51%`**.)*
- **Regulation vs Supervision (Classic Prelims Trap!):** RRBs are **Scheduled Commercial Banks** regulated by the **RBI** (which sets their CRR, SLR, interest-rate policy, and licensing), whereas their **statutory on-site inspection and supervision** are delegated to **NABARD** under **`Section 35(6)` of the `Banking Regulation Act, 1949`**!
- **Rural Focus & PSL:** RRBs carry India's highest Priority Sector Lending mandate at **`75%` of ANBC** (including **`18%` Agriculture** and **`15%` Weaker Sections**), with roughly **`92%`** of their branches situated in rural and semi-urban areas.

#### 2. Four Phases of Consolidation: From 196 RRBs to 28 (`One State–One RRB`, May 2025)

By the early 2000s, India had proliferated **196 tiny, geographically fragmented RRBs**, dozens of which suffered chronic losses, high cost-to-income ratios, obsolete technology, and overlapping rural jurisdictions within the same state. Acting on the **V.S. Vyas Advisory Committee (2004)**, the Government launched a systematic four-phase structural consolidation under **`Section 23A(1)` of the `RRB Act, 1976`**:

- **Phase I (`2005–2010`):** Amalgamated RRBs of the *same Sponsor Bank* within a state → **`196 → 82 RRBs`**.
- **Phase II (`2012–2014`):** Amalgamated RRBs *across Sponsor Banks* in contiguous areas within a state → **`82 → 56 RRBs`**.
- **Phase III (`2019–2021`):** Merged weaker RRBs into stronger peers within states → **`56 → 43 RRBs`**.
- **Phase IV — `One State–One RRB` (`Effective 1 May 2025`):** Guided by a 2021 NABARD impact study confirming that prior amalgamations sharply boosted profitability, leverage, and solvency, the Ministry of Finance (DFS) issued Gazette notifications on **5–7 April 2025** under **`Section 23A(1)` of the `RRB Act, 1976`**, amalgamating **26 RRBs across 10 States and 1 Union Territory** (including Andhra Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Bihar, Gujarat, Jammu & Kashmir, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, and Rajasthan) into **11 unified state-level RRBs** effective **1 May 2025**!
  - **Outcome:** Total RRBs nationwide dropped from **`43` to `28` RRBs** operating across **26 States and 2 UTs** (Goa and Sikkim have no RRBs), spanning **over 22,000 branches across ~700 districts**, with every restructured RRB endowed with a uniform **authorised capital of `₹2,000 crore`**—unlocking scale efficiency, unified Core Banking Solutions (`CBS`), stronger digital banking, and lower overhead costs without diluting rural reach.

### Cooperative Banks, End of Dual Control and NUCFDC

Rooted in the **Co-operative Credit Societies Act, 1904**, **Cooperative Banks** are **member-owned, democratically governed financial institutions** operating on the principle of *"one member, one vote"* (regardless of share capital held) and **"no profit, no loss" mutual self-help**—unlike commercial banks that maximise shareholder equity returns. Cooperatives gained explicit constitutional recognition through the **97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011**, which added the right to form cooperative societies under **`Article 19(1)(c)`**, inserted **`Article 43B`** in DPSPs, and added **`Part IXB`** (though the Supreme Court in *Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah*, July 2021 struck down Part IXB's application to *single-state* cooperatives for lacking state ratification under Art. 368(2), while upholding it for **Multi-State Cooperative Societies**).

#### 1. Dual Architectural Structure: Rural versus Urban Cooperative Banks

India's cooperative banking edifice splits into two distinct wings:

1. **Rural Cooperative Credit Institutions** (supervised by **NABARD**, regulated on banking aspects by **RBI** for StCBs/DCCBs):
   - **Short-Term (ST) 3-Tier Pyramid (Crop & Working-Capital Loans):**
     - *Apex Tier (State Level):* **State Cooperative Banks (`StCBs`)** — link rural credit to RBI/NABARD refinance.
     - *Middle Tier (District Level):* **District Central Cooperative Banks (`DCCBs`)** — finance and coordinate village societies.
     - *Grassroots Tier (Village Level):* **Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (`PACS`)** — over 1 lakh village-level member societies currently undergoing **₹2,516 crore Centrally Sponsored PACS Computerisation** and conversion into **Multipurpose PACS (`M-PACS`)** (delivering grain storage, Jan Aushadhi Kendras, CSC digital services, and FPO links under the Ministry of Cooperation).
     - *CRITICAL PRELIMS TRAP:* **`PACS` are NOT "banks" under the `Banking Regulation Act, 1949`!** They are governed exclusively by **State Cooperative Societies Acts** (Registrar of Cooperative Societies [`RCS`]), **NOT regulated by RBI**, cannot use the word "Bank" in their name, and are **NOT covered by DICGC deposit insurance**!
   - **Long-Term (LT) Rural Cooperatives (Term Investment in Irrigation/Farm Machinery):** **State Cooperative Agriculture and Rural Development Banks (`SCARDBs`)** and **Primary Cooperative Agriculture and Rural Development Banks (`PCARDBs`)**—note that these are also **outside RBI's direct banking regulation** and rely heavily on NABARD refinance.
2. **Urban Cooperative Banks (`UCBs` / Primary Cooperative Banks):** Operate in urban and semi-urban centres lending to small traders, artisans, and salaried borrowers. Following the **N.S. Vishwanathan Expert Committee on UCBs (2021)**, the RBI adopted a **Four-Tier Regulatory Framework (Dec 2022)** scaling capital (`CRAR` from `9%` for Tier 1 up to `12%` for Tiers 2–4) and governance rules strictly by deposit size:
   - **Tier 1:** Unit UCBs and single-district UCBs with deposits up to **`₹100 crore`**.
   - **Tier 2:** Deposits **`> ₹100 crore` up to `₹1,000 crore`**.
   - **Tier 3:** Deposits **`> ₹1,000 crore` up to `₹10,000 crore`**.
   - **Tier 4:** Deposits **`> ₹10,000 crore`** (systemically large UCBs subject to near-universal-bank governance).

#### 2. The PMC Bank Crisis (2019), End of Dual Control (2020) and NUCFDC (2024)

Historically, UCBs suffered from a toxic **"Dual Control" regime**: while the **RBI** regulated banking parameters (CRR, SLR, credit norms), the **Registrar of Cooperative Societies (`RCS`)** (State Govt for single-state UCBs; Central Registrar for Multi-State UCBs) controlled incorporation, board elections, CEO appointments, audits, and supersession of boards! Politicised boards frequently indulged in fraudulent connected lending while RBI lacked statutory power to supersede corrupt boards or force timely amalgamations—culminating in the **September 2019 collapse of Punjab and Maharashtra Cooperative (`PMC`) Bank**, where over `70%` of its loan book was secretly siphoned to a single bankrupt real estate group (`HDIL`), trapping lakhs of middle-class depositors.

Parliament and the RBI responded with three decisive structural reforms:

1. **`Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2020`:** Brought **all UCBs and Multi-State Cooperative Banks squarely under RBI's direct governance, audit, and resolution powers**—matching commercial banks! Specifically, RBI gained power to approve/remove CEOs and chairpersons, prescribe "fit-and-proper" board qualifications, supersede errant boards, regulate issuance of equity/preference shares and unsecured debentures to raise capital, and formulate binding reconstruction or amalgamation schemes *without* needing prior moratoriums (used in 2021–22 to merge PMC Bank into **Unity Small Finance Bank**!). Crucially, the Act explicitly preserved the **exemption of `PACS`** and agricultural credit societies from RBI regulation, and left cooperative registration/administrative functions with the RCS.
2. **`NUCFDC` — National Umbrella Organisation for UCBs (2024):** Acting on the **Y.H. Malegam Committee (2011)** and **N.S. Vishwanathan Committee (2021)** recommendations (modelled on Europe's *Rabobank* in the Netherlands and *BVR* in Germany), the RBI granted a final Certificate of Registration (`CoR`) on **8 February 2024** (under `Sec. 45-IA` of the `RBI Act`) to the **National Urban Co-operative Finance and Development Corporation Limited (`NUCFDC`)**, formally inaugurated on **2 March 2024** by Union Home & Cooperation Minister Amit Shah. Operating as a **non-deposit-taking NBFC-ICC (Middle Layer)** owned by member UCBs (targeting ₹300 crore paid-up capital to qualify as a **Self-Regulatory Organisation [`SRO`]**), NUCFDC provides **fund-based support** (capital infusion, emergency liquidity lines, refinancing) and **non-fund-based shared infrastructure** (common cloud Core Banking IT platform, cybersecurity, treasury management, and director/KMP capacity building) so over 1,400 fragmented UCBs can modernise safely.
3. **`Banking Laws (Amendment) Act, 2025` Governance Upgrade:** Effective **1 August 2025**, raised the **maximum continuous tenure of directors** (excluding chairperson and whole-time directors) in **cooperative banks from `8 years` to `10 years`** (aligning with the 97th Constitutional Amendment's 5-year × 2 terms standard, while keeping commercial bank directors at 8 years), and permitted **DCCB directors to serve on the Board of their State Cooperative Bank (`StCB`)** to strengthen federal cooperative coordination.

### Differentiated Banks: Payments Banks and Small Finance Banks

Traditional "one-size-fits-all" universal banks struggled to reach migrant labourers, street vendors, and micro-enterprises profitably because brick-and-mortar branches carried heavy fixed costs. Acting on the landmark **Nachiket Mor Committee** (*Committee on Comprehensive Financial Services for Small Businesses and Low Income Households*, **January 2014**), the RBI introduced **Differentiated (Niche) Banking Licences** in **November 2014**—splitting banking vertically into two specialised models: **Payments Banks** (payments + small savings, zero credit risk) and **Small Finance Banks** (basic banking + micro-credit).

#### 1. Payments Banks (`PBs`)

Licensed under `Sec. 22` of the `Banking Regulation Act, 1949` and registered under the Companies Act, **Payments Banks** are ultra-safe transactional banks focused on **small savings deposits and low-cost digital payments/remittances** for migrant workers, low-income households, and small businesses.

- **Strict Prudential Ring-Fence (Highest-Yield Prelims Rules!):**
  - **Minimum Paid-Up Equity Capital:** **`₹100 crore`** (minimum `CRAR` **`15%`**).
  - **Deposit Ceiling:** Initially capped at `₹1 lakh`, doubled by RBI in **April 2021** to **`₹2 lakh` maximum end-of-day balance per individual customer** (demand deposits only—savings and current accounts; **NO NRI deposits**!).
  - **STRICT PROHIBITION ON LENDING:** Payments Banks **CANNOT undertake ANY lending activity** and **CANNOT issue credit cards**! (They *can* issue ATM/debit cards, internet/mobile banking, UPI, and act as third-party Business Correspondents distributing loans, mutual funds, and insurance of partner banks/NBFCs.)
  - **Narrow-Banking Investment Mandate (`75%` SLR Rule!):** To keep depositor funds virtually risk-free, after maintaining standard `CRR` with RBI, Payments Banks must invest **at least `75%` of their demand deposit balances in `SLR`-eligible Government Securities / Treasury Bills with maturity up to 1 year**, and park a maximum of **`25%`** as current/time deposits with other Scheduled Commercial Banks!
- **Reality Check & Paytm Payments Bank Lesson (2024):** Of 11 entities granted in-principle licences in 2015, only **6 Payments Banks** remain operational today—**Airtel Payments Bank** (first to launch, Jan 2017), **India Post Payments Bank (`IPPB`, 100% GoI-owned via Dept. of Posts)**, **Fino Payments Bank**, **Jio Payments Bank**, **NSDL Payments Bank**, and **Paytm Payments Bank (`PPBL`)**. Because Payments Banks cannot earn lending spreads while UPI merchant fees are zero, profitability proved elusive for most—and in **January 2024**, RBI invoked **`Section 35A` of the `BR Act`** to bar **Paytm Payments Bank** from accepting fresh deposits or top-ups after persistent KYC, governance, and related-party compliance breaches, underscoring RBI's zero tolerance for fintech regulatory arbitrage.

#### 2. Small Finance Banks (`SFBs`) and Universal Bank Transition (AU SFB 2025)

**Small Finance Banks (`SFBs`)** provide the complete bouquet of **basic banking services—both deposit acceptance AND credit lending**—explicitly targeted at unserved and underserved segments: small and marginal farmers, micro and small industries, and unorganised-sector workers.

- **Key Regulatory Norms (Including June 2025 Revisions!):**
  - **Minimum Paid-Up Capital / Net Worth:** **`₹200 crore`** under **December 2019 On-Tap SFB Guidelines** (raised from ₹100 crore in 2014; ₹100 crore initially for transitioning UCBs rising to ₹200 crore within 5 years); minimum `CRAR` **`15%`**.
  - **Granular Micro-Loan Mandate:** At least **`50%` of the SFB's loan portfolio must consist of loans and advances of ticket size up to `₹25 lakh`**—preventing mission drift toward large corporate lending.
  - **Revised PSL Mandate (`60%` eff. FY 2025–26):** Originally mandated at `75%` of ANBC, on **20 June 2025** the RBI **reduced SFBs' overall PSL target from `75%` to `60%` of ANBC/CEOBE** (retaining `40%` core sub-sector allocation + cutting the flexible competitive-advantage component from `35%` to `20%`), granting SFBs greater portfolio diversification and lower rural-stress concentration!
  - **Rural Footprint & Deposit Scope:** Must open at least **`25%` of banking outlets in unbanked rural centres**; unlike Payments Banks, SFBs have **NO cap on deposit size** and can offer fixed deposits, credit cards, and forex services (as AD-II).
- **Voluntary Transition to Universal Bank — Historic AU SFB Milestone (2025–2026):** In **April 2024**, the RBI issued formal eligibility criteria allowing mature SFBs to graduate into **Universal Banks**: minimum **5 years** of satisfactory SFB operations, **listed** on a recognised stock exchange, minimum net worth of **`₹1,000 crore`**, net profit in the preceding **2 financial years**, **`GNPA ≤ 3%` and `NNPA ≤ 1%`** in the preceding 2 FYs, and a diversified loan portfolio (preference to non-promoter-dominated boards). On **7 August 2025**, **AU Small Finance Bank (`AUSFB`)** became the **first SFB in Indian history to receive RBI's `in-principle` approval to transition into a Universal Bank** (with an 18-month completion window; in **March 2026**, RBI further waived the mandatory `NOFHC` holding-company condition for AU SFB unless it establishes group entities in future!).

## 4. Non-Banking Financial Companies and Shadow Banking

Walk into a rural tractor dealership, buy a smartphone on zero-cost EMI, apply for a gold loan in ten minutes, or finance a solar park: chances are high that your lender is not a traditional commercial bank at all, but a **Non-Banking Financial Company (`NBFC`)**. Operating globally under the umbrella term **"shadow banking"** (coined by economist Paul McCulley in 2007 and formally labelled *Non-Bank Financial Intermediation* [`NBFI`] by the Financial Stability Board), NBFCs perform credit intermediation outside the conventional commercial-banking deposit perimeter.

In India, NBFCs are no peripheral sideshow—they are an indispensable **second engine of financial intermediation**, commanding total assets exceeding **25% of India's banking system** and delivering vital **last-mile credit** to used-vehicle operators, small shopkeepers, affordable-housing buyers, self-help micro-borrowers, and long-gestation power and railway projects that risk-averse commercial banks often decline. Yet because NBFCs borrow predominantly from **banks and short-term capital markets** rather than sticky insured retail deposits, distress inside a single giant NBFC (as India witnessed traumatically with **IL&FS in 2018** and **DHFL in 2019**) can instantly freeze credit across the entire economy.

---

### Statutory Definition, the 50–50 Criteria and Banks versus NBFCs

#### 1. Legal Basis and the `50–50` Principal Business Criteria (`PBC`)

An **NBFC** is a company **incorporated under the `Companies Act, 1956 / 2013`** engaged in the business of loans and advances, acquisition of shares/stocks/bonds/securities, leasing, hire-purchase, insurance business, or chit business—but **explicitly excluding** any institution whose principal business is **agriculture, industrial activity, purchase/sale of goods (other than securities), or sale/purchase/construction of immovable property**.

- **Governing Statute (Prelims Trap!):** NBFCs are registered with and regulated by the RBI under **Chapter IIIB (`Section 45-IA`) of the `RBI Act, 1934`**—**NOT under the `Banking Regulation Act, 1949`**!
- **The `50–50` Principal Business Criteria (`PBC`) Test:** Under RBI's statutory test, a company is classified as carrying on financial activity as its "principal business"—and must mandatorily obtain a **Certificate of Registration (`CoR`)** from RBI under `Sec. 45-IA`—when **BOTH** conditions are satisfied simultaneously:
  1. Its **Financial Assets** constitute **more than `50%` of its total assets** (netted off by intangible assets); **AND**
  2. Its **Income from Financial Assets** constitutes **more than `50%` of its gross income**.
- **Minimum Net Owned Fund (`NOF`):** Under RBI's Scale-Based Regulation (`SBR`), the minimum NOF required for registration/operation of core lending categories (`NBFC-ICC`, `NBFC-MFI`, `NBFC-Factors`) was raised from `₹2 crore` to **`₹10 crore`** (with a phased glide-path up to March 2027 for existing legacy entities; `NBFC-P2P` and `NBFC-AA` retain `₹2 crore`, while `HFCs` require `₹20 crore` and `NBFC-IFCs` require `₹300 crore`).

#### 2. Exact Distinctions: Commercial Banks versus NBFCs

This comparison is one of UPSC Prelims' most frequently tested conceptual checklists (appearing in multiple Prelims papers, including 2010, 2017, and 2021 variants):

```compare
# Commercial Banks vs Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs)
Commercial Banks | Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs)
Primary Statute :: Banking Regulation Act, 1949 (& RBI Act, 1934) | Companies Act, 2013 & Chapter IIIB (Sec. 45-IA), RBI Act, 1934
Demand Deposits (CASA) :: YES — Savings & Current accounts are core funding | STRICTLY NO — Cannot accept demand deposits (only legacy NBFC-Ds take fixed deposits)
Payment & Settlement System :: Core members — CAN issue cheques drawn on themselves | NOT part of payment/settlement system — CANNOT issue self-drawn cheques
DICGC Deposit Insurance :: YES — Insured up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank | ZERO DICGC insurance — Depositors bear full credit risk!
Reserve Ratios (CRR & SLR) :: Mandatory CRR (3%) with RBI + SLR (18%) with self | NO CRR and NO SLR (NBFC-Ds hold 15% liquid assets; large NBFCs hold LCR)
Minimum Capital Adequacy (CRAR) :: 9% (+ 2.5% CCB = 11.5% under Indian Basel III) | 15% minimum CRAR (higher buffer due to wholesale funding dependence)

```

> [!prelims]
> **Four Classic UPSC Prelims Traps on NBFCs:**
> 1. **Can NBFCs Accept *ANY* Deposits?** Be razor-sharp with wording! NBFCs **CANNOT accept DEMAND deposits** (withdrawable on demand via cheques/UPI). However, a small, shrinking cohort of RBI-authorised **Deposit-taking NBFCs (`NBFC-D`)**—such as Bajaj Finance, Shriram Finance, and LIC Housing Finance—**ARE allowed to accept PUBLIC TIME (FIXED) DEPOSITS** (for 12–60 months, capped at RBI interest ceilings, non-insured by DICGC). RBI stopped issuing fresh `NBFC-D` licences back in 1997!
> 2. **Housing Finance Companies (`HFCs`) Regulator:** Via the **Finance Act, 2019**, regulatory powers over **HFCs were transferred from `NHB` to the `RBI`**, bringing HFCs officially into the NBFC regulatory fold (while NHB retains supervision and refinancing).
> 3. **Who Regulates Non-RBI Financial Entities?** To avoid overlapping dual regulation, several non-bank financial entities are **exempted from RBI NBFC registration** because another statutory regulator oversees them: **Venture Capital Funds, Merchant Bankers, Stock Brokers, Mutual Funds (`SEBI`)**; **Insurance Companies (`IRDAI`)**; **Pension Funds (`PFRDA`)**; **Nidhi Companies (`Ministry of Corporate Affairs`)**; and **Chit Funds (`State Governments` under the Chit Funds Act, 1982)**.
> 4. **SARFAESI Eligibility for NBFCs:** Originally excluded from the `SARFAESI Act, 2002`, the Central Government progressively notified eligible NBFCs (currently NBFCs with asset size `≥ ₹100 crore` for secured loans `≥ ₹20 lakh`) to enforce security interests directly under SARFAESI without court intervention!

### The IL&FS (2018) and DHFL (2019) Crises: Anatomy of Shadow-Bank Contagion

For nearly two decades after 2000, RBI regulated NBFCs with a deliberately "light-touch" philosophy, reasoning that they did not accept demand deposits and therefore posed minimal systemic risk. Between 2014 and 2018, however, as Public Sector Banks retreated under the weight of the Twin Balance Sheet NPA crisis, NBFCs stepped aggressively into the breach—expanding their loan books at `20%–30%` annually, heavily concentrated in long-gestation **real estate developers and infrastructure projects**.

Crucially, NBFCs funded these **10-to-15-year illiquid assets** by issuing **ultra-short-term 30-to-90-day Commercial Papers (`CPs`) and Non-Convertible Debentures (`NCDs`)** bought eagerly by cash-rich **Liquid and Debt Mutual Funds** and commercial banks. This created a ticking **Asset-Liability Mismatch (`ALM`) time-bomb**: the model worked smoothly *only* as long as mutual funds kept rolling over expiring CPs every quarter.

> [!example]
> **How a Single Default Froze India's Credit Market — The IL&FS (2018) & DHFL (2019) Domino Chain:**
> 1. **The Trigger (`September 2018`):** **Infrastructure Leasing & Financial Services (`IL&FS`)**—a sprawling Core Investment Company sitting atop **348 subsidiaries** carrying **`₹91,000+ crore`** of debt (rated `AAA` by credit agencies barely weeks earlier!)—defaulted on short-term Commercial Paper and inter-corporate deposits.
> 2. **The Liquidity Freeze:** Terrified Debt Mutual Funds refused to roll over CPs across the *entire* NBFC sector indiscriminately. Overnight, borrowing costs spiked, triggering severe liquidity distress even at solvent NBFCs.
> 3. **Second Domino (`June 2019`):** **Dewan Housing Finance Corporation (`DHFL`)**—India's fourth-largest HFC with `₹87,000+ crore` of liabilities—defaulted on bond repayments amid massive promoter-linked real-estate diversion, becoming the **first financial service provider (`FSP`) ever resolved under `IBC` (`Section 227`)** (acquired by Piramal Group in 2021).
> 4. **Systemic Spillover:** Contagion rippled into **Altico Capital**, **Reliance Capital**, **PMC Bank** (2019), **Yes Bank** (2020), and **Franklin Templeton's closure of 6 Indian debt mutual funds (`₹26,000 crore`, April 2020)**—demonstrating conclusively that **giant NBFCs are fully capable of triggering systemic financial crises** even without taking demand deposits!

### RBI Scale-Based Regulation (SBR) Framework (2022–2026)

Determined never to allow another "regulatory arbitrage" blind spot like IL&FS, the RBI issued its landmark **Scale-Based Regulation (`SBR`) Framework** on **22 October 2021** (effective **1 October 2022**, subsequently consolidated in the *RBI [NBFCs – Registration, Exemptions and Framework for Scale Based Regulation] Directions, 2025* and **updated in June–August 2026**).

Guided by the principle of **Proportionality**—*lighter oversight for tiny non-systemic firms, bank-like scrutiny for systemically massive shadow banks*—SBR organises the entire NBFC ecosystem into a **four-layer regulatory pyramid**:

```flow
# RBI Four-Layer Scale-Based Regulation (SBR) Pyramid for NBFCs (2026 Revised)
Top Layer (NBFC-TL) — Ideally EMPTY | Populated ONLY if RBI flags extreme, sudden systemic risk in an Upper Layer entity → triggers steep capital & supervisory curbs
Upper Layer (NBFC-UL) — 17 Entities (FY27 List, Aug 2026) | Revised threshold: Assets ≥ ₹1 lakh crore (added REC, PFC, IRFC, HUDCO); bank-like rules: CET1 ≥ 9%, mandatory stock listing within 3 yrs, 5-yr lock-in
Middle Layer (NBFC-ML) — ~7.0% of Count · 64.6% of Assets | All Deposit-taking NBFCs (NBFC-D), Non-deposit NBFCs ≥ ₹1,000 cr assets, plus HFCs, IFCs, CICs, IDFs & SPDs; stricter governance & concentration limits
Base Layer (NBFC-BL) — ~92.8% of Count · 5.2% of Assets | Non-deposit NBFCs < ₹1,000 cr assets + NBFC-P2P, NBFC-AA, NOFHC & non-public-fund entities; harmonised 90-day NPA glide-path completed by March 2026
```

#### Core Pillar-by-Pillar Features of Scale-Based Regulation (`2022–2026`)

1. **Base Layer (`NBFC-BL`):** Comprises non-deposit-taking NBFCs below **`₹1,000 crore`** asset size (raising the earlier ₹500 crore "systemically important" boundary), plus zero-intermediation entities (`NBFC-P2P`, `NBFC-AA`, `NOFHC`, and NBFCs accessing zero public funds with no customer interface). Though accounting for **`92.8%` of all NBFCs by count**, Base Layer firms hold just **`5.2%` of total NBFC assets** (RBI *Report on Trend and Progress of Banking*, Dec 2025). Crucially, SBR ended the lax 180-day NPA recognition rule for small NBFCs, transitioning Base Layer NBFCs along a multi-year glide-path (`150 days` by Mar 2024 → `120 days` by Mar 2025 → **`90 days` by 31 March 2026**!) to achieve full system-wide NPA parity with banks!
2. **Middle Layer (`NBFC-ML`):** Covers **all deposit-taking NBFCs (`NBFC-D`)** irrespective of size, all non-deposit NBFCs with assets **`≥ ₹1,000 crore`**, plus **HFCs, IFCs, CICs, IDFs, and Standalone Primary Dealers (`SPDs`)**. Accounting for **`7.0%` of entities** yet **`64.6%` of sector assets** (heavily buoyed by large government-owned NBFCs), `NBFC-ML` entities face stricter sensitive-sector exposure caps, mandatory **Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (`ICAAP`)**, independent Chief Risk Officers (`CROs`), and restrictions on lending to directors and senior officers.
3. **Upper Layer (`NBFC-UL`) — Bank-Like Regulation & August 2026 Overhaul:** Reserved for India's largest, most interconnected NBFCs warranting bank-style prudential guardrails:
   - **Bank-Like Prudential Rules:** Must maintain a mandatory **Common Equity Tier 1 (`CET1`) ratio of at least `9%`** within their `15%` CRAR; adhere to a stricter **Large Exposure Framework (`LEF`)**; maintain **differential standard-asset provisioning**; appoint a majority of independent directors; and undergo **mandatory stock-exchange listing within 3 years** of identification! Once classified in `NBFC-UL`, an entity stays under enhanced regulation for **at least 5 years** even if its asset size later drops below threshold.
   - **Verified June–August 2026 Revision (`15 → 17 Entities`):** Initially, RBI identified ~15 Upper Layer NBFCs using a complex 70:30 quantitative/qualitative scoring methodology (where the top 10 by asset size automatically entered). Following a comprehensive FY26 review (during which no FY26 list was published), the RBI replaced the scoring formula in June 2026 with a clean, transparent criterion: **NBFCs with total assets of `₹1 lakh crore` (`₹1 trillion`) or more** qualify for `NBFC-UL`. Consequently, on **6 August 2026**, the RBI published the **FY 2026–27 `NBFC-UL` list expanding to `17` entities**—bringing four state-owned infrastructure giants (**`REC Ltd`, `Power Finance Corporation [PFC]`, `IRFC`, and `HUDCO`**) into the Upper Layer for the first time alongside private titans like **Bajaj Finance, Shriram Finance, LIC Housing Finance, Cholamandalam, Tata Capital, Muthoot Finance, and Tata Sons Pvt Ltd** (whose de-registration application to avoid mandatory IPO listing remains under RBI review; meanwhile PNB Housing Finance and Sammaan Capital exited the list while remaining subject to the 5-year supervisory lock-in!).
4. **Top Layer (`NBFC-TL`):** Intentionally kept **EMPTY**—designed as a macro-prudential "break-glass" chamber where RBI can immediately park any Upper Layer NBFC displaying acute systemic vulnerability, imposing punitive capital add-ons and intensive on-site supervision.

### Bank–NBFC Interconnectedness, Risk Weights and Sector Health (2025–2026)

A central recurring warning in RBI's **Financial Stability Reports (`FSR`)** is the **deep bilateral interconnectedness between Scheduled Commercial Banks and NBFCs**. Banks remain the **single largest source of borrowings for NBFCs** (supplying roughly `35%–40%` of NBFC wholesale funding through term loans, commercial paper, and corporate bonds), while NBFCs simultaneously compete with and co-lend alongside banks in retail personal loans, microfinance, and gold loans.

- **The 2023–2025 Risk-Weight Tightening & Recalibration Cycle:**
  - **16 November 2023 (Pre-Emptive Macro-Prudential Tightening):** Alarmed by breakneck `25%–30%` growth in **unsecured personal loans, credit cards, and bank lending to NBFCs**, the RBI raised **risk weights by `+25 percentage points`** on **(a)** consumer credit exposures of banks and NBFCs (`100% → 125%`, excluding housing, education, vehicle, and gold loans), **(b)** credit-card receivables (`125% → 150%` for banks; `100% → 125%` for NBFCs), and **(c)** **bank exposures to NBFCs** (whenever the external-rating risk weight was below `100%`, e.g., AAA-rated NBFC loans jumped from `20%` to `45%`!). The objective was classic counter-cyclical prudence: force lenders to set aside higher regulatory capital *before* retail delinquencies surged.
  - **25 February 2025 (Calibrated Rollback for Bank Loans to NBFCs):** The tightening worked dramatically—bank credit growth to NBFCs plunged to a four-year low of **`6.7%` in 2024**, squeezing funding for healthy MSME and retail NBFCs. On **25 February 2025** (effective **1 April 2025**), Governor Sanjay Malhotra's RBI **restored risk weights on SCB exposures to NBFCs back to external-rating norms** (removing the `+25 pp` bank-to-NBFC surcharge, alongside targeted microfinance relief), while retaining the higher `125%`–`150%` risk weights on direct unsecured consumer credit and credit cards!
- **Verified NBFC Financial Soundness (`RBI Financial Stability Report, 30 June 2026`):** As of **March 2026**, the NBFC sector exhibited strong macro-resilience alongside emerging niche watchpoints:
  - **Credit Growth:** Robust **`16.6%` YoY** (outpacing bank credit growth of `14.5%`).
  - **Asset Quality:** **`GNPA = 2.4%`**, **`NNPA = 0.8%`**.
  - **Capital & Profitability:** Aggregate **`CRAR = 24.6%`** (`22.3%` across the 174 Upper/Middle Layer stress-test sample—far above the `15%` regulatory floor) and healthy **Return on Assets (`RoA`) of `2.3%`**.

## 5. Non-Performing Assets, Twin Balance Sheet Turnaround and Banking Reforms

A commercial bank is fundamentally a highly leveraged fiduciary: for every ₹10 of its own shareholder equity, it lends roughly ₹90 belonging to public depositors. When loans are serviced punctually, credit acts as the lifeblood of investment and employment. When borrowers default at scale, however, non-repayment does not merely reduce bank profits—it erodes core bank capital, freezes fresh lending to healthy factories and MSMEs, forces sovereign bailouts from taxpayers, and threatens systemic depositor confidence. Between 2015 and 2019, India stared directly into that abyss during the **Twin Balance Sheet (`TBS`) Crisis**, when gross bad loans across Scheduled Commercial Banks (`SCBs`) surged to **`11.2%` of total advances (`14.6%` in Public Sector Banks)**. Over the ensuing decade, an unprecedented combination of clean recognition, legal insolvency overhaul (`IBC`), sovereign recapitalisation, bank consolidation, and strict prudential oversight engineered one of the sharpest banking turnarounds among major economies—slashing SCB Gross NPAs to a multi-decadal low of **`1.8%` in March 2026** (`RBI Financial Stability Report, June 2026`).

Mastering this chapter's concluding section equips aspirants simultaneously for **Prelims precision** (the 90-day & agricultural crop-season NPA rules, `SMA` stages, `SARFAESI` exclusions, `IBC` timelines & the **April 2026 Amendment**, `Basel III` Indian vs global norms, `D-SIBs`, `PCA`, `DICGC`, and the **2025–2026 Project Finance & ECL Directions**) and for **high-scoring Mains GS-3 essays** on sustaining financial stability to finance **Viksit Bharat @2047**.

### Asset Classification Norms, Early Warning Watchlist (SMA) and Core NPA Metrics

In bank accounting, **loans and advances are ASSETS** (because they generate interest income), whereas **public deposits are LIABILITIES** (because the bank owes them back to depositors). An asset becomes **non-performing** the moment it ceases to generate income for the bank.

#### 1. The 90-Day Overdue Rule and the Agricultural Crop-Season Exception

Under RBI's *Income Recognition, Asset Classification and Provisioning (`IRACP`)* norms (most recently consolidated in the November 2025 Commercial Banks IRACP Directions, to be succeeded by the April 2026 ECL Directions on 1 April 2027), a loan or advance is classified as a **Non-Performing Asset (`NPA`)** when:

- **Term Loans:** Interest and/or instalment of principal remains **overdue for a period of more than `90 days`** (i.e., from Day 91 onward).
- **Overdraft (`OD`) / Cash Credit (`CC`) Accounts:** The account remains **"out of order"** for more than `90 days` (outstanding balance continuously exceeds the sanctioned drawing limit, or credits during the previous 90 days are insufficient to cover accrued interest).
- **Bills Purchased and Discounted:** The bill remains overdue for more than `90 days`.
- **Agricultural Loans — Crucial Prelims Exception!** Because farm cash flows follow harvests rather than monthly salaries, applying a rigid 90-day rule would wrongly brand solvent farmers as defaulters:
  - **Short-Duration Crops** (e.g., paddy, wheat, cotton, pulses, maize): Instalment of principal or interest remains overdue for **TWO crop seasons**.
  - **Long-Duration Crops** (crops with crop season longer than one year, e.g., sugarcane, banana, plantation crops): Instalment of principal or interest remains overdue for **ONE crop season**.
- **Borrower-Wise Classification Rule:** NPA status applies **borrower-wise, NOT facility-wise**. If a company defaults for >90 days on *one* working-capital loan with a bank, **all other performing loans of that same borrower with that bank must simultaneously be downgraded to NPA** (preventing banks from hiding distress across multiple accounts).

#### 2. Early-Warning System: Special Mention Accounts (`SMA`) and `CRILC`

Waiting 90 days until an account legally turns into an NPA often leaves too little time to rescue a viable business. To force proactive intervention, the RBI established the **Special Mention Account (`SMA`)** early-warning watchlist (mandating weekly reporting of all borrowers with aggregate exposures **`≥ ₹5 crore`** to RBI's **Central Repository of Information on Large Credits [`CRILC`]**):

- **`SMA-0`:** Principal or interest payment partially or wholly overdue between **`1 and 30 days`**.
- **`SMA-1`:** Principal or interest overdue between **`31 and 60 days`**.
- **`SMA-2`:** Principal or interest overdue between **`61 and 90 days`** (final warning bell before NPA downgrade on Day 91!).

#### 3. Four-Stage Asset Classification Ladder and 2026 ECL Mapping

Once a loan crosses 90 days overdue, it stops being a **Standard Asset** and marches down three progressively severe NPA buckets—forcing banks to set aside larger cash buffers (**provisions**) out of current profits:

| Asset Category | Overdue / Duration Trigger | Minimum RBI Provisioning (IRACP Norms) | Corresponding 2026 ECL Stage (Eff. 1 Apr 2027) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Standard Asset (`SMA-0`)** | Performing / `0–30 days` overdue | `0.25%` (Agri/SME/Individual Housing), `0.40%` (General), `0.75%` (`CRE-RH`), `1.00%` (`CRE`) | **Stage 1** (`12-month ECL` — no Significant Increase in Credit Risk [`SICR`]) |
| **Standard Watchlist (`SMA-1` & `SMA-2`)** | `31–90 days` overdue (stressed but not yet NPA) | Standard asset rates + mandatory `CRILC` reporting (`≥ ₹5 cr`) & resolution review | **Stage 2** (`Lifetime ECL` — rebuttable presumption of `SICR` triggered at **`> 30 days past due`**!) |
| **Sub-Standard Asset (NPA)** | Remained NPA for **`≤ 12 months`** | **`15%`** on secured portion; **`25%`** on unsecured portion (`20%` infra escrow) | **Stage 3** (`Lifetime ECL` — credit-impaired; subject to RBI prudential floors) |
| **Doubtful Asset (NPA)** | Remained in Sub-Standard category for **`> 12 months`** | **Unsecured portion: `100%`**. **Secured portion:** `D1` (up to 1 yr) **`25%`**; `D2` (1–3 yrs) **`40%`**; `D3` (>3 yrs) **`100%`** | **Stage 3** (`Lifetime ECL` + stricter duration-based prudential floors) |
| **Loss Asset (NPA)** | Identified as uncollectible by bank, internal/external auditor, or RBI inspection | **`100%`** provisioning (or written off wholly) | **Stage 3** (`100%` write-down / loss recognition) |

#### 4. Core Analytical Ratios and Busting the "Write-Off ≠ Waiver" Myth

- **Gross NPA (`GNPA`) Ratio:** Total value of all non-performing loans expressed as a percentage of Gross Advances (`GNPA / Gross Advances × 100`). Measures the **headline magnitude** of bad loans before deducting provisions.
- **Net NPA (`NNPA`) Ratio:** **`Net NPA = Gross NPA − Provisions`** (expressed as `%` of Net Advances). Measures the **actual uncovered residual hit** still threatening the bank's equity capital.
- **Provision Coverage Ratio (`PCR`):** **`PCR = (Total NPA Provisions / Gross NPAs) × 100`**. A high PCR (India's SCB aggregate reached **`75.6%`** in March 2026!) means over three-quarters of bad loans are already fully cushioned out of past earnings, protecting depositors and capital even if recoveries disappoint.
- **Slippage Ratio:** Fresh loans slipping into NPA during a year as a percentage of standard advances at the start of the year (moderated for four consecutive years to **`1.2%`** in FY 2025–26!).
- **Technical Write-Off vs Loan Waiver (Vital Prelims & Mains Clarification!):** A **Loan Waiver** (e.g., a state farm-loan waiver) legally extinguishes the borrower's obligation to repay—the bank loses all legal claim and the government compensates the bank. A **Technical / Prudential Write-Off**, by contrast, is purely an **accounting cleanup** permitted under RBI norms once a bank has already made **`100%` provisioning** against a sticky NPA (typically 4+ years old): the bank removes the bad loan from its active balance sheet to optimise tax liabilities and capital ratios, **while keeping the borrower's legal liability 100% intact**! Recovery proceedings continue relentlessly in `DRTs`, `SARFAESI`, and `NCLT`—and any subsequent recovery flows directly into bank profits! (Still, as the RBI June 2026 FSR cautioned, persistently high write-offs in PVBs and FBs can flatter headline GNPA drops unless backed by genuine cash recoveries.)

> [!prelims]
> **High-Yield Prelims Traps on NPA Classification & Asset Quality:**
> 1. **Loans = Assets; Deposits = Liabilities:** On a commercial bank's balance sheet, loans given to borrowers are **assets** and public deposits are **liabilities** (never invert them!).
> 2. **Agri Crop-Season Rule:** Standard non-farm loans turn NPA after **> 90 days overdue**; **short-duration crops** turn NPA after **2 crop seasons** overdue, and **long-duration crops** after **1 crop season** overdue.
> 3. **Borrower-Wise, NOT Account-Wise:** Default on a single loan facility (> 90 days) drags **all other performing accounts of the same borrower** in that bank down to NPA.
> 4. **Technical Write-Off ≠ Waiver:** Writing off a fully provisioned NPA removes it from active books for tax/capital efficiency, **NOT** from the borrower's legal liability to repay!

### The Twin Balance Sheet Crisis (2015–2019), the 4R Strategy and the 2026 Turnaround

#### 1. Anatomy of India's Twin Balance Sheet (`TBS`) Crisis

Popularised by Chief Economic Adviser **Arvind Subramanian** in the ***Economic Survey 2016–17***, the **Twin Balance Sheet (`TBS`) Crisis** described a vicious macroeconomic trap in which **BOTH India's over-leveraged corporate sector AND its heavily exposed Public Sector Banks suffered severely impaired balance sheets simultaneously**—stifling private capital expenditure for nearly a decade.

- **How It Built Up (`2004–2012`):** During India's heady `8%–9%` boom years, Indian conglomerates borrowed massively from PSBs (often with thin promoter equity and lax due diligence, colloquially dubbed *"phone banking"*) to build thermal power plants, steel mills, telecom spectrum bids, and highways—expecting perpetual double-digit demand. Crucially, India had wound down legacy DFIs (`IDBI`, `ICICI` converted into commercial banks), leaving short-deposit-funded PSBs to carry huge **15-year infrastructure asset-liability mismatches**.
- **How It Detonated (`2012–2018`):** Following the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, global steel/power prices crashed, domestic land-acquisition and environmental clearances stalled projects mid-construction, Supreme Court rulings cancelled coal blocks (`2014`) and 2G telecom licences (`2012`), and the rupee weakened—destroying corporate cash flows. Initially, banks hid bad loans via **"extend and pretend"** (evergreening loans through lenient RBI Corporate Debt Restructuring [`CDR`] schemes). Once RBI's strict **Asset Quality Review (`AQR`, 2015)** forced honest recognition, SCB Gross NPAs exploded from **`~2.3%` in 2008 → `4.3%` in March 2015 → a historic peak of `11.2%` in March 2018** (with **PSB GNPA hitting a catastrophic `14.6%`** and 11 of 21 PSBs placed under Prompt Corrective Action!).

#### 2. The Government–RBI `4R` Turnaround Strategy

Rather than piecemeal fixes, the Central Government and RBI executed a coordinated **4R Strategy** between 2015 and 2025:

1. **`Recognition` (Transparent Sunlight):** RBI's **Asset Quality Review (`AQR`, August–December 2015)** under Governor Raghuram Rajan audited large bank balance sheets directly, forced recognition of hidden evergreened accounts, abolished opaque restructuring loopholes (`CDR`, `SDR`, `S4A`), and—after the Supreme Court struck down RBI's rigid 12 February 2018 circular in ***Dharani Sugars and Chemicals Ltd. v. Union of India* (April 2019)** for lacking specific Central Government authorisation under `Sec. 35AA`—issued the flexible, principle-based ***Prudential Framework for Resolution of Stressed Assets* (7 June 2019)** mandating a 30-day review period and Inter-Creditor Agreement (`ICA`) upon default.
2. **`Resolution` (Creditor-Empowering Laws):** Enactment of the **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (`IBC`), 2016**, strengthening of **SARFAESI & DRTs**, and establishment of the **`NARCL–IDRCL` Bad Bank** (2021) to aggregate massive legacy NPAs cleanly off bank books.
3. **`Recapitalisation` (Rebuilding Equity Cushions):** Launching **Mission Indradhanush (August 2015)** followed by a massive **`₹3.1+ lakh crore` sovereign recapitalisation of PSBs between FY 2016–17 and FY 2020–21**—cleverly financed largely through **cash-neutral Sovereign Bank Recapitalisation Bonds** (PSBs subscribed to non-SLR government bonds and GoI immediately infused that cash back as PSB equity, restoring CRAR swiftly without spiking near-term fiscal cash deficits!).
4. **`Reforms` (Governance, Consolidation & Discipline):** Mega-consolidation of PSBs (`27 → 12`), creation of **Banks Board Bureau (`BBB`, 2016)** → upgraded into **Financial Services Institutions Bureau (`FSIB`, July 2022)** for merit-based top appointments, **EASE Reforms (`EASE 1.0` to `EASE 7.0`)**, digital credit underwriting, `CRILC` real-time monitoring, and strict criminal action under the **Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018**.

> [!news]
> **Historic Turnaround Verified — RBI Financial Stability Report (`30 June 2026`, End-March 2026 Data):**
> A full decade after the AQR shock, India's banking system achieved its strongest balance-sheet health in over three decades:
> - **SCB Gross NPA (`GNPA`) Ratio:** Declined to a **multi-decadal low of `1.8%`** in March 2026 (down from `11.2%` peak in March 2018 and `2.3%` in March 2025!). Breakdown: **PSBs `1.9%`** (down from `14.6%` peak!), **Private Sector Banks `1.4%`**.
> - **SCB Net NPA (`NNPA`) Ratio:** Fell to an ultra-clean **`0.4%`** (both **PSBs `0.4%`** and **PVBs `0.4%`**!).
> - **Provision Coverage Ratio (`PCR`):** Robust **`75.6%`**; **Annual Slippage Ratio:** Moderated for the fourth straight year to **`1.2%`**.
> - **Capital Adequacy (`CRAR`):** Reached **`17.5%`** across the 46-bank stress-test sample (`17.7%` aggregate SCBs)—far above the `9%` (`11.5%` with CCB) regulatory minimum!
> - **Macro Stress-Test Resilience (2-Year Horizon to March 2028):** Even under RBI's severe hypothetical adverse scenarios (prolonged geopolitical conflict, oil shocks, elevated inflation), aggregate SCB GNPA rises modestly from `1.8%` to **`1.9%` (Baseline)** and **`3.8%` / `4.1%` (Adverse Scenarios 1 & 2)**, while aggregate CRAR remains comfortably resilient at **`15.6%` (Baseline)** and **`13.3%` / `13.0%` (Adverse)**—and **every single bank meets the `5.5%` minimum CET1 requirement across all scenarios**!
> - **Residual Watchpoints:** **Large-borrower GNPA** dropped sharply to **`1.2%`** (vs `2.4%` in Sep 2024), **BUT Agriculture still exhibits the highest sectoral GNPA at `5.1%`** (accounting for **`37.2%` of all SCB GNPAs**), and elevated technical write-offs in PVBs/FBs require continued vigilance.

### Stressed Asset Resolution Architecture: DRT, SARFAESI, IBC (2016 & 2026) and NARCL

Before 2016, India's insolvency landscape was a fragmented graveyard of overlapping statutes (`Presidency Towns Insolvency Act 1909`, `SICA 1985 / BIFR`, `Companies Act 1956`, `RDDBFI 1993`, `SARFAESI 2002`) where defaulting promoters exploited endless civil stays for 4–8 years while assets rusted away. Understanding all four modern pillars—and their exact statutory limits—is indispensable for UPSC.

#### 1. Debt Recovery Tribunals (`DRTs`, 1993)

Constituted under the **`Recovery of Debts Due to Banks and Financial Institutions (RDDBFI) Act, 1993`** (recommended by the **Tiwari Committee 1981** and **Narasimham Committee I 1991**), **DRTs** (with appeals to **Debt Recovery Appellate Tribunals [`DRATs`]**) are specialised quasi-judicial forums for adjudicating bank recovery claims above **`₹20 lakh`** (raised from `₹10 lakh` in September 2018). While faster than civil courts initially, DRTs suffer from acute judicial vacancies, protracted adjournments, and recovery yields historically hovering around just `5%–15%`.

#### 2. The `SARFAESI Act, 2002` (Out-of-Court Enforcement of Secured Assets)

Enacted on the recommendations of **Narasimham Committee II (1998)** and the **Andhyarujina Committee (1999)**, the **`Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest (SARFAESI) Act, 2002`** revolutionised collateral enforcement by giving banks, financial institutions, and notified NBFCs (`≥ ₹100 cr` assets for loans `≥ ₹20 lakh`) **three direct powers WITHOUT going to court**:

1. **Direct Enforcement of Security Interest (`Section 13`):** Once a secured loan becomes an NPA, the lender issues a **`60-day` statutory demand notice (`Sec. 13(2)`)**. If the borrower fails to repay within 60 days, the bank may directly **take possession of the mortgaged factory, building, or machinery, take over management, or auction the asset** (with District Magistrate assistance under `Sec. 14` where needed)—challengable by the borrower only *after* enforcement before a DRT (`Sec. 17`).
2. **Securitisation & Asset Reconstruction (`ARCs`):** Enabled creation of RBI-regulated **Asset Reconstruction Companies (`ARCs`)** that buy NPAs from banks against Cash + **Security Receipts (`SRs`)**.
3. **Central Registry (`CERSAI`):** Established the **Central Registry of Securitisation Asset Reconstruction and Security Interest of India** to record all equitable mortgages electronically, preventing unscrupulous borrowers from pledging the exact same land or property simultaneously to five different banks!

- **Three Mandatory `SARFAESI` Exclusions (Classic UPSC Prelims Trap!):**
  1. **Applies ONLY to SECURED Loans:** Cannot be invoked for unsecured personal loans, credit cards, or uncollateralised corporate credit!
  2. **EXCLUDES Agricultural Land (`Section 31(i)`):** Banks **CANNOT** seize or auction agricultural land under SARFAESI!
  3. **Minimum Threshold & Remaining Balance (`Sec. 31(h) & (j)`):** Excludes loans where the amount due is **`≤ ₹1 lakh`**, OR where the remaining unpaid amount is **less than `20%` of the principal amount and interest thereon**.

#### 3. Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (`IBC`), 2016 and the April 2026 Amendment Act

Drafted by the **Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee (`BLRC`, 2015)** chaired by **Dr. T.K. Viswanathan** and enacted in **May 2016** (CIRP provisions effective **1 December 2016**), the **`Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016`** consolidated India's broken insolvency laws into a single unified statute executing a historic paradigm shift from **"Debtor-in-Possession"** (where errant promoters stayed in charge under `SICA/BIFR` draining value) to **"Creditor-in-Control"**.

- **Four Institutional Pillars of IBC:**
  1. **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (`IBBI`):** Statutory regulator overseeing insolvency professionals, agencies, and information utilities.
  2. **Adjudicating Authorities (`AA`):** **National Company Law Tribunal (`NCLT`)** for companies and LLPs (appeals → **`NCLAT`** → Supreme Court); **Debt Recovery Tribunal (`DRT`)** for individuals and partnership firms (appeals → **`DRAT`** → Supreme Court).
  3. **Insolvency Professionals (`IPs`) & IP Agencies (`IPAs`):** Licensed turnaround specialists who take over management of the distressed company during resolution.
  4. **Information Utilities (`IUs`):** Electronic repositories (led by **National E-Governance Services Ltd. [`NeSL`]**) storing authenticated financial-default records so courts do not waste months verifying whether a debt exists.
- **Step-by-Step Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (`CIRP`):**
  - **Default Trigger:** Minimum default threshold raised from `₹1 lakh` to **`₹1 crore`** (March 2020) to shield MSMEs from frivolous insolvency triggers. Applications can be filed before NCLT by a **Financial Creditor (`Sec. 7`)**, an **Operational Creditor (`Sec. 9` — suppliers/employees)**, or the **Corporate Debtor itself (`Sec. 10`)**.
  - **Admission & Moratorium (`Sec. 14`):** Upon NCLT admission, an **Interim Resolution Professional (`IRP`)** immediately **suspends the promoter's Board of Directors** and assumes management control, while a statutory **Moratorium** freezes all parallel lawsuits, asset attachments, and recovery proceedings to keep the company running as a **going concern**.
  - **Committee of Creditors (`CoC`) — Financial Creditors ONLY:** The IRP constitutes the **CoC** composed exclusively of **unrelated Financial Creditors** (banks, bondholders—*operational creditors do NOT vote in the CoC!*). A Resolution Plan rescuing the firm requires approval by **`≥ 66%` of CoC voting share** (reduced from `75%` in the 2018 amendment to prevent minority holdouts; routine decisions need `51%`).
  - **`Section 29A` (Anti-Backdoor Promoter Bar):** Inserted in late 2017/2018 to prevent **wilful defaulters, undischarged insolvents, and promoters whose accounts remained NPA for `≥ 1 year`** (along with connected persons) from bidding to buy back their own distressed company at a massive haircut! (Exempted selectively for bona fide MSME promoters under `Sec. 240A` because MSMEs rarely attract outside bidders.)
  - **Statutory Timeline (`180 + 90 = 270` → `330 Days`):** Originally `180 days` (extendable once by `90 days` = `270 days`), the **2019 Amendment** set an outer limit of **`330 days`** inclusive of all litigation extensions (ruled directory rather than rigid in *Essar Steel*). If no plan passes within the timeline, the company enters **Liquidation** governed by the **`Section 53` Waterfall Mechanism** (1st: CIRP/liquidation costs; 2nd: **Workmen's dues [24 months] *pari passu* with Secured Creditors**; 3rd: Employee wages [12 months]; 4th: Unsecured Financial Creditors; 5th: **Government tax dues** [crucially subordinated *below* secured & unsecured financial creditors!]; 6th: Preference/Equity shareholders).
  - **Pre-Packaged Insolvency Resolution Process (`PPIRP`, April 2021):** Tailored exclusively for distressed **MSMEs** (minimum default **`₹10 lakh`**), combining a hybrid **"debtor-in-possession with creditor oversight"** model where the MSME negotiates a base resolution plan informally with `66%` financial creditors *before* approaching NCLT—slashing the statutory timeline to **`120 days`** (`90 days` plan submission + `30 days` NCLT approval).

> [!case]
> **Two Foundational Supreme Court Judgments Anchoring IBC Jurisprudence:**
> 1. ***Swiss Ribbons Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India* (January 2019, 2-Judge Bench — Justice R.F. Nariman):** Upheld the **entire constitutional validity of IBC, 2016** against Article 14 challenges. Held that distinguishing **Financial Creditors** (large-ticket, long-term financiers assessing viability) from **Operational Creditors** (trade suppliers) rests on an **intelligible differentia**; upheld **`Section 29A`** barring defaulting promoters from backdoor buybacks; and observed memorably that **"the defaulter's paradise is lost — the economy's rightful position has been regained."**
> 2. ***Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel India Ltd. v. Satish Kumar Gupta* (November 2019, 3-Judge Bench — Justice R.F. Nariman):** Resolved the landmark `₹42,000 crore` ArcelorMittal acquisition of Essar Steel, establishing three bedrock doctrines: **(a) Supremacy of CoC's "Commercial Wisdom"** (NCLT/NCLAT can exercise only limited judicial review and cannot override how the CoC distributes proceeds between secured and unsecured creditors); **(b) Equitable ≠ Equal Treatment** (secured creditors hold superior priority over unsecured/operational creditors); and **(c) Struck down the word "mandatorily"** in the 330-day cap so viable rescues aren't killed by judicial delays outside litigants' control!

- **Empirical Assessment of IBC (`IBBI Data up to September 2026`, Q2 FY27) and the `IBC (Amendment) Act, 2026`:**
  - **What Worked:** Since 2016, IBC has engineered a **behavioural deterrence revolution**—roughly **`29.6%` of admitted CIRPs** were withdrawn or settled before resolution as promoters rushed to repay debts rather than lose control of their companies! Across resolved firms up to **September 2026**, creditors realised **`₹4.46 lakh crore`**, representing **`167.35%` of liquidation value** and **`95.44%` of fair value**—proving resolution preserves far more enterprise value than piecemeal liquidation!
  - **Two Persistent Critiques:** First, headline realisations equalled **`30.19%` of admitted claims** (a `~69.8%` nominal "haircut"—though IBBI notes that admitted claims in ancient legacy cases are massively inflated by years of compounded penal interest over original principal!). Second, **acute NCLT delays**: the average approved resolution plan took **`890 days`** (excluding NCLT-excluded periods) and **`1,005 days`** overall—nearly triple the 330-day statutory goal, with `1,917` CIRPs still pending at end-September 2026!
  - **The Legislative Breakthrough — `Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2026` (`Act No. 6 of 2026`, Presidential Assent 6 April 2026):** Originating as the August 2025 Bill scrutinised by a **Lok Sabha Select Committee chaired by Shri Baijayant Panda** (reported 17 Dec 2025) and passed by Parliament in March–April 2026, this sweeping overhaul directly attacks NCLT bottlenecks through six reforms:
    1. **`Creditor-Initiated Insolvency Resolution Process (CIIRP)`:** Introduces a rapid **out-of-court alternative** for specified financial institutions where the corporate debtor's board remains in place under an RP and NCLT oversight—preventing business disruption and easing NCLT dockets!
    2. **Mandatory 14-Day Admission:** Tightens NCLT's statutory obligation to admit undisputed Financial Creditor applications within `14 days` (cutting pre-admission delays that previously averaged 6–12 months!).
    3. **Group Insolvency & Cross-Border Insolvency:** Inserts enabling frameworks to resolve interconnected multi-subsidiary corporate groups (learning from Videocon/IL&FS) and coordinate cross-border asset recovery (aligned with the **UNCITRAL Model Law**).
    4. **Guarantors' Assets Inclusion:** Permits integrating personal/corporate guarantors' assets into the main resolution plan with CoC approval, closing valuation leakages.
    5. **Enhanced CoC Control in Liquidation:** Empowers the CoC to supervise liquidation directly and approve asset transfers seamlessly.
    6. **Heavy Penalties for Frivolous Litigation:** Empowers NCLT and DRTs to impose fines of **`₹1 lakh to ₹2 crore`** on vexatious applications filed purely to stall resolution!

#### 4. `NARCL` ("Bad Bank") and `IDRCL` (2021–2026)

Even with IBC, resolving massive legacy NPAs exceeding `₹500 crore` stuck across 15–20 consortium banks suffered from severe inter-creditor coordination paralysis and vigilance fear among PSB executives. Announced in Union Budget 2021–22, India launched a **twin Bad Bank architecture**:

- **`NARCL` (National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited):** Incorporated in July 2021 as a **public-sector-majority ARC (`PSBs hold ≥ 51%`**, led by **Canara Bank** as sponsor bank with ~12% stake), registered with RBI under `SARFAESI Act, 2002`. NARCL aggregates and acquires fully-provisioned legacy NPAs **`> ₹500 crore`** via a **`15%` Cash + `85%` Security Receipts (`SRs`)** structure.
- **Sovereign Guarantee Backstop (`₹30,600 Crore`):** To make banks willing to accept `85%` SRs without fear of hidden write-downs, the Central Government provided a **5-year `₹30,600 crore` sovereign guarantee** covering any shortfall between the face value of SRs and actual cash realised upon final resolution or liquidation!
- **`IDRCL` (India Debt Resolution Company Limited):** A **private-sector-majority operational asset manager (`Private banks/FIs hold 51%`; `PSBs/PFIs ≤ 49%`)** hired by NARCL on a **principal-agent basis** to bring specialised private turnaround talent, restructure distressed businesses, and attract buyers.
- **Verified Progress (`PIB, 30 April 2026`):** After an initially slow take-off in 2022–2024, NARCL accelerated strongly—having acquired **33 stressed borrower entities with aggregate debt exposure of `₹1.65 lakh crore`** (closing in on its ₹2 lakh crore initial target) and achieving **`₹6,345 crore` in cumulative cash recoveries**, of which **`₹4,364 crore` (~70% of all recoveries to date!)** was realised in **FY 2025–26 alone**!

### Global Prudential Standards (Basel III), D-SIBs, PCA, DICGC and 2025–2026 Reforms

Preventing the *next* banking crisis requires building shock-absorbing capital and liquidity buffers *before* defaults strike.

#### 1. The Basel III Capital and Liquidity Accord (`BCBS`, 2010)

Formulated by the **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (`BCBS`)** hosted by the **Bank for International Settlements (`BIS`)** in Basel, Switzerland following the **2008 Global Financial Crisis** (succeeding **Basel I [1988]** — simple `8%` credit-risk CRAR — and **Basel II [2004]** — three-pillar architecture adding market & operational risk), **Basel III** dramatically raised both the **quantity and quality of bank capital** and introduced the world's first **harmonised global liquidity ratios**.

- **Three Pillars of Basel III:**
  1. **Pillar 1 — Minimum Capital & Liquidity Requirements:** Quantitative risk-weighted rules covering **Credit Risk, Market Risk, and Operational Risk** (`CRAR = Regulatory Capital / Risk-Weighted Assets [RWAs] × 100`).
  2. **Pillar 2 — Supervisory Review Process:** Banks conduct internal risk self-assessments (**`ICAAP`**), reviewed rigorously by the RBI (**`SREP`**), which can impose bank-specific capital add-ons.
  3. **Pillar 3 — Market Discipline:** Mandatory quarterly public disclosures of capital structure, NPAs, leverage, and risk exposures so investors and depositors price bank risk transparently.
- **Why RBI's Indian Basel III Norms Are STRICTER Than Global Norms (Classic Prelims Favourite!):** Because emerging economies face sharper macroeconomic volatility and higher sovereign-bank linkages, the RBI deliberately prescribed **`+100 bps` higher minimum capital** than BCBS global standards!

| Basel III Prudential Metric | Global BCBS Standard | RBI Prescription for Indian SCBs | Core Economic Purpose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Common Equity Tier 1 (`CET1`)** | `4.5%` of RWA | **`5.5%`** of RWA | Highest-quality loss-absorbing paid-up equity + retained earnings |
| **Additional Tier 1 (`AT1`, incl. Perpetual / CoCo Bonds)** | `1.5%` of RWA | **`1.5%`** of RWA | Going-concern subordinated bonds (famously written down in Yes Bank 2020!) |
| **Minimum Tier 1 Capital (`CET1 + AT1`)** | `6.0%` of RWA | **`7.0%`** of RWA | Core going-concern capital absorbing losses while bank stays open |
| **Tier 2 Capital** | `2.0%` of RWA | **`2.0%`** of RWA | Gone-concern subordinated debt & general provisions protecting depositors in liquidation |
| **Minimum Total `CRAR` (Tier 1 + Tier 2)** | **`8.0%`** of RWA | **`9.0%`** of RWA *(SCB actual: **`17.5%`** Mar 2026!)* | Hard baseline solvency threshold |
| **Capital Conservation Buffer (`CCB` — in CET1)** | `2.5%` of RWA | **`2.5%`** of RWA | Rainy-day buffer built in good times; breach restricts dividends/bonuses |
| **Minimum Total `CRAR + CCB`** | **`10.5%`** of RWA | **`11.5%`** of RWA | True operational capital floor for Indian commercial banks! |
| **Counter-Cyclical Capital Buffer (`CCyB`)** | `0% – 2.5%` | `0% – 2.5%` *(currently `0%`)* | Macro-prudential brake activated during excessive credit-to-GDP booms |
| **Leverage Ratio (`Tier 1 / Total Unweighted Exposure`)** | `3.0%` | **`3.5%`** (**`4.0%`** for D-SIBs!) | Simple non-risk-weighted cap preventing banks from gaming RWA formulas |
| **Liquidity Coverage Ratio (`LCR`)** | **`≥ 100%`** | **`≥ 100%`** | **Short-term (30-day) survival:** High-Quality Liquid Assets (`HQLA`) ≥ 30-day net cash outflows |
| **Net Stable Funding Ratio (`NSFR`)** | **`≥ 100%`** | **`≥ 100%`** (eff. Oct 2021) | **Long-term (1-year) ALM stability:** Available Stable Funding ≥ Required Stable Funding |

#### 2. Domestic Systemically Important Banks (`D-SIBs` — "Too Big to Fail")

Under RBI's **D-SIB Framework (July 2014, updated Dec 2023)**—the Indian counterpart to the Financial Stability Board's Global SIBs (`G-SIBs`)—banks whose failure would inflict catastrophic disruption on the national financial system and real economy are identified annually based on four indicators: **Size (`40%`), Interconnectedness (`20%`), Substitutability (`20%`), and Complexity (`20%`)**. D-SIBs must hold **extra Common Equity Tier 1 (`CET1`) capital surcharges** *over and above* the `5.5%` CET1 + `2.5%` CCB (`8.0%`) requirement, across five buckets (`0.20%` to `1.00%`):

- **Verified RBI D-SIB List (`2 December 2025` — Latest Active Classification):**
  1. **State Bank of India (`SBI`):** Placed in **Bucket 4 → `+0.80%` additional CET1** (total minimum `CRAR + CCB + D-SIB = 12.30%`!).
  2. **HDFC Bank:** Placed in **Bucket 2 → `+0.40%` additional CET1** (total `11.90%` — moved up from Bucket 1 to Bucket 2 after its July 2023 HDFC merger!).
  3. **ICICI Bank:** Placed in **Bucket 1 → `+0.20%` additional CET1** (total `11.70%`).
  - *(Note: **Bucket 3 [`0.60%`]** and **Bucket 5 [`1.00%`]** are currently empty; India has **zero** G-SIBs on the global FSB list.)*

#### 3. Prompt Corrective Action (`PCA`) Framework and `DICGC` Deposit Insurance

- **Prompt Corrective Action (`PCA` — Introduced 2002; Overhauled Effective 1 January 2022):** An early-intervention supervisory guard-rail triggered automatically whenever a bank's financial health slips across **THREE quantitative parameters** (note: *Return on Assets [RoA]* was removed as a direct trigger in the November 2021 revision!):
  1. **Capital:** Breach of **`CRAR`** (below `11.5%`) and/or **`CET1`** (below `8.0%`).
  2. **Asset Quality:** **`Net NPA (NNPA)`** reaching **`≥ 6.0%`** (Risk Threshold 1), **`≥ 9.0%`** (RT2), or **`≥ 12.0%`** (RT3).
  3. **Leverage:** **`Tier-1 Leverage Ratio`** falling below `4.0%` (RT1) / `3.5%` (RT2).
  - *Escalating Curbs:* RT1 restricts dividends and promoter remittances; RT2 additionally restricts branch expansion and capital expenditure; RT3 restricts credit expansion to unrated/high-risk borrowers, caps management compensation, and empowers RBI to supersede the board or trigger forced merger/resolution. *(Importantly, RBI extended a dedicated **PCA Framework to NBFCs effective October 2022**—excluding Government-owned NBFCs initially, extended to them from October 2024—and maintains a parallel **Supervisory Action Framework** for UCBs! Payments Banks and SFBs were excluded from the 2022 bank PCA circular.)*
- **`DICGC` (Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation — `DICGC Act, 1961`):** A **wholly owned subsidiary of the RBI** insuring bank depositors against bank failure:
  - **Coverage Ceiling:** **`₹5 lakh` per depositor per bank** (covering **principal AND accrued interest combined** across all accounts held in the same capacity and right—increased five-fold from ₹1 lakh on **4 February 2020**!).
  - **`90-Day` Statutory Payout Guarantee (`DICGC [Amendment] Act, 2021`):** Previously, depositors of frozen banks (like PMC Bank) waited 5–10 years for final liquidation before receiving insurance. The 2021 Amendment legally guarantees depositors **interim access to their insured funds (up to ₹5 lakh) within `90 days`** from the date the RBI imposes moratorium/All-Inclusive Directions (`AID`)!
  - **Who Pays Premium & Who Is Covered? (Prelims Trap!):** Insured **banks pay the insurance premium** to DICGC (NOT depositors—banks cannot pass the premium directly onto customers!). Covers **all Commercial Banks (PSBs, PVBs, Foreign branches in India, RRBs, SFBs, Payments Banks, LABs) and licensed Cooperative Banks**—and **STRICTLY EXCLUDES NBFCs, Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (`PACS`), inter-bank deposits, and deposits of Central/State/Foreign Governments**!

#### 4. Next-Generation 2025–2026 Prudential Reforms: Project Finance & Expected Credit Loss (`ECL`)

Moving decisively from post-crisis firefighting to global best-in-class *forward-looking* risk management, the RBI enacted two landmark regulatory overhauls in 2025–2026:

1. **`RBI (Project Finance) Directions, 2025` (Issued 19 June 2025; Effective 1 October 2025):**
   - Harmonises project-finance regulation across **Commercial Banks (including SFBs), NBFCs/HFCs, UCBs, and AIFIs** (closing regulatory gaps left outside the 2019 Prudential Framework).
   - Caps permissible extensions of the **Date of Commencement of Commercial Operations (`DCCO`)** at **`3 years` for infrastructure projects** and **`2 years` for non-infrastructure projects**, giving lenders commercial flexibility within hard guardrails.
   - **Rationalised Provisioning:** After industry pushback against May 2024's proposed `5%` construction-phase provisioning, RBI calibrated final **under-construction standard-asset provisioning** to **`1.00%` for infrastructure & CRE-Residential Housing (`CRE-RH`)** and **`1.25%` for Commercial Real Estate (`CRE`)**—falling upon commercial operations to **`0.40%` (infra/others)**, **`0.75%` (`CRE-RH`)**, and **`1.00%` (`CRE`)** (with additional `0.375%` infra / `0.5625%` non-infra provisioning for every quarter of DCCO deferment, and grandfathering existing projects that achieved financial closure before 1 October 2025).
2. **`RBI Expected Credit Loss (ECL) Directions, 2026` (*Commercial Banks – Asset Classification, Provisioning and Income Recognition Directions, 2026*, Notified 27 April 2026; Effective 1 April 2027):**
   - Represents the **single biggest accounting & risk-management shift in Indian banking since Basel III**!
   - Under India's legacy **Incurred-Loss (`IRACP`) model**, banks recognised heavy provisions *only after* a borrower had already defaulted for 90 days—creating pro-cyclical "too little, too late" profit cliffs during downturns (as seen in 2015–18).
   - Aligned with international accounting standards (`IFRS 9` / `Ind AS 109`), the **ECL framework** compels commercial banks (excluding SFBs, Payments Banks, LABs, and RRBs for now) to estimate **forward-looking, probability-weighted expected credit losses** using macroeconomic forecasts at every reporting date across **Three Stages**:
     - **Stage 1 (`12-month ECL`):** Performing assets with no Significant Increase in Credit Risk (`SICR`) (SLR G-Secs & sovereign exposures carved out).
     - **Stage 2 (`Lifetime ECL`):** Watchlist assets experiencing `SICR`—with a mandatory **rebuttable presumption triggered at `> 30 days past due`** (`60 days` for revolving facilities)—forcing banks to provision for *entire remaining lifetime losses* well before NPA Day 91!
     - **Stage 3 (`Lifetime ECL`):** Credit-impaired assets (retaining India's 90-day NPA definition + RBI prudential provisioning floors), paired with the **Effective Interest Rate (`EIR`)** income-recognition method.
   - **Smooth 4-Year Glide-Path (`2027–2031`):** To prevent any abrupt capital shock on 1 April 2027, excess Day-1 ECL provisions over legacy IRACP provisions can be absorbed via opening reserves and phased into **CET1 capital over four years (April 2027 – March 2031)** using a declining **`80% → 20%` regulatory capital add-back**!

### Governance Reforms, Landmark Committees and Mains GS-3 Way Forward

While balance-sheet cleaning restored India's banks to peak solvency, financing **Viksit Bharat @2047**—requiring sustained `7.5%–8%` real GDP growth, green-energy transition capex, manufacturing scale-up, and universal MSME formalisation—demands moving from *balance-sheet repair* to **deep institutional governance, technological resilience, and capital-market depth**.

#### 1. P.J. Nayak Committee (`2014`), `FSIB` and `EASE` Reforms

In **May 2014**, the RBI's **Committee to Review Governance of Boards of Banks in India**, chaired by former Axis Bank CEO **Dr. P.J. Nayak**, diagnosed the structural governance handicap of Public Sector Banks: **dual control** by both the RBI (regulator) and the Department of Financial Services (`DFS`, Ministry of Finance — owner), short 2–3 year CEO tenures hindering long-term strategy, bureaucratic board appointments, and relentless fear of hindsight investigation by vigilance agencies (`CBI`, `CVC`, `CAG`). Its signature recommendations shaped the last decade of reforms:

- **Implemented Partially:** Creation of the **Banks Board Bureau (`BBB`, 2016)** to depoliticise top appointments—upgraded in **July 2022** into the **Financial Services Institutions Bureau (`FSIB`)** selecting whole-time directors and non-executive chairpersons across PSBs, public-sector insurers, and AIFIs; introduction of **EASE Reforms (`Enhanced Access and Service Excellence` — progressing from `EASE 1.0` in 2018 through `EASE 7.0` in 2024–25)** institutionalising data-analytics underwriting, digital customer journeys, cyber defence, and MSME turnaround metrics; and legal safeguards under **`Section 17A` of the Prevention of Corruption (Amendment) Act, 2018** requiring prior sanction before investigating bona fide commercial banking decisions.
- **Unfinished Agenda:** Transferring GoI's bank shareholdings into an autonomous **Bank Investment Company (`BIC`)** (to end DFS micro-management), repealing the separate Bank Nationalisation & SBI Acts to bring PSBs under uniform `Companies Act` corporate governance, and further lengthening executive tenures with market-linked talent recruitment for AI, cybersecurity, and treasury risk.

## 1. Monetary Policy Framework and Flexible Inflation Targeting

Terms: `2016–21` → `2021–26` → **Renewed unchanged on 25 March 2026 for `1 Apr 2026 – 31 Mar 2031`** (retained Headline over Core CPI to curb food-to-wage spillovers).

## 2. Instruments of Monetary Policy and Policy Transmission

- **LAF Corridor (`50 bps` Symmetric; Operating Target = `WACR`):**
  - **Policy Repo (`5.50%`):** Short-term collateralised borrowing from RBI against **excess G-Secs OVER SLR** (hike = anti-inflation; cut = growth).
  - **SDF (`5.25%` = `Repo − 25 bps`):** Introduced **8 Apr 2022 (`Sec. 17(1A)`)**; **UNCOLLATERALISED overnight absorption floor** replacing Reverse Repo; counts for **`SLR`, NOT `CRR`**.
  - **MSF (`5.75%` = `Repo + 25 bps`):** Penal overnight ceiling (2011); allows dipping **up to `2%` of NDTL INTO SLR**; equals Bank Rate. **Fixed Reverse Repo (`3.35%`)** inactive floor; fine-tuning via **VRRR / VRR**.
- **Reserve Ratios:** **CRR (`Sec. 42` RBI Act; `3.00%`)**: **CASH ONLY with RBI**, **ZERO interest** (2006), **NO floor/ceiling**, `90%` daily min. **SLR (`Sec. 24` BR Act; `18.00%`)**: **Cash + Gold + G-Secs/SDLs + SDF kept WITH BANK ITSELF**, earns return, **NO floor (2007), `40%` ceiling**. **2025 Fortnight Trap (`Banking Laws Amend Act, 2025`, eff. 15 Dec 2025):** Redefined CRR/SLR fortnight from alternate Fridays to **1st–15th & 16th–last calendar day**!
- **OMO, MSS, Bank Rate & LTRO:** **OMO** = permanent G-Sec buy (injects `M0`) / sell (absorbs `M0`); **Operation Twist** = buy long + sell short (liquidity-neutral, flattens yield curve). **MSS (2004)** = special Govt bonds sterilising forex inflows; funds **impounded in ring-fenced RBI account** (no deficit financing), **Centre bears interest cost**. **Bank Rate (`Sec. 49`, `5.75%`)** = long-term uncollateralised penal rate aligned with MSF. **LTRO/TLTRO (2020)** = 1–3 yr repo liquidity.
- **Qualitative Tools & PSL (2025 Master Directions eff. 1 Apr 2025):** Margin (`100%−LTV`), consumer risk weights, credit rationing (`LEF` 20%/25% Tier-1), moral suasion, direct action (`Sec. 35A`). **8 PSL categories** (Housing caps raised `₹50L/₹45L/₹35L`; Renewable Energy `₹30→₹35 cr`). **Targets (`%` ANBC/CEOBE):** **Domestic SCBs & Large Foreign Banks (`≥20` br) = `40%`** (`18%` Agri [`10%` SMF], `7.5%` Micro, `12%` Weaker); **Foreign (`<20` br) = `40%`** (`≤32%` export); **RRBs = `75%`** (`15%` Weaker); **SFBs = CUT `75% → 60%` eff. FY26 (20 Jun 2025: `40%` core + `20%` flexible!)**; **UCBs = `60%`** (Apr 2025). **PSLCs** (e-Kuber trading without loan/risk transfer); shortfall parked in **NABARD `RIDF` (1995–96)**; `125%/90%` district weights.
- **Transmission & Loan Pricing:** 5 channels (Interest, Credit, Asset Price, Exchange Rate, Expectations). Evolution: **BPLR (2003)** → **Base Rate (2010, average cost)** → **MCLR (2016, marginal internal)** → **`EBLR` (Oct 2019, Janak Raj Panel — mandatory for floating retail & MSME loans, Repo/T-Bill linked, ≤3-mo reset; `60%+` loans)**. Frictions: sticky small-savings rates, **`82.06%` C-D ratio (Oct 2026)**, fixed term deposits.

## 3. Structure of the Indian Banking System

- **Scheduled vs Non-Scheduled:** **Scheduled** (`Second Schedule, Sec. 42(6)` RBI Act: `≥₹5L` capital+reserves) hold CRR at RBI, access LAF/clearing houses (SCBs + Scheduled Coops). **Non-Scheduled** (`BR Act 1949`) keep CRR **with themselves (`Sec. 18` BR Act)**, no regular RBI borrowing (e.g., **2 LABs** — Coastal & Krishna Bhima Samruddhi — & small UCBs).
- **Commercial Banks:** **12 PSBs (`≥51%` GoI):** SBI (`1 Jul 1955`, Gorwala Panel; GoI bought RBI stake 2007); **19 Jul 1969** (`14` banks `≥₹50 cr` — *R.C. Cooper* 1970); **15 Apr 1980** (`6` banks `≥₹200 cr`); **IDBI Private (Jan 2019)**; **Mega-Mergers (`2017–20`: `27 → 12 PSBs`)** [SBI+5 Assoc+BMB; BoB+Vijaya+Dena; PNB+OBC+UBI; Canara+Syndicate; Union+Andhra+Corp; Indian+Allahabad]. **PVBs (`21`):** Old vs New (`1993/2001/2013`; HDFC merged HDFC Ltd Jul 2023); **Nov 2025 On-Tap Universal Licensing**: `₹1,000 cr` min net worth, `13%` initial CRAR, `25%` rural branches, **industrial houses EXCLUDED**. **Foreign Banks:** Branch vs **WOS (2013)** (DBS India rescued LVB 2020).
- **RRBs (`Narasimham Working Group 1975` → `2 Oct 1975` Prathama Bank → `RRB Act 1976`):** Ownership **`50%` Centre : `15%` State : `35%` Sponsor Bank** (`2015 Amend`: Centre+Sponsor `≥51%`); regulated by **RBI**, **supervised by NABARD (`Sec. 35(6)`)**; `75%` PSL. **4 Consolidation Phases (`196 → 28`):** `05–10` (196→82), `12–14` (82→56), `19–21` (56→43), **Phase IV `One State–One RRB` (eff. `1 May 2025`, `Sec. 23A(1)`: 26 RRBs across 10 States & 1 UT merged → `43 → 28 RRBs`, `₹2,000 cr` auth. capital, >22,000 branches, 92% rural/semi-urban)**.
- **Cooperatives & 2024–2025 Reforms:** `1 member = 1 vote` (97th Amend `Art. 19(1)(c), 43B, Part IXB` — *Rajendra N. Shah* 2021). **Rural ST:** `StCB → DCCB → PACS` (**PACS NOT RBI-regulated & NO DICGC!**); LT: `SCARDB/PCARDB` (outside RBI). **UCBs:** 4-Tier RBI norms (Dec 2022, Vishwanathan Panel); post-**PMC Bank (2019)** → **`BR (Amend) Act, 2020`** ended dual-control loopholes (RBI controls UCB governance/audits/mergers; PACS exempt). **`NUCFDC`** (UCB Umbrella Org: RBI CoR **8 Feb 2024**, launched **2 Mar 2024**, NBFC-ICC ML, IT/liquidity/SRO). **`Banking Laws (Amend) Act, 2025` (15 Apr 2025):** Substantial interest `₹5L → ₹2 cr` & coop director tenure **`8 → 10 yrs`** (`1 Aug 2025`); **up to 4 nominees** (`1 Nov 2025`: simultaneous/successive deposits; successive-only lockers); calendar fortnight (`15 Dec 2025`).
- **Differentiated Banks (`Nachiket Mor, 2014`) & 5 AIFIs:** **Payments Banks (`6`):** `₹100 cr` capital, **`₹2L` deposit cap**, **NO lending / NO credit cards / NO NRI deposits**, **`75%` SLR in `≤1-yr` G-Secs** (Airtel, IPPB, Fino, Jio, NSDL, Paytm PB [`Sec. 35A` curbs 2024]). **SFBs (`11`):** `₹200 cr` capital, `≥50%` loans `≤₹25L`, **PSL cut `75%→60%` (Jun 2025)**, `25%` rural branches; **AU SFB = first SFB granted RBI Universal Bank in-principle approval (7 Aug 2025; NOFHC waived Mar 2026)**. **5 RBI-Regulated AIFIs (Basel III Apr 2024):** **EXIM (1982)**, **NABARD (1982, Sivaraman)**, **NHB (1988; GoI 2019; supervises HFCs)**, **SIDBI (1990, Lucknow)**, **NaBFID (2021, ₹20k cr equity + ₹5k cr grant)**.

## 4. Non-Banking Financial Companies and Shadow Banking

- **Legal Basis & `50–50` PBC:** Registered under **`Companies Act`**; regulated by RBI under **Ch. IIIB (`Sec. 45-IA`) of `RBI Act`** (NOT `BR Act`!). Qualifies when **Financial Assets `>50%` total assets AND Financial Income `>50%` gross income**. Min `NOF` raised to **`₹10 cr`** (by Mar 2027; `₹20 cr` HFC, `₹300 cr` IFC).
- **NBFC vs Commercial Bank:** NBFCs **(1) CANNOT accept demand deposits** (only legacy `NBFC-Ds` take fixed time deposits; no new `NBFC-D` since 1997), **(2) NOT in payment system → NO self-drawn cheques**, **(3) ZERO `DICGC` insurance**, **(4) NO `CRR`/`SLR`** (`NBFC-Ds` hold `15%` liquid assets), **(5) `15%` min CRAR**, **(6) NO mandatory PSL**.
- **Types & Regulators:** **`NBFC-ICC`** (merged AFC/LC/IC 2019), **`NBFC-IFC`** (`≥75%` infra), **`IDF-NBFC`**, **`HFC`** (**shifted NHB → RBI in 2019!**), **`NBFC-MFI`** (Mar 2022: income `≤₹3L`, `FOIR ≤50%`), **`CIC`**, **`ARC`** (`SARFAESI`), **`NBFC-P2P`** (Aug 2024 curbs), **`NBFC-AA`** (Account Aggregators — consent DPI data fiduciary, zero lending/storage). Exempt: MFs/Brokers (`SEBI`), Insurers (`IRDAI`), Pensions (`PFRDA`), Nidhis (`MCA`), Chits (`States`).
- **IL&FS (2018, `₹91k+ cr`) & DHFL (2019, 1st FSP under `IBC Sec. 227`) → Scale-Based Regulation (`SBR`, eff. Oct 2022):** Exposed CP-to-infra **ALM crisis** → **4 Layers:** **`NBFC-BL`** (`<₹1,000 cr`, P2P/AA; `92.8%` count, `5.2%` assets; **90-day NPA** completed Mar 2026); **`NBFC-ML`** (`NBFC-D`, `≥₹1,000 cr`, HFC/IFC/CIC/IDF/SPD; `7.0%` count, `64.6%` assets); **`NBFC-UL`** (bank-like: **`CET1 ≥9%`**, **listing within 3 yrs**, 5-yr lock-in; **6 Aug 2026 Revised FY27 List expanded `15 → 17`** via **`₹1 lakh crore` asset threshold**, adding **REC, PFC, IRFC, HUDCO**; Tata Sons pending deregistration); **`NBFC-TL`** (**EMPTY**).
- **Risk Weights & 2026 Health:** Nov 2023 `+25 pp` hike slowed bank credit to NBFCs to `6.7%` → **25 Feb 2025 RBI rolled back `+25 pp` surcharge on SCB loans to NBFCs (eff. 1 Apr 2025)** while keeping unsecured retail curbs. **June 2026 FSR:** NBFC credit **`16.6%`**, **`GNPA 2.4%`**, **`NNPA 0.8%`**, **`CRAR 24.6%`**, **`RoA 2.3%`**; watchpoint = gold loans (`17.4%` of retail book).

## 5. Non-Performing Assets, Twin Balance Sheet Turnaround and Banking Reforms

- **NPA Rules & SMA:** **>90 days overdue** (borrower-wise); **Agri:** **2 crop seasons** (short) / **1 season** (long). **SMA (`CRILC` `≥₹5 cr`):** `SMA-0` (1–30d), `SMA-1` (31–60d), `SMA-2` (61–90d). **NPA Tiers:** **Sub-Standard** (`≤12m`; `15%/25%`), **Doubtful** (`>12m`; `100%` unsec; `D1 25%/D2 40%/D3 100%` sec), **Loss** (`100%`). **`NNPA = GNPA − Provisions`**; **`PCR = Provisions/GNPA`**. **Technical Write-Off ≠ Waiver** (legal recovery continues!).
- **TBS (`11.2%` GNPA Mar 2018; `14.6%` PSBs) → `4R` Turnaround → June 2026 FSR:** **4Rs** = **Recognition (`AQR 2015`, 7 Jun 2019 Framework post-*Dharani Sugars*)** + **Resolution (`IBC/NARCL`)** + **Recapitalisation (`Indradhanush` + `₹3.1L+ cr` Recap Bonds)** + **Reforms (`27→12` PSBs, `FSIB`, `EASE 1.0–7.0`)**. **Verified Mar 2026 (June 2026 FSR):** **SCB `GNPA 1.8%`** (PSBs `1.9%`, PVBs `1.4%`; Mar 2028 stress `1.9%` base / `4.1%` severe), **`NNPA 0.4%`**, **Slippage `1.2%`**, **`PCR 75.6%`**, **`CRAR 17.5%–17.7%`** (`13.0%` severe); Large borrowers `1.2%`, **Agri highest `5.1%` (`37.2%` of SCB GNPAs)**.
- **Resolution Pillars:** **DRTs (1993, `≥₹20L`)**; **`SARFAESI 2002`** (60-day out-of-court seizure, ARCs, `CERSAI`; **ONLY secured loans; EXCLUDES agri land & `≤₹1L` / `<20%` unpaid**); **`IBC 2016` (`Viswanathan BLRC`):** Creditor-in-Control; `IBBI`, `NCLT/DRT`, `IPs`, `NeSL`; `₹1 cr` default; `CoC` (**Financial Creditors ONLY, `66%` vote**); **`Sec. 29A`** promoter bar; `330-day` cap (directory); **`Sec. 53` waterfall** (Workmen + Secured > Govt taxes!); **`PPIRP` (2021)** MSMEs (`₹10L`, 120d). ***Swiss Ribbons* (2019)** upheld IBC & `Sec. 29A`; ***Essar Steel* (2019)** upheld **CoC commercial wisdom**. **IBBI (Sep 2026):** **`₹4.46L cr`** realised (**`30.19%` claims**, **`167.35%` liq. value**, **`95.44%` fair value**; `29.6%` settled/withdrawn), avg delay **`890d/1,005d`**. **`IBC (Amend) Act, 2026` (Assent 6 Apr 2026, Baijayant Panda Panel):** Adds **`CIIRP`** (out-of-court FI fast-track), **14-day admission**, **Group & Cross-Border Insolvency**, guarantors' assets, **`₹1L–₹2 cr` frivolous-filing fines**. **`NARCL–IDRCL`:** `>₹500 cr` NPAs; `NARCL` (`≥51%` PSB, Canara sponsor) **`15%` cash + `85%` SRs** (`₹30,600 cr` GoI guarantee); `IDRCL` (`51%` Pvt); **Apr 2026:** 33 entities (`₹1.65L cr`), **`₹6,345 cr` recovered** (`₹4,364 cr` FY26).
- **Basel III, D-SIBs, PCA, DICGC & 2025–2026 Reforms:** **India Basel III (`+1%` stricter):** `5.5%` CET1 + `1.5%` AT1 (`7%` T1) + `2%` T2 = **`9%` CRAR + `2.5%` CCB = `11.5%`** (vs `10.5%` global); Leverage **`3.5%` (`4.0%` D-SIBs)**; **`LCR ≥100%`** (30d) & **`NSFR ≥100%`** (1-yr). **D-SIBs (Dec 2025):** **SBI `+0.80%` (B4)**, **HDFC `+0.40%` (B2)**, **ICICI `+0.20%` (B1)**. **PCA (Jan 2022):** **Capital, `NNPA` (`6/9/12%`), Leverage** (RoA dropped; NBFC PCA Oct 2022). **`DICGC`:** **`₹5L`/depositor/bank** (principal+interest), guaranteed within **`90 days`** (2021 Amend), paid by banks; excludes **NBFCs, PACS, inter-bank & Govt deposits**. **Project Finance Directions 2025 (eff. 1 Oct 2025):** DCCO `3/2 yrs`; construction provisioning **`1.00%` (infra/CRE-RH) / `1.25%` (CRE)** → `0.40%/0.75%/1.00%` operational. **Final ECL Directions 2026 (27 Apr 2026, eff. 1 Apr 2027):** Forward-looking **3-Stage ECL** (Stage 1 12m, Stage 2 Lifetime `>30 DPD` SICR, Stage 3 NPA) + **4-yr CET1 glide-path (`2027–31`)**. **Governance:** **P.J. Nayak (2014)** → `FSIB` (2022), `EASE`, pending `BIC`, `ULI` & `MuleHunter.AI`.
