## 1. Evolution of India's Industrial Policies (1948–2026) and Industrial Output Measurement

Industrial policy is the deliberate, strategic architecture through which a state shapes the pace, composition, spatial distribution, and ownership structure of industrial development. Unlike narrow sectoral regulation, industrial policy answers fundamental political-economy questions: **which industries should the state own and operate**, **how far should private and foreign capital be permitted to enter**, **how should competition be balanced against concentration of economic power**, and **what institutional tools—tariffs, licences, subsidies, corridors, or performance-linked incentives—best propel structural transformation**. For a newly independent postcolonial nation in 1947 inheriting a shattered artisanal base, minimal capital-goods capacity, weak domestic savings, and an overwhelmingly agrarian workforce, industrial policy was not an abstract technical document; it was the primary instrument of nation-building and economic sovereignty.

Across nearly eight decades since Independence, India's industrial journey has traced a remarkable arc across three distinct epochs. The first phase (**1948–1990**) constructed a state-led, import-substituting mixed economy anchored by public sector control of the "commanding heights", comprehensive industrial licensing, small-scale reservation, and strict curbs on monopolies and foreign equity. The second phase (**1991–2013**) dismantled the **Licence–Permit–Quota Raj** through the historic **New Industrial Policy of 24 July 1991**, unleashing delicensing, trade openness, competition law, and foreign investment. The third and contemporary phase (**2014–2026**) represents a calibrated **"New Industrial Policy" paradigm**—combining market openness with targeted, outcome-based state facilitation via **Make in India**, **Production-Linked Incentive (`PLI`) schemes**, greenfield industrial corridors, decriminalisation (`Jan Vishwas`), MSME scaling reforms, and the **National Manufacturing Mission** announced in Union Budget 2025–26.

For the UPSC Civil Services Examination, this chapter bridges **GS Paper I** (post-independence industrial policy and mixed-economy consolidation), **GS Paper III** (liberalisation, industrial policies before and after 1991, MSMEs, Make in India, PLI, SEZs, and disinvestment), and **Prelims** (where questions repeatedly target IPR 1956 Schedules, compulsory licensing industries, exact weights of the **Index of Core Industries (`ICI`)** and **Index of Industrial Production (`IIP`)**, revised MSME thresholds, Maharatna criteria, and disinvestment vs asset monetisation distinctions).

### The Formative Post-Independence Architecture (1948–1956)

When India attained independence on 15 August 1947, its industrial structure was strikingly lopsided. Modern manufacturing accounted for barely **7–8 per cent of national income** and employed less than **2 per cent of the labour force**, concentrated overwhelmingly in agro-processing consumer industries such as cotton textiles (Bombay and Ahmedabad), jute (Calcutta), and sugar, alongside nascent iron and steel capacity (principally TISCO at Jamshedpur). Heavy machinery, electrical equipment, machine tools, petrochemicals, fertilisers, and defence production were virtually absent. Domestic private capital, represented by Indian business houses who had authored the **Bombay Plan (1944)**, openly acknowledged that the private sector lacked both the financial scale and the risk appetite to undertake long-gestation capital-goods and infrastructure investments on its own.

#### Industrial Policy Resolution of 1948: Birth of India's Mixed Economy

Announced on **6 April 1948** by Industry Minister **Dr. Syama Prasad Mookerjee**, the **Industrial Policy Resolution (`IPR`) 1948** laid the institutional foundation of India's **mixed economy**—rejecting both *laissez-faire* capitalism and total Soviet-style nationalisation. While recognising that the state must play a progressively active role in establishing new industries, it assured existing private enterprises that they would not be arbitrarily nationalised for at least **ten years**, after which their performance would be reviewed.

The hallmark of IPR 1948 was its **four-fold classification of industries**, dividing industrial space between the public and private sectors:

1. **Category I — Exclusive State Monopoly (3 Strategic Industries):** Manufacture of **arms and ammunition**, production and control of **atomic energy**, and ownership and management of **railway transport**. In national emergencies, the Government retained the power to acquire any industry vital for national defence.
2. **Category II — Public Sector Lead in New Undertakings (6 Basic/Key Industries):** **Coal**, **iron and steel**, **aircraft manufacture**, **shipbuilding**, **manufacture of telephone, telegraph and wireless apparatus** (excluding radio receiving sets), and **mineral oils**. Existing private enterprises in these six fields were permitted to continue and expand for ten years, but all *new* undertakings were to be established solely by the State (except where the State sought private cooperation in the national interest).
3. **Category III — Regulated Private Sector (18 Industries of National Importance):** Eighteen essential industries—including automobiles, tractors, heavy chemicals, machine tools, cotton and woollen textiles, cement, sugar, paper, salt, fertilisers, and air/sea transport—remained open to private enterprise, but subject to **central regulation and control** in consultation with state governments because their location, scale, or strategic character demanded national coordination.
4. **Category IV — Unregulated Residual Private & Cooperative Sector:** All remaining industries were left open to private entrepreneurs, individuals, and cooperatives, though the State reserved the right to intervene if private performance proved unsatisfactory.

Beyond this four-fold division, IPR 1948 underscored the vitality of **cottage and small-scale industries** for rural employment, regional dispersal, and decentralised production, and welcomed **foreign capital** cautiously—recognising its utility for bringing advanced technology and scarce foreign exchange, provided major ownership and effective control ordinarily remained in Indian hands.

#### Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951 (`IDRA 1951`)

An industrial policy resolution is merely a statement of executive intent; to acquire legally enforceable teeth, Parliament enacted the **Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951** (which came into force on **8 May 1952**). `IDRA 1951` became the **statutory backbone of India's industrial licensing regime**—popularly dubbed the **Licence Raj**—for the next forty years, and remarkably, it remains the parent statute under which India's residual compulsory licensing operates even in 2026!

Under `IDRA 1951`, industries listed in its **First Schedule** (termed **Scheduled Industries**) were brought under central legislative control via Entry 52 of the Union List. Its core regulatory instruments were sweeping:

- **Registration and Compulsory Licensing:** Every existing industrial undertaking in a Scheduled Industry had to register with the Central Government, while **establishing a new unit**, **producing a "new article"**, **effecting "substantial expansion"** of existing capacity, or **changing factory location** required a prior **Industrial Licence** from the Centre.
- **Power of Investigation and Takeover:** The Central Government was empowered to order investigations into any scheduled industry witnessing an unjustifiable fall in production, deterioration in quality, or price surge, and even to **take over management control** of mismanaged undertakings.
- **Price, Supply and Distribution Control (`Section 18G`):** Empowered the Centre to regulate the supply, distribution, and maximum prices of articles produced by scheduled industries.
- **Advisory Architecture:** Created the **Central Advisory Council (`CAC`)** (comprising industry, labour, consumer, and primary-producer representatives) and sector-specific **Development Councils** to guide efficiency, standardisation, and capacity planning.

While `IDRA 1951` was conceived with noble developmental goals—channelising scarce capital and foreign exchange into priority Plan sectors, curbing luxury consumption, preventing regional concentration in Bombay–Calcutta–Madras, and protecting small producers—in practice it degenerated into a labyrinth of discretionary bureaucratic clearances that rewarded political lobbying and pre-emptive licence-hoarding by large business houses rather than entrepreneurial efficiency.

#### Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956: The "Economic Constitution of India"

Eight years after 1948, India's constitutional and political landscape had transformed fundamentally. The Constitution of India (1950) had enshrined the **Directive Principles of State Policy (`Articles 38` and `39(b)–(c)`)**, mandating the State to minimise inequalities and prevent concentration of wealth and means of production to the common detriment. In December 1954, Parliament formally adopted the **"Socialistic Pattern of Society"** (subsequently endorsed at the Congress's **Avadi Session, January 1955**) as the guiding objective of socio-economic policy. Simultaneously, the **Second Five-Year Plan (`1956–61`)**, designed around **Prof. P.C. Mahalanobis's** two-sector growth model, prioritised rapid expansion of heavy and basic capital-goods industries (steel, heavy electricals, heavy machinery, mining) to achieve long-run self-reliance.

To align industrial governance with this ambitious vision, the Government adopted the **Industrial Policy Resolution (`IPR`) of 30 April 1956**—celebrated across Indian economic literature as the **"Economic Constitution of India"** (and sometimes called the *Bible of State Capitalism* in India). Unlike IPR 1948's four tiers, IPR 1956 introduced a sharper **three-schedule classification** that governed Indian industry for thirty-five years:

Alongside this three-schedule division, IPR 1956 articulated five enduring policy pillars: (i) **State-supported cottage, village, and small-scale industries** via differential taxation, direct subsidies, and industrial estates; (ii) **reduction of regional disparities** by steering public sector steel plants (Bhilai, Rourkela, Durgapur) and infrastructure toward backward states; (iii) **fair treatment and non-discrimination** between public and private units operating within the same industry; (iv) **industrial peace and workers' participation in management**; and (v) **technical and managerial training** to supply skilled human capital for rapid industrialisation.

### Tightening of State Controls and Early Incremental Shifts (1969–1990)

By the late 1960s, official committees discovered a striking paradox: the very licensing machinery designed under `IDRA 1951` and IPR 1956 to prevent monopoly had *accentuated* industrial concentration! The **Mahalanobis Committee on Distribution of Income and Levels of Living (1960–64)**, the **Monopolies Inquiry Commission (1964–65, chaired by Justice K.C. Dasgupta)**, **Prof. R.K. Hazari's Report on Industrial Planning and Licensing Policy (1967)**, and finally the landmark **Industrial Licensing Policy Inquiry Committee (`ILPIC`) (1969, chaired by Subimal Dutt — the Dutt Committee)** documented how large business conglomerates systematically submitted multiple applications to **pre-empt and foreclose industrial capacity**, securing licences they never implemented purely to block smaller competitors from entering the market.

#### The Dutt Committee (1969), MRTP Act (1969) and FERA (1973)

Acting on the Dutt Committee and Monopolies Inquiry Commission reports, the Indira Gandhi government erected the most stringent phase of command-and-control industrial regulation between 1969 and 1973:

- **Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices (`MRTP`) Act, 1969 (enforced June 1970):** Aimed to prevent concentration of economic power to the common detriment, control monopolies, and prohibit monopolistic, restrictive, and unfair trade practices. It established the **MRTP Commission** and defined **"MRTP Companies"** using an asset threshold—originally **₹20 crore** (raised to **₹100 crore** in 1985)—or a **"dominant undertaking"** controlling **one-third or more** (later revised to **one-fourth**) of market share. Any MRTP company seeking to expand capacity, establish a new undertaking, merge, amalgamate, or appoint directors had to obtain prior Central Government approval—effectively penalising domestic firms simply for achieving economies of scale!
- **Industrial Licensing Policy of 1970 & Joint Sector:** Accepting the Dutt Committee's recommendation, the 1970 Policy created a **"Core Sector"** (capital-intensive basic industries) and a **"Heavy Investment Sector"** (projects above ₹5 crore) to which large industrial houses were largely confined, while reserving the **"Middle Sector"** (₹1–5 crore) for medium entrepreneurs and expanding reservation for small-scale industries. It also formalised the **"Joint Sector"** concept—where public financial institutions held at least 26% equity alongside private promoters (25%) and the public, combining state oversight with private managerial efficiency.
- **Industrial Policy Statement of 1973:** Consolidated the list of 19 high-priority **"Core Industries"** open to large business houses and foreign companies, and introduced the concept of **Large Industrial Houses** aligned with MRTP thresholds.
- **Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (`FERA`), 1973 (enforced 1 January 1974):** Enacted amid acute foreign-exchange scarcity following the 1973 OPEC oil shock, FERA strictly conserved forex by capping foreign equity participation in Indian companies at **40 per cent** (with narrow exceptions up to 74% only for high-technology, core-sector, or 100% export-oriented units). Multinationals refusing to dilute equity to 40%—most famously **IBM** and **Coca-Cola** in 1977—exited India altogether. Violations of FERA were treated as **criminal offences** carrying imprisonment, with the burden of proof often inverted against the accused.

#### The 1977 Janata Policy and the 1980–1985 Hesitant Liberalisation

The political shift after the Emergency triggered an ideological pivot toward Gandhian decentralisation before returning to cautious modernisation in the 1980s:

- **Industrial Policy Statement of 1977 (Janata Government — George Fernandes):** Sharply criticised the Nehru–Mahalanobis focus on capital-intensive heavy industry for failing to solve rural unemployment and poverty. Its signature maxim was stark: *"whatever can be produced by small and cottage industries must only be so produced."* Consequently, it:
  1. Expanded the list of items **exclusively reserved for the Small-Scale Sector** dramatically from **180 to 504 items** (later reaching **807 items** by the early 1980s!).
  2. Carved out a brand-new **"Tiny Sector"** within small-scale industries (units with investment in plant and machinery up to **₹1 lakh**, located in towns/villages with population below 50,000).
  3. Launched **District Industries Centres (`DICs`)** in every district as a single-window focal agency providing raw materials, credit linkages, marketing, and clearances under one roof for small and rural entrepreneurs.
  4. Strictly enforced FERA's 40% cap and restricted large industrial houses from entering consumer goods or setting up new units inside metropolitan limits.
- **Industrial Policy Statement of 1980 (Indira Gandhi Government):** Shifted emphasis back toward **"economic federalism"**, productivity, optimum utilisation of installed capacity, and technological modernisation. It introduced the concept of **"Nucleus Plants"** in industrially backward districts to foster ancillary small units around a mother plant, regularised excess capacity installed in 34 key industries, allowed **automatic capacity expansion of 5 per cent per annum** (up to 25% over five years) in core industries, and raised small-scale investment ceilings (Tiny to ₹2 lakh; SSI to ₹20 lakh; Ancillaries to ₹25 lakh).
- **1985–86 Rajiv Gandhi Reforms ("Broad-Banding" and Partial Delicensing):** Recognising that rigid licensing was crippling industrial productivity, the mid-1980s introduced **"broad-banding"** (permitting licensed firms to adjust their product mix across related goods—such as switching between two-wheelers, cars, and commercial vehicles—within overall capacity), delicensed 25 broad industry groups for non-MRTP/non-FERA firms, and raised the **MRTP asset threshold five-fold from ₹20 crore to ₹100 crore**. Yet these incremental half-measures left the core edifice of licensing, import barriers, and PSU monopolies intact—setting the stage for the decisive 1991 rupture.

### The Watershed: New Industrial Policy of 24 July 1991

By June 1991, India stood on the brink of sovereign default. Foreign exchange reserves had plunged to barely **$1.1 billion** (less than two weeks of essential imports), fiscal deficits had surged above **8 per cent of GDP**, inflation exceeded **13–16 per cent**, and industrial growth stagnated under high-cost, technologically obsolete import substitution. Seizing the crisis as a historic window for structural reform, Prime Minister **P.V. Narasimha Rao** (who also held the Industry portfolio) and Finance Minister **Dr. Manmohan Singh** tabled the **Statement on Industrial Policy in Parliament on 24 July 1991**—the very day of the landmark 1991 Union Budget.

> "No power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come. I suggest to this august House that the emergence of India as a major economic power in the world happens to be one such idea." — Dr. Manmohan Singh (quoting Victor Hugo), Union Budget Speech, 24 July 1991

The **New Industrial Policy (`NIP`) 1991** dismantled the four-decade-old **Licence–Permit–Quota Raj** and pivoted Indian industry toward **Liberalisation, Privatisation, and Globalisation (`LPG`)**. Its reforms rested on **five interlocking pillars**:

1. **Abolition of Industrial Licensing (Delicensing):** Compulsory industrial licensing was abolished for **all industrial projects** irrespective of investment size, *except* for a short negative list of **18 industries** related to security, strategic concerns, social hazards, environmental safety, and elitist consumption. For delicensed industries, entrepreneurs merely had to file a simple informational **Industrial Entrepreneur Memorandum (`IEM`)** with the Secretariat for Industrial Assistance (now DPIIT).
   - Over subsequent decades, even this list of 18 was pruned relentlessly (de-licensing cars, white goods, sugar, coal, petroleum refining, bulk drugs, leather, paper, etc.).
   - **Current Status (2025–2026):** As per DPIIT's official Industrial Policy notifications (amending Notification `S.O. 477(E)` dated 25 July 1991), compulsory central industrial licensing under `IDRA 1951` applies to **only 4 active industries** (often still cited as **5 industries** in older textbooks when counting potable alcohol, which ceased to be centrally licensed after the Supreme Court's 1997 *Bihar Distillery* verdict!):
     1. **Cigars and cigarettes of tobacco and manufactured tobacco substitutes** *(note: fresh licences are not being issued on public-health grounds since 1999)*.
     2. **Electronic aerospace and defence equipment: all types** *(subsequently liberalised for components/accessories under Press Note 1 of 2019 Series)*.
     3. **Industrial explosives** including detonating fuses, safety fuses, gunpowder, nitrocellulose, and matches.
     4. **Specified hazardous chemicals:** *(a) Hydrocyanic acid and its derivatives; (b) Phosgene and its derivatives; and (c) Isocyanates and diisocyanates of hydrocarbons not elsewhere specified (such as Methyl Isocyanate — a direct legislative lesson of the 1984 Bhopal Gas Tragedy)*.
     *(Note on the 5th historical item: Distillation and brewing of alcoholic drinks remains in Schedule II textually, but DPIIT ceased issuing central licences for potable alcohol following the Supreme Court's order of 29 January 1997 placing potable alcohol under State jurisdiction!)*
   - Simultaneously, **location restrictions** were scrapped for cities with populations below 1 million (except polluting industries), and **small-scale reservation** was progressively phased out—with the final **20 remaining reserved items** completely **de-reserved on 10 April 2015** (`Notification No. 998(E)`). Today, **zero items** are reserved exclusively for manufacture in the small-scale sector!

2. **Contraction of Public Sector Monopoly (De-reservation):** The 17 industries reserved exclusively for the public sector under Schedule A of IPR 1956 were slashed immediately to **8 core strategic areas** in July 1991 (arms & ammunition/defence equipment, atomic energy, coal & lignite, mineral oils, mining of iron/manganese/chrome/gypsum/sulphur/gold/diamonds, mining of copper/lead/zinc/tin/molybdenum/wolfram, atomic minerals, and railway transport).
   - Progressive opening in the 1990s and 2000s opened coal mining, mineral oils, non-ferrous metals, and even private defence manufacturing (since 2001, with up to 74% FDI under automatic route since 2020) to private enterprise.
   - **Current Status:** Only **two sectors** remain formally reserved for the public sector: **(i) Atomic Energy** (and specified atomic minerals—though private partnerships in small modular reactors and nuclear supply chains are actively evolving under Budget 2025–26's Nuclear Energy Mission), and **(ii) Railway Operations** (even here, private participation is permitted in dedicated freight lines, port/mine rail connectivity, private freight terminals, container trains, rolling stock manufacturing, and station redevelopment).
   - Chronically sick PSUs were brought within the ambit of the **Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction (`BIFR`)** (established under `SICA 1985`), and a **National Renewal Fund (`NRF`, 1992)** was created as a social safety net for Voluntary Retirement Schemes (`VRS`) and worker retraining.

3. **Abolition of MRTP Asset Limits → Competition Act, 2002:** NIP 1991 recognised that capping corporate size in a globalising world crippled Indian firms' international competitiveness. It **scrapped the pre-entry scrutiny and ₹100 crore asset threshold** of the MRTP Act, shifting regulatory focus away from *size per se* toward *anti-competitive behaviour*. Following the **S.V.S. Raghavan Committee (2000)**, Parliament repealed the MRTP Act and enacted the **Competition Act, 2002** (enforced progressively from 2009), establishing the **Competition Commission of India (`CCI`)** to prohibit **anti-competitive agreements** (cartels, bid-rigging), curb **abuse of dominant position**, and regulate **combinations** (mergers and acquisitions—modernised further by the **Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023**, which added deal-value thresholds above ₹2,000 crore for Big Tech acquisitions).

4. **Liberalisation of Foreign Direct Investment (`FDI`) and Foreign Technology:** Breaking sharply from FERA's hostility toward foreign equity, NIP 1991 introduced **automatic approval for up to 51 per cent foreign equity** in **34 high-priority industries** (Annex III), later expanded to 74% and **100% under the Automatic Route** across most manufacturing and infrastructure sectors today. The **Foreign Investment Promotion Board (`FIPB`)** was created to fast-track non-automatic proposals (later abolished in **May 2017**, decentralising approval to respective administrative ministries via the Foreign Investment Facilitation Portal). In **1999**, the draconian FERA 1973 was repealed and replaced by the **Foreign Exchange Management Act (`FEMA`), 1999** (effective 1 June 2000), transforming forex infractions from criminal offences into **civil compoundable contraventions**. Automatic approval was also granted for foreign technology agreements involving lump-sum fees and royalty payments within prescribed parameters.

5. **Abolition of Phased Manufacturing Programmes (`PMP`) and Convertibility Clause:** Mandatory domestic-content indigenisation schedules (`PMP`) for new projects were scrapped, and public financial institutions were freed from the mandatory "convertibility clause" that had previously allowed them to convert corporate loans into equity and seize board control of private enterprises.

> [!case]
> **Why Textbooks Say "5" Compulsory-Licensing Industries While DPIIT Says "4": *Bihar Distillery v. Union of India* (1997)**
> - **Legal Issue:** Schedule II of DPIIT's 1991 licensing notification listed **five** residual industries, beginning with *"Distillation and brewing of alcoholic drinks."* However, under the Seventh Schedule of the Constitution, **intoxicating liquors** fall under **State List (Entry 8)**, whereas industrial alcohol (non-potable) falls within Union industrial regulation (`Entry 52`, Union List).
> - **Supreme Court Holding (29 January 1997):** Building upon the 7-Judge Constitution Bench precedent in *Synthetics and Chemicals Ltd. v. State of U.P.* (1990), the Supreme Court held that industries manufacturing **potable alcohol** lie exclusively within the legislative and licensing jurisdiction of **State Governments**, not the Union under `IDRA 1951`. *(Later, in October 2024, a 9-Judge Constitution Bench in State of U.P. v. Lalta Prasad Vaish overruled Synthetics & Chemicals to affirm States' concurrent authority even over industrial alcohol as "intoxicating liquor" under Entry 8!)*
> - **Prelims Takeaway:** Following the 1997 ruling, DPIIT ceased issuing central licences for potable alcohol. Consequently, **DPIIT's official policy portal and Annual Report (2024–25 / 2025–26) list exactly FOUR active compulsory-licensing industries**: (1) Tobacco cigars/cigarettes, (2) Electronic aerospace & defence equipment, (3) Industrial explosives, and (4) Specified hazardous chemicals!

> [!mains]
> **GS-1 & GS-3 Analytical Framing — Evaluating the Pre-1991 Industrial Legacy Fairly:**
> Avoid painting the 1948–1990 era as an unqualified failure—UPSC examiners reward nuanced historical judgement. The Nehru–Mahalanobis industrial strategy **succeeded** in laying a diversified heavy industrial base (steel, power equipment, refineries, fertilisers, nuclear/space foundations, engineering institutes like IITs) that colonial rule had deliberately suppressed, achieving ~6% industrial growth during 1951–65 and insulating India from commodity dependence. Its **failure** lay in persisting with rigid licensing, import quotas, and small-scale reservation *long after* their infant-industry utility had expired—producing high-cost, low-productivity domestic monopolies, stifling labour-intensive export manufacturing precisely when East Asia (South Korea, Taiwan, China) captured global markets, and triggering the 1991 Balance of Payments crisis.

### Measuring Industrial Output: Index of Industrial Production (IIP) and Index of Core Industries (ICI)

To evaluate whether industrial policy actually translates into factory-floor output, economists and policymakers rely on two monthly, high-frequency **volume (quantum) indices** that are perennial favourites in UPSC Prelims: the **Index of Industrial Production (`IIP`)** and the **Index of Core Industries (`ICI`)**. Crucially, **both indices underwent a historic base year revision from `2011–12` to `2022–23` in June–July 2026** to align seamlessly with MoSPI's new `2022–23` GDP and Producer Price Index (`PPI`) series!

#### Index of Industrial Production (`IIP`): Concept, Agency and the Historic June 2026 Revision

Compiled and published **monthly** (with a six-week lag) by the **National Statistical Office (`NSO`)** under the **Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (`MoSPI`)**, the **IIP** is a **composite physical volume indicator** measuring short-term changes in the volume of production of a representative basket of industrial products during a given period relative to a chosen base year (set equal to `100`). Because it tracks physical output quantities (or value-based output deflated into real volumes) across factories and utilities, IIP is a **fixed-weight Laspeyres index**—and a critical leading input for quarterly National Accounts estimates.

For nearly a decade (May 2017 to May 2026), India tracked IIP using **Base Year `2011–12`** across **three broad sectors** (`Mining` 14.37%, `Manufacturing` 77.63%, and `Electricity` 7.99%) covering `407` item groups. Based on the recommendations of the **Technical Advisory Committee on Base Year Revision of IIP (`TAC-IIP`, report released 25 May 2026)**, MoSPI launched the **new `2022–23` Base Year IIP series on 1 June 2026**—and subsequently upgraded it on **29 June 2026** by replacing the Wholesale Price Index (`WPI`) with DPIIT's brand-new **Output Producer Price Index (`Output PPI`, base `2022–23`, launched 15 June 2026)** as the deflator for the `234` value-reported item groups (accounting for `36.02%` of total index weight)!

Four structural transformations define the **`2022–23` IIP Series** (prime UPSC Prelims territory):

1. **Expansion from 3 Sectors to 4 Sectors:** Aligned with the **National Industrial Classification (`NIC-2025`)**, the new IIP introduces an entire **fourth broad sector**—**Water Supply, Sewerage and Waste Management (`2.02%` weight)**—while expanding Electricity to **Electricity and Gas Supply (`10.87%`)** and broadening Mining to include **minor minerals and rare earth minerals**.
2. **Modernised Item Basket:** Expands from `407` item groups (809 items) to **`463` item groups (`1,042` products)**—adding modern manufacturing lines (smartphones, solar modules, EV components, speciality chemicals, medical devices) while dropping obsolete items, and introducing a dynamic **factory-substitution mechanism** to prevent sample decay.
3. **Shift from WPI to Output PPI Deflator:** Eliminates wholesale trade-margin distortions when deflating value-reported manufacturing output.
4. **Dual Classification (Sectoral and Use-Based):** Like the old series, IIP reports output both by **Broad Sector** (4 sectors) and by **Six Use-Based Categories** (`Primary Goods`, `Capital Goods`, `Intermediate Goods`, `Infrastructure/Construction Goods`, `Consumer Durables`, and `Consumer Non-Durables`).

## 2. Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs): Backbone of Employment and Inclusive Growth

If large corporations and capital-intensive conglomerates provide the visible skyline of industrial India, **Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (`MSMEs`)** form its indispensable bedrock—operating across semi-urban clusters, tier-3 manufacturing towns, rural artisan workshops, component supplier networks, and neighbourhood service firms. Globally, MSMEs represent roughly **90 per cent of all businesses** and over **50 per cent of total employment**; in a labour-abundant, regionally diverse economy like India, their macroeconomic and socio-political significance is even deeper.

According to the **Economic Survey 2025–26** (tabled 29 January 2026) and Ministry of MSME / PIB official data, India's MSME ecosystem commands four extraordinary macroeconomic weights:

- **Share in Gross Domestic Product (`GDP`):** Roughly **`31.1 per cent`** of India's total GDP (consistently hovering around `30–31%` over the past decade).
- **Share in Manufacturing Output:** Approximately **`35.4 per cent`** of India's total manufacturing output, supplying vital components, precision castings, textiles, leather, food products, and auto ancillaries into domestic and global supply chains.
- **Share in Total Exports:** Nearly **`48.58 per cent`** of India's overall merchandise and related exports (making MSMEs central to India's trade balance and export competitiveness).
- **Second-Largest Employer After Agriculture:** Spanning over **`7.47 crore` enterprises** (with formal registrations across the **Udyam** and **Udyam Assist** portals crossing **`7.9 crore` by March 2026** and surging toward **`9.78 crore` by October 2026** as informal micro enterprises onboarded digitally), MSMEs sustain livelihoods for **`32.8 crore` to `43+ crore` reported persons**—second only to agriculture in employment absorption, with **women-owned enterprises** representing **`37.6 per cent`** of total formal registrations!

Beyond sheer volume, MSMEs perform three irreplaceable **inclusive-development functions**: (i) **Lower Capital-to-Labour Ratio**—creating far more jobs per crore of capital invested than heavy industry; (ii) **Balanced Regional Development**—industrialising rural, semi-urban, and backward districts (such as Tiruppur's knitwear, Moradabad's brassware, Ludhiana's hosiery/bicycles, Rajkot's castings, Surat's diamonds/textiles, and Sivakasi's printing/fireworks), thereby moderating distress migration into megacities; and (iii) **Ancillarisation and Entrepreneurship Nursery**—supplying tier-2/tier-3 inputs to large OEMs (automobiles, electronics, defence) while lowering entry barriers for first-generation, SC/ST, and women entrepreneurs.

### Legal and Definitional Evolution: From MSMED Act 2006 to Budget 2025–26 Revised Limits

How the State defines an MSME is never a trivial clerical detail: it determines which enterprises qualify for **Priority Sector Lending (`PSL`)**, collateral-free credit guarantees (`CGTMSE`), public procurement quotas (`25%`), delayed-payment legal protection, and tax/compliance benefits. Over two decades, India's MSME definition has undergone **three major evolutionary phases**, systematically correcting perverse incentives that once punished growth.

#### Phase 1: The MSMED Act, 2006 (Separate Investment-Only Criteria)

Prior to 2006, small-scale units were governed piecemeal under `IDRA 1951` notifications. Parliament enacted the comprehensive **Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises Development (`MSMED`) Act, 2006** (effective **2 October 2006**) to provide a unified statutory framework for promoting, developing, and enhancing the competitiveness of MSMEs, establishing the **National Board for MSMEs (`NBMSME`)** and **Micro and Small Enterprises Facilitation Councils (`MSEFCs`)** for delayed-payment adjudication.

However, the **2006 Act** suffered from two acute design flaws:
1. It classified enterprises **solely on historical investment in plant & machinery (for manufacturing) or equipment (for services)**—requiring physical verification of asset invoices that invited inspector harassment and under-reporting of machinery investments.
2. It maintained **separate, much lower thresholds for services** than for manufacturing (e.g., a small manufacturer could invest up to ₹5 crore, whereas a small service enterprise was capped at ₹2 crore), distorting modern hybrid businesses combining manufacturing with design, software, and maintenance.

#### Phase 2: The Atmanirbhar Bharat Overhaul of June 2020 (Composite Criteria & Export Exclusion)

Notified vide `S.O. 2119(E)` on **26 June 2020** (effective **1 July 2020**), the Government implemented a paradigm shift long recommended by the **U.K. Sinha RBI Expert Committee on MSMEs (2019)**:

- **Unified Definition (Manufacturing = Services):** Abolished the artificial distinction between manufacturing and service enterprises.
- **Composite Criteria (Investment AND Annual Turnover):** Introduced **annual turnover** (automatically verifiable via **GSTN** and **Income Tax `PAN`** linkages without physical inspection!) alongside **net investment in plant and machinery/equipment** (written-down value from ITR, excluding land, buildings, and pollution-control/R&D equipment).
- **Crucial "AND / Upper-Limit Trigger" Rule (Classic Prelims Trap!):** To remain in a given category, an enterprise must satisfy **BOTH** limits simultaneously—if it crosses **EITHER** the investment ceiling **OR** the turnover ceiling of its current slab, it automatically graduates to the next higher category! Conversely, to move *downward*, it must fall below *both* ceilings.
- **Exclusion of Exports from Turnover:** In a visionary pro-trade reform, **export turnover of goods or services is COMPLETELY EXCLUDED** while calculating annual turnover for MSME classification! This allows high-performing export-oriented MSMEs to scale global shipments freely without losing domestic MSME credit and procurement support.
- **Inclusion of Retail and Wholesale Trade (July 2021):** Following pandemic distress, retail and wholesale traders were formally brought back under the MSME umbrella (effective 2 July 2021) strictly for the limited benefit of **Priority Sector Lending (`PSL`)** via Udyam registration.

#### Phase 3: Union Budget 2025–26 Revised Limits (Effective 1 April 2025)

Recognising that inflation, capital-intensive automation, and rising global order sizes were pushing growing Indian small firms prematurely past the 2020 thresholds, Finance Minister **Nirmala Sitharaman** announced in the **Union Budget 2025–26 (1 February 2025)**—notified via **Gazette Notification `S.O. 1364(E)` dated 21 March 2025**, **effective 1 April 2025**—a sweeping **2.5× enhancement in investment limits** and **2× enhancement in annual turnover limits** across all three MSME tiers!

### Structural Challenges Confronting Indian MSMEs

Despite contributing nearly a third of GDP and almost half of India's exports, the MSME sector remains overwhelmingly skewed toward tiny, informal, low-productivity micro units—over **95–99 per cent of Indian MSMEs fall in the Micro tier**, often single-person or household survival enterprises with fewer than five workers. Six deeply intertwined structural bottlenecks explain why Indian MSMEs struggle to scale into globally competitive mid-sized "Mittelstand" champions (like Germany's renowned family-owned industrial leaders):

#### 1. The "Missing Middle" and "Dwarfism" (Economic Survey 2018–19 Thesis)

In a landmark analytical chapter (*"Nourishing Dwarfs to Become Giants"*, Chapter 3 of the **Economic Survey 2018–19**), Chief Economic Adviser Krishnamurthy Subramanian diagnosed India's defining industrial pathology: **Dwarfism**. Unlike healthy "infants" (young small firms that grow rapidly into medium and large enterprises within 5–10 years, creating high-wage formal jobs), **"dwarfs"** are **firms employing fewer than 100 workers despite being more than 10 years old**.

The Survey revealed stunning empirical evidence from India's Annual Survey of Industries:
- Dwarfs accounted for **over 50 per cent of all organised manufacturing firms**, yet generated merely **14.1 per cent of employment** and a dismal **7.6 per cent of Net Value Added (`NVA`)**!
- Conversely, large firms (>100 workers), though barely **15 per cent of firms**, generated **over 75 per cent of manufacturing employment** and **nearly 90 per cent of NVA**, exhibiting labour productivity roughly **4–5 times higher** than dwarfs!

**Why Does India Produce Dwarfs Instead of Giants?** The Survey traced dwarfism directly to **perverse threshold-based policy incentives** (sometimes called the **"size-based regulatory cliff"**). Historically, crossing 10, 20, 50, or 100 workers—or crossing old SSI/MSME investment caps—triggered a sudden avalanche of labour-law compliance (such as Chapter V-B of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 requiring prior government permission for retrenchment at ≥100 workers, later raised to 300 in the Industrial Relations Code, 2020), factory inspections, loss of Priority Sector Lending subsidies, loss of tax exemptions, and loss of reserved public procurement! Rational entrepreneurs therefore deliberately **stayed small** or **horizontally fragmented** one factory into five legally separate micro units under relatives' names—sacrificing economies of scale, automation, R&D, and export competitiveness.

> [!debate]
> **Policy Dilemma: Should India Protect Small Size Permanently or Incentivise Young Growth?**
> - **The Protectionist / Welfare Case:** Small enterprises provide vital rural livelihoods, absorb low-skilled labour, empower women and marginalised castes, and cannot survive head-on competition against deep-pocketed conglomerates or predatory e-commerce without preferential credit (`PSL`), procurement quotas (`25%`), and simplified compliance.
> - **The Scale-Neutrality / Economic Survey Case:** Subsidising firms purely for *being small forever* rewards stagnation ("dwarfism"). As the Economic Survey 2018–19 argued, policy must **sunset size-based incentives after 5–7 years of firm age** (focusing support on **young "infants"**, not ageing "dwarfs"), lift labour and classification thresholds (as the **2025 2.5×/2× MSME limit hike** and **3-year post-graduation benefit retention** actively begin to do!), and reward **employment growth and productivity upgrades** instead of perpetual smallness.

#### 2. The Chronic Formal Credit Gap and Collateral Barrier

According to the **U.K. Sinha Committee (2019)**, **IFC / SIDBI estimates**, and parliamentary panels, India's addressable **MSME credit gap** stands at roughly **`₹25–30 lakh crore`** (with total unmet demand even higher when informal micro-enterprises are included). Fewer than one-fifth of India's micro units access formal institutional bank credit; the vast majority depend on retained earnings, family savings, or informal moneylenders charging `24–36%` annual interest. Commercial banks remain risk-averse toward small borrowers due to **information asymmetry** (absence of audited balance sheets and credit histories), **lack of immovable collateral**, high transaction costs of servicing tiny ticket-size loans, and fear of Non-Performing Assets (`NPAs`) during macroeconomic slowdowns.

#### 3. Delayed Payments and Working-Capital Strangulation

Even profitable MSMEs frequently collapse into insolvency not from lack of orders, but from **working-capital starvation** caused by delayed payments from large private corporations, Central/State Public Sector Undertakings, and government departments. Under **`Section 15` of the MSMED Act, 2006**, buyers are legally mandated to pay Micro and Small suppliers within the agreed credit period—which **cannot exceed 45 days** from the date of acceptance (or **15 days** if no written agreement exists)—failing which compound interest at **three times the RBI Bank Rate** applies! In reality, small suppliers routinely wait **90 to 180 days** and hesitate to sue dominant corporate buyers for fear of losing future business.

To break this impasse with fiscal teeth, the **Finance Act, 2023** inserted a landmark provision—**`Section 43B(h)` of the Income-tax Act, 1961** (effective **1 April 2024**, AY 2024–25 onwards). Under `Section 43B(h)`, **any sum payable by a buyer to a Micro or Small enterprise** beyond the MSMED Act's statutory `15/45-day` limit is **disallowed as a tax-deductible business expense** in that financial year and allowed only in the year of *actual payment*! While `Section 43B(h)` dramatically accelerated timely payments to registered MSEs, some large garment and retail buyers initially responded by shifting orders to *unregistered* or *Medium* suppliers (since Medium enterprises are outside `Section 43B(h)`!)—underscoring the delicate balance required in enforcement.

#### 4. Technological Obsolescence, Low Productivity and Informal Compliance Burden

Most Indian micro and small workshops rely on dated machinery, minimal quality certification, weak digital ERP/accounting tools, and negligible R&D expenditure, trapping them in low-margin commodity segments vulnerable to cheaper imports from China, Vietnam, and Bangladesh. Simultaneously, formalisation exposes MSMEs to a formidable **compliance thicket**—monthly/quarterly GST returns, EPFO/ESIC filings, factory/pollution clearances, and municipal trade licences—which disproportionately burdens small firms lacking dedicated legal or accounting departments.

### Comprehensive Policy Ecosystem: Formalisation, Credit, Liquidity and Market Access

Over the past decade—accelerated sharply through Union Budgets **2024–25**, **2025–26**, and **2026–27**—India has constructed a multi-layered digital and financial support architecture addressing every bottleneck above:

#### 1. Digital Formalisation: Udyam Registration Portal and Udyam Assist Platform (`UAP`)

- **Udyam Registration Portal (launched 1 July 2020):** Completely paperless, zero-fee, self-declaration registration integrated seamlessly via APIs with **CBDT (`PAN/ITR`)** and **GSTN**, generating a permanent 19-digit **Udyam Registration Number (`URN`)** and QR-coded certificate.
- **Udyam Assist Platform (`UAP`, launched January 2023):** Developed by **SIDBI** and the Ministry of MSME specifically to bring **Informal Micro Enterprises (`IMEs`)**—street vendors, artisans, and tiny rural shops exempt from GST—into the formal fold via Designated Agencies (banks, NBFCs, MFIs). UAP certificates are treated at par with Udyam certificates for **Priority Sector Lending (`PSL`)** benefits! Together, **Udyam + UAP registrations crossed `7.9 crore` by March 2026 and `9.78 crore` by October 2026**, marking one of the largest formalisation drives in developing-world history.

#### 2. Liquidity & Working Capital: TReDS, SAMADHAAN and `Section 43B(h)`

- **Trade Receivables Discounting System (`TReDS`):** An RBI-regulated electronic invoice-factoring platform (launched 2014; currently operated by **five RBI-licensed entities**, including RXIL, M1xchange, Invoicemart, C2treds, and KredX/DTX) that enables MSMEs to auction approved trade receivables due from large corporate/PSU/government buyers to multiple competing banks and NBFC factors **without recourse** to the MSME—converting 60–90 day bills into instant cash at competitive corporate-grade interest rates!
  - **2024–2026 Expansion:** Following Union Budget 2024–25, the Ministry of MSME issued a **Gazette Notification dated 7 November 2024** slashing the **mandatory TReDS onboarding turnover threshold for corporate buyers and CPSEs from `₹500 crore` to `₹250 crore`**, onboarding ~7,000 additional companies and 22 more CPSEs and unlocking over **`₹7 lakh crore`** in cumulative MSME liquidity! Union Budget **2026–27** further mandated TReDS as the settlement platform for CPSE purchases from MSMEs and introduced credit guarantee backing via CGTMSE for invoice discounting on TReDS.

```flow
# How TReDS Unlocks Instant Working-Capital Liquidity for MSMEs
MSME Delivers Goods / Services | Supplies inputs to a large corporate, CPSE (turnover ≥ ₹250 cr), or Govt buyer & uploads e-invoice on TReDS
Buyer Validates Factoring Unit | Large buyer digitally accepts invoice on RBI-regulated TReDS platform (creating an enforceable payment obligation)
Competitive Financier Bidding | Multiple Banks & NBFC-Factors bid lowest discount rate online (priced on the large buyer's AA/AAA credit rating, not the MSME's!)
Instant Cash to MSME (T+1) | Winning financier credits discounted cash directly to MSME WITHOUT RECOURSE || Settlement on Due Date | On maturity (30–45 days), large buyer pays the financier directly via NACH/RBI settlement
```

- **MSME SAMADHAAN Portal (launched October 2017):** Online delayed-payment monitoring portal allowing Micro and Small enterprises to file applications directly before **161 Micro and Small Enterprises Facilitation Councils (`MSEFCs`)** across States/UTs for statutory conciliation and arbitration (mandated to conclude within 90 days under `Section 18` of MSMED Act 2006).

#### 3. Credit Guarantees, Risk Equity and Micro-Finance Architecture

- **Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises (`CGTMSE`, established 2000 by GoI & SIDBI):** Provides collateral-free and third-party-guarantee-free credit guarantees to Member Lending Institutions (banks, NBFCs, SFBs, RRBs) lending to MSEs. Celebrating its 25th anniversary in 2025, CGTMSE **crossed `1 crore` cumulative guarantees**!
  - **Union Budget 2025–26 Enhancements (effective 1 April 2025):** Enhanced the **collateral-free credit guarantee ceiling for Micro and Small Enterprises from `₹5 crore` to `₹10 crore`** (unlocking `₹1.5 lakh crore` in additional credit over five years), doubled guarantee cover for **Startups from `₹10 crore` to `₹20 crore`**, extended guarantee cover for **well-run exporter MSMEs for term loans up to `₹20 crore`**, and introduced a **special 10% guarantee-fee concession and 85% coverage for transgender-promoted MSEs** (eff. 1 March 2025).
  - **Customised Credit Cards for Micro Enterprises (Budget 2025–26):** Introduced **`₹5 lakh` limit** customised credit cards for Udyam-registered micro enterprises (targeting 10 lakh cards in year one).
  - **Mutual Credit Guarantee Scheme for MSMEs (`MCGS-MSME`, announced Budget 2024–25 / launched 2025):** Separate self-financing guarantee fund offering up to **`₹100 crore`** collateral-free guarantee cover for MSMEs purchasing machinery/equipment in manufacturing.
- **Pradhan Mantri MUDRA Yojana (`PMMY`, launched 8 April 2015):** Implemented via **MUDRA** (a wholly owned subsidiary of **SIDBI** — strictly a *refinancing* institution, NOT a direct retail lender!), extending collateral-free micro-enterprise loans through banks, NBFCs, and MFIs across four tiers:
  - **Shishu:** Up to `₹50,000`
  - **Kishore:** `₹50,001` to `₹5 lakh`
  - **Tarun:** `₹5,00,001` to `₹10 lakh`
  - **Tarun Plus (introduced Union Budget 2024–25, notified Oct 2024):** **`₹10 lakh` to `₹20 lakh`** exclusively for entrepreneurs who have availed and **successfully repaid** previous Tarun-category loans!
- **Equity & Growth Capital (SRI Fund & 2026 SME Growth Fund):** Addressing MSMEs' chronic debt-over-equity vulnerability, the **`₹50,000 crore` Self-Reliant India (`SRI`) Fund** (established 2021 via a Fund-of-Funds structure with `₹10,000 cr` GoI anchor + `₹40,000 cr` private/PE capital) had invested **`₹15,442 crore` across `682` MSMEs** by November 2025. Building on this success, **Union Budget 2026–27** announced a dedicated **`₹10,000 crore` SME Growth Fund** to nurture high-potential "Champion" MSMEs alongside a `₹2,000 crore` top-up for the SRI Fund!
- **PSB In-House Digital Credit Assessment (2024–2026):** Public Sector Banks launched digital cash-flow-based credit underwriting models relying on **GSTN, Account Aggregator, ITR, and digital payments footprints** rather than solely on immovable land collateral.

## 3. Make in India, Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) Schemes and the Manufacturing Ecosystem

Every major economy that has achieved sustained high-income prosperity over the past century—from Britain and Germany in the nineteenth century to Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and China in the twentieth—executed a decisive **Lewisian structural transformation**: pulling surplus, low-productivity rural workers off small farms into higher-productivity, export-oriented **manufacturing** before transitioning into advanced services. India, uniquely among large developing nations, bypassed that canonical middle rung. While India's services sector leapt to roughly **54–55 per cent of Gross Value Added (`GVA`)**, its **manufacturing share of GDP remained stubbornly range-bound at `14–17 per cent`** across three decades—well short of the ambitious **`25 per cent` target** proclaimed first under the **National Manufacturing Policy (`NMP`), 2011** and reiterated under **Make in India (2014)**, and far below East Asian benchmarks (China ~`26–28%`, South Korea ~`25%`, Vietnam ~`24%`).

Understanding *why* Indian manufacturing stalled—and evaluating India's assertive 2014–2026 industrial revival built on **Make in India 2.0**, **Production-Linked Incentive (`PLI`) schemes**, **National Industrial Corridors**, **Special Economic Zones (`SEZs`)**, **PM MITRA textile parks**, the **National Manufacturing Mission (2025)**, and **Jan Vishwas** regulatory reform—constitutes one of the highest-yielding Mains GS Paper III themes in the UPSC Civil Services Examination.

### Why Manufacturing Stalled at 14–17% of GDP: Structural Diagnosis and 2025–2026 Momentum

Economists diagnose India's manufacturing plateau not as a single failure, but as a compounding set of **factor-market, infrastructure, trade-policy, and historical distortions**:

1. **The "Cost Disability" Penalty (Logistics, Power and Finance):** Historically, high multimodal logistics costs (traditionally estimated at `13–14%` of GDP vs `8–9%` in OECD peers, though modern NCAER/DPIIT assessments show steady declines toward ~`8–9%` following expressway, Dedicated Freight Corridor, and GST gains), cross-subsidised **high industrial electricity tariffs** (where factories pay inflated rates to subsidise agricultural and domestic power), and higher real cost of capital imposed an estimated **`8–12 per cent` cost handicap** on Indian factories compared to China or Vietnam.
2. **Rigid Factor Markets (Land and Labour):** Fragmented land titles, protracted land acquisition under state statutes, and legacy labour laws (which historically discouraged scale beyond 100 workers before the four consolidated **Labour Codes, 2019–2020** were enacted) pushed Indian capital disproportionately into **capital-intensive and skill-intensive sectors** (petrochemicals, automobiles, pharmaceuticals, IT) rather than **labour-intensive mass assembly** (apparel, footwear, toys, electronics) where India's demographic comparative advantage naturally lay.
3. **Inverted Duty Structures and Tariff Volatility:** Historically, India's customs tariff architecture frequently levied **higher duties on raw materials and intermediate components** than on finished imports under Free Trade Agreements (`FTAs`)—penalising domestic manufacturers instead of domestic importers.
4. **Premature Deindustrialisation and China's Scale Advantage:** As Harvard economist **Dani Rodrik** famously documented, globalisation and rapid factory automation lowered the peak manufacturing share attainable by late industrialisers compared to earlier decades; simultaneously, China's post-2001 WTO entry captured unmatched scale economies across global supply chains just as India struggled with infrastructure deficits.
5. **Low R&D Intensity and Shallow Ancillary Depth:** India's gross expenditure on R&D (`GERD`) remains stuck around **`0.64–0.65 per cent` of GDP** (with private corporate R&D contributing barely one-third, compared to `70%+` in South Korea, the US, or China), constraining transition from contract assembly to indigenous intellectual property and capital-goods manufacturing.

**Yet the Structural Tide Is Beginning to Turn (Economic Survey 2025–26):** Tabled in Parliament on **29 January 2026**, the **Economic Survey 2025–26** documented clear structural recovery despite turbulent global trade headwinds:
- **Industrial & Manufacturing Acceleration:** Real Industry GVA accelerated to **`7.0 per cent` YoY in H1 FY26** (up from `5.9%` in FY25), powered by a sharp surge in **Real Manufacturing GVA growth of `7.72 per cent` in Q1 FY26 and `9.13 per cent` in Q2 FY26**!
- **Technological Upgrading:** **Medium and high-technology activities now account for `46.3 per cent` of India's total manufacturing value added**, placing India among an upper tier of developing industrialisers.
- **Global Competitiveness & Innovation Gains:** India's rank on UNIDO's **Competitive Industrial Performance (`CIP`) Index** improved from `40th` to **`37th`**, while its **Global Innovation Index (`GII`)** rank surged from `66th` (2019) to **`38th` (2025)**.
- **Semiconductor Breakthrough:** Under the **India Semiconductor Mission (`ISM`)** (`₹76,000 crore` Semicon India outlay), **`10` semiconductor fabrication, ATMP (assembly, testing, marking, packaging), and OSAT projects** across `6` States involving **`~₹1.60 lakh crore`** in investment had received approval by early 2026—signalling India's entry into the strategic frontier of high-tech silicon manufacturing!

### Make in India (2014), Make in India 2.0 and the National Manufacturing Mission (2025)

#### National Manufacturing Policy (2011) vs Make in India (2014 & 2.0)

India's first formal standalone manufacturing blueprint was the **National Manufacturing Policy (`NMP`), November 2011**, which aimed to raise manufacturing's share of GDP to **`25 per cent`** and create **`100 million` additional jobs** within a decade, primarily through **National Investment and Manufacturing Zones (`NIMZs`)** (greenfield townships of at least `5,000` hectares). However, land acquisition hurdles and delayed infrastructure execution stalled most NIMZs on paper.

Building a far broader investment-facilitation campaign, the Government launched **Make in India** on **25 September 2014**, coordinated by **DPIIT** alongside **Invest India** (the national investment promotion agency). Designed around **four strategic pillars**—**(i) New Processes** (Ease of Doing Business & delicensing), **(ii) New Infrastructure** (industrial corridors, smart cities, logistics), **(iii) New Sectors** (opening FDI caps in defence, railways, insurance, medical devices, space), and **(iv) New Mindset** (shifting the State from *regulator* to *facilitator*)—Make in India initially targeted **25 priority sectors**.

In **2020**, recognising that modern manufacturing is inseparable from high-value services ("servicification of manufacturing"), the Government launched **Make in India 2.0**, sharpening execution across **`27` focus sectors**:
- **`15` Manufacturing Sectors** (coordinated by DPIIT): Aerospace & defence, automotive & auto components, pharmaceuticals & medical devices, biotechnology, capital goods, telecom & networking, electronics system design & manufacturing (`ESDM`), textiles & apparel, chemicals, food processing, leather & footwear, metals & mining, gems & jewellery, railways, and renewable energy.
- **`12` Champion Service Sectors** (coordinated by Department of Commerce): IT & ITeS, tourism & hospitality, medical value travel, transport & logistics, accounting & finance, audio-visual, legal, communication, construction, environmental, financial, and education services.

#### The National Manufacturing Mission (Union Budget 2025–26)

To unify fragmented central schemes and state-level industrial policies into a coherent Viksit Bharat @2047 roadmap, Union Budget **2025–26** (1 February 2025) announced the **National Manufacturing Mission (`NMM`)** covering **small, medium, and large industries** simultaneously. Functioning as an apex policy-coordination, execution-roadmap, and governance-monitoring framework across Union Ministries and State Governments, the Mission anchors itself on **five core focal areas** plus a dedicated **Clean Tech Manufacturing** pillar:

1. **Ease and Cost of Doing Business:** Cutting state and municipal compliance bottlenecks, land/utility costs, and logistics friction.
2. **Future-Ready Workforce for In-Demand Jobs:** Aligning industrial skill training (`5` National Centres of Excellence for Skilling) with Industry 4.0 robotics, semiconductors, EVs, and precision engineering.
3. **A Vibrant and Dynamic MSME Sector:** Integrating scaled MSMEs (aided by the April 2025 `2.5×/2×` threshold hike) directly as Tier-1/Tier-2 suppliers to anchor OEMs.
4. **Availability of Technology:** Expanding domestic R&D linkages, testing laboratories, and tech-transfer partnerships.
5. **Quality Products:** Embedding global quality certifications (`BIS`, `ZED` — Zero Defect Zero Effect) to build a globally trusted *"Made in India"* brand.
6. **Dedicated Clean Tech Manufacturing Ecosystem:** Explicitly building domestic value addition across **solar PV cells, EV batteries, motors and controllers, green hydrogen electrolysers, wind turbines, very-high-voltage transmission equipment, and grid-scale batteries**—positioning India as a competitive alternative to China in the global green transition!

Complementing the Mission, Budget 2025–26 also targeted **labour-intensive employment engines**—launching a **Focus Product Scheme for Footwear and Leather Sectors** (projected to facilitate **`22 lakh` jobs**, `₹4 lakh crore` turnover, and `>₹1.1 lakh crore` exports) alongside a **National Action Plan for Toys** to transform India into a global hub for high-quality, innovative toys.

### Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) Schemes Across 14 Strategic Sectors

The undisputed centrepiece of India's contemporary industrial policy—signalling the global return of targeted **"New Industrial Policy"** alongside the US CHIPS Act and EU Green Deal Industrial Plan—is the **Production-Linked Incentive (`PLI`) Scheme**. Piloted first in **March–April 2020** for three critical sectors (Large Scale Electronics Manufacturing, Bulk Drugs/APIs, and Medical Devices) to exploit the post-COVID **"China+1"** supply-chain diversification wave, the scheme was expanded in November 2020 to **14 strategic sectors** with an initial headline outlay of **`₹1.97 lakh crore`** (subsequently consolidated/approved at **`₹1.91 lakh crore` — `₹1,91,049 crore`** across line ministries, with **DPIIT** serving as nodal coordinating department).

#### The Conceptual Innovation: Outcome-Based Incremental-Sales Incentives vs Legacy Subsidies

What makes PLI structurally distinct from India's failed pre-1991 subsidies or generic tax holidays? Traditional industrial subsidies were **input-based and upfront** (cheap land, capital investment grants, power rebates)—creating moral hazard where politically connected firms pocketed subsidies and built underutilised "ghost factories" regardless of actual production or global competitiveness.

PLI inverts this logic completely through an **outcome-based, post-facto performance contract**:
- **Zero Upfront Handouts:** Companies receive **not a single rupee** until they *first* invest their own risk capital, install verified plant capacity, and actually manufacture and sell goods!
- **Incremental Sales Reward:** Eligible firms receive a cash incentive typically equal to **`4 per cent to 6 per cent`** (varying by sector/product technology tier) strictly on **incremental net sales** of goods manufactured in India over a stipulated base year, normally for a **five-year tenure** (tapering downward over time to force self-sufficiency).
- **Scale & Global Champions Focus:** Threshold investment and sales floors deliberately favour anchor global and domestic firms capable of achieving world-class economies of scale and pulling an entire domestic supplier ecosystem behind them.
- **WTO-Conscious Design:** Crucially, unlike banned export subsidies (such as India's old MEIS/EPCG schemes challenged at the WTO), **PLI payouts are linked to domestic production and total incremental sales—NOT contingent upon export performance**—designed specifically to stay compliant with the **WTO Agreement on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures (`ASCM`)**!

```flow
# How the PLI Outcome-Based Incremental-Sales Incentive Chain Works
1. Competitive Selection | Global & domestic firms apply under sectoral PLI windows, committing minimum incremental Capex & Sales milestones
2. Upfront Private Capex | Selected firms invest private capital, build greenfield/brownfield lines & onboard domestic Tier-2/3 component suppliers (NO upfront State cash!)
3. Verified Incremental Production | Factories manufacture & sell goods domestically and globally; Project Management Agencies (PMAs) audit actual incremental sales above base year
4. Performance Payout (4–6% for 5 Yrs) | Administrative Ministry disburses cash incentive ONLY on verified threshold achievement || 5. Ecosystem Spillover | Anchor factories attract global sub-suppliers, cut import dependence, deepen local value addition & generate formal jobs
```

> [!example]
> **Case Study — India's Smartphone & Apple Ecosystem Turnaround Under LSEM PLI:**
> In `2014–15`, India imported **nearly 78 per cent** of its mobile phones, with negligible smartphone exports (`~₹1,500 crore`). Following the **Large Scale Electronics Manufacturing (`LSEM`) PLI (2020)**—which attracted global contract manufacturers **Foxconn, Pegatron, Tata Electronics (which acquired Wistron's Narasapura plant), and Dixon Technologies**—India transformed into the **world's second-largest mobile phone manufacturer**, producing over **`99 per cent`** of its domestic mobile demand locally! By the completion of the LSEM tenure in June 2026, PLI firms alone recorded **`₹11.61 lakh crore`** in cumulative production and **`₹20,580 crore`** in capex (~3× target), catapulting smartphones into India's **single largest exported product line** (crossing `$20+ billion` annually, anchored by Apple iPhone exports from Tamil Nadu and Karnataka) and creating over `1.69 lakh` direct formal factory jobs—a high share of them young women workers!

> [!mains]
> **Critical Mains GS-3 Evaluation of PLI — Balancing Triumphs Against Four Deep Risks:**
> High-scoring UPSC Mains answers never treat PLI as an unqualified panacea; pair its genuine achievements (`₹2.58L cr` capex, electronics/pharma breakout) with **four rigorous critiques**:
> 1. **Assembly vs Domestic Value Addition (`DVA`) Critique:** Former RBI Governor **Raghuram Rajan** and **Rohit Lamba** (*Breaking the Mould*, 2023) argued that electronics PLI subsidised low-margin **"screwdriver assembly"** while imports of Chinese chips, displays, and PCBs surged alongside finished exports. **Nuanced Rebuttal & Policy Evolution:** Empirical evidence shows electronics DVA has climbed from `<10%` toward `18–20%+` (historically how China and Vietnam *also* started before localising sub-components); crucially, recognising this exact gap, the Government approved the dedicated **`₹22,919 crore` Electronics Component Manufacturing Scheme (`ECMS`, March 2025)** specifically targeting *passive/active sub-components* rather than final assembly!
> 2. **Skewed Sectoral & Fiscal Concentration:** Over **70 per cent** of cumulative PLI payouts (`₹36,754 cr` by Jun 2026) have gone to just **two sectors** (LSEM Electronics `₹19,091 cr` + Pharma `₹6,662 cr`), while critical green-energy and labour-intensive schemes (`ACC Batteries`, `Textiles`) lag due to rigid capex floors and upstream raw-material deficits.
> 3. **Subsidy Dependence vs Horizontal Competitiveness:** Subsidies cannot permanently substitute for high electricity tariffs, slow judicial contract enforcement, inverted customs duties, and skill gaps. Strict **five-year sunset clauses** must be honoured so PLI does not mutate into permanent corporate welfare.
> 4. **WTO Dispute Vulnerability:** Even though PLI avoids explicit export mandates, trading partners closely scrutinise local-value-addition (`DVA`) thresholds under Article `3.1(b)` of the WTO `ASCM` (prohibiting *import-substitution subsidies* contingent on domestic over imported goods)—demanding deft legal design and non-discriminatory ecosystem support.

### Industrial Corridors (NICDC), Special Economic Zones (SEZs) and PM MITRA Parks

Incentives alone cannot compensate for missing plug-and-play industrial land, multimodal logistics, and shared environmental infrastructure. India's spatial industrialisation strategy therefore rests on three complementary spatial instruments: **Greenfield Industrial Smart Cities (`NICDC`)**, **Special Economic Zones (`SEZs`)**, and **Sectoral Mega Parks (`PM MITRA`, Bulk Drug & Medical Device Parks)**.

#### National Industrial Corridor Development Programme (`NICDP` / `NICDC`)

Spearheaded by the **National Industrial Corridor Development Corporation (`NICDC`)** (under DPIIT, aligned with the **PM GatiShakti National Master Plan**), India is constructing **`11` Industrial Corridors** in four phases across the Golden Quadrilateral and Dedicated Freight Corridors—headed by flagship spines including the **Delhi–Mumbai Industrial Corridor (`DMIC`)** (with pioneering smart cities at **Dholera** in Gujarat, **Aurangabad/AURIC** in Maharashtra, **Vikram Udyogpuri** in MP, and **IITGNL** in Greater Noida), **Amritsar–Kolkata (`AKIC`)**, **Chennai–Bengaluru (`CBIC`)**, **Visakhapatnam–Chennai (`VCIC`)**, **Bengaluru–Mumbai (`BMIC`)**, **Hyderabad–Nagpur**, **Hyderabad–Warangal**, **Hyderabad–Bengaluru**, **Odisha Economic Corridor**, **Delhi–Nagpur**, and **East Coast Economic Corridor (`ECEC`)**.

**Landmark August 2024 Expansion — 12 New Greenfield Industrial Smart Cities:** On **28 August 2024**, the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (`CCEA`) approved **`12` new greenfield industrial nodes/smart cities** worth **`₹28,602 crore`** across **`10` States** along **`6` major corridors**, envisioned as a *"grand necklace of Industrial Smart Cities"* built **ahead of demand** on **"plug-and-play"** (ready-to-allot trunk utilities: roads, power, water, CETPs, multimodal freight links) and **"walk-to-work"** urban planning principles:
- **Northern & Eastern Nodes:** Khurpia (Uttarakhand), Rajpura–Patiala (Punjab), Hisar (Haryana), Agra & Prayagraj (Uttar Pradesh), Gaya (Bihar).
- **Western & Southern Nodes:** Jodhpur–Pali–Marwar (Rajasthan), Dighi Port Industrial Area (Maharashtra), Zaheerabad (Telangana), Orvakal & Kopparthy (Andhra Pradesh), and Palakkad (Kerala).
- **2026 Execution Status (PIB, 13 February 2026):** Special Purpose Vehicles (`SPVs`) have been incorporated for **all `12` cities**, environmental clearances obtained for **`11`**, equity released and Programme Managers onboarded for **`9`**, and EPC trunk-infrastructure contractors awarded for **`9` cities**—demonstrating markedly faster project execution than earlier decades!

#### Special Economic Zones (`SEZs`): Evolution, Stagnation, Baba Kalyani Panel and SEZ 2.0

India was actually **Asia's first country** to recognise the power of export-processing zones, establishing the **Kandla Export Processing Zone (`EPZ`) in Gujarat in 1965**! Yet bureaucratic controls and weak infrastructure kept India's EPZs minuscule compared to China's transformative Shenzhen SEZ (1980). To replicate China's export miracle, India introduced the **SEZ Policy in April 2000** and enacted the statutory **Special Economic Zones Act, 2005** (notified with **SEZ Rules, 2006** on 10 February 2006).

**Core Features of the SEZ Act, 2005:**
- **Deemed Foreign Territory:** Under `Section 53`, an SEZ is treated as territory *outside the customs territory of India* exclusively for authorised trade operations—exempting raw-material/capital-goods imports from customs duty and IGST, while sales from an SEZ into the **Domestic Tariff Area (`DTA`)** attract full customs duties as if they were foreign imports!
- **Net Foreign Exchange (`NFE`) Positive Mandate:** Every SEZ unit must earn cumulative **positive Net Foreign Exchange** over a five-year block.
- **Single-Window Governance:** Administered centrally by the **Board of Approval (`BoA`)** (Department of Commerce) and locally by a **Development Commissioner**.

**Why Indian SEZs Lost Momentum After 2011:** Unlike China's massive multi-product manufacturing cities, **over 60 per cent of operational Indian SEZs became tiny IT/ITeS real-estate enclaves** rather than factories! Three policy shocks compounded stagnation: (i) imposition of **Minimum Alternate Tax (`MAT`)** at `18.5%` in 2011–12 and Dividend Distribution Tax (`DDT`), (ii) the **sunset clause on `Section 10AA` Income-Tax exemptions** (ending for new units commencing operations after **31 March 2020**), and (iii) **WTO adverse ruling (`DS541`, October 2019)** holding India's export-contingent SEZ tax breaks inconsistent with the WTO `ASCM` once India crossed the `$1,000` per-capita GNI threshold (in 1990 dollars)! Meanwhile, **strict DTA customs walls** left vast tracts of notified SEZ land lying vacant because units could not profitably serve India's massive domestic market when global export demand slowed.

**The Reform Roadmap — Baba Kalyani Committee (2018), DESH Bill and 2025–2026 Amendments:**
- **Baba Kalyani Committee (constituted June 2018; report November 2018):** Recommended overhauling SEZs from narrow export enclaves into **"3Es — Employment and Economic Enclaves"**, **delinking fiscal incentives from export performance** (tying them instead to investment, employment, value addition, and inclusivity to ensure WTO compliance), shifting from supply-driven real-estate parks to demand-driven port/corridor hubs, enabling **seamless DTA domestic sales** (paying duties only on imported raw inputs rather than finished goods!), and creating a unified IFSC regulator (realised as **IFSCA** at GIFT City!).
- **Draft Development of Enterprise and Service Hubs (`DESH`) Bill, 2022:** Proposed replacing the SEZ Act, 2005 with integrated "Development Hubs" serving both export and domestic markets—though inter-ministerial revenue/customs concerns stalled parliamentary passage.
- **Targeted June 2025 SEZ Rules Amendments & February 2026 "SEZ 2.0" Committee:** Rather than waiting indefinitely for DESH, the Commerce Ministry issued surgical amendments to the **SEZ Rules, 2006 in June 2025** specifically for **semiconductor and electronics component manufacturing**: slashing the **minimum contiguous land requirement five-fold from `50 hectares` to `10 hectares`**, relaxing encumbrance-free land norms for government-leased/mortgaged plots, and permitting **domestic DTA sales** upon duty payment (immediately unlocking Micron's semiconductor SEZ in Sanand, Gujarat and Aequs's electronics component SEZ in Dharwad, Karnataka!). Furthermore, on **26 February 2026**, the Department of Commerce constituted an inter-ministerial **SEZ 2.0 Reform Committee** to harmonise SEZs, EOUs, and **MOOWR** (Manufacture and Other Operations in Warehouse Regulations) into a single competitive export-manufacturing architecture!

#### PM MITRA Mega Textile Parks (`5F` Vision)

To cure the extreme fragmentation of India's textile value chain—where spinning happens in Tamil Nadu, weaving in Maharashtra, processing in Gujarat, and garmenting in NCR/Bengaluru, inflating logistics costs by `8–10%`—the Government launched the **PM Mega Integrated Textile Region and Apparel (`PM MITRA`) Parks Scheme** (outlay **`₹4,445 crore`**, FY22–FY28). Anchored on the **"5F" Vision** (*Farm to Fibre to Factory to Fashion to Foreign*), **seven integrated greenfield/brownfield parks** (`~1,000+` acres each with plug-and-play factories, CETPs, worker hostels, and logistics hubs) were selected across **Tamil Nadu (Virudhunagar), Telangana (Warangal), Gujarat (Navsari), Karnataka (Kalaburagi), Madhya Pradesh (Dhar), Uttar Pradesh (Lucknow), and Maharashtra (Amravati)**. By **August 2026** (PIB), **100 per cent of land had been acquired**, environmental clearances secured, and DPRs worth `₹13,537 crore` finalised across all seven sites, attracting **`₹68,590 crore`** in early investment interests!

### Ease of Doing Business, World Bank B-READY, Jan Vishwas and NSWS

A decisive competitive advantage in modern manufacturing is **regulatory predictability**—the speed and transparency with which an enterprise registers land, secures power/environmental permits, pays taxes, and resolves commercial disputes.

- **From World Bank `Doing Business` to `B-READY` (Business Ready):** Between 2014 and 2019, India achieved an unprecedented **`79`-rank leap** in the World Bank's *Ease of Doing Business* rankings—climbing from **`142nd` (2014) to `63rd` (2019)** (led by Insolvency & Bankruptcy Code `IBC 2016`, GST, SPICe+ incorporation, and online construction permits)—before the World Bank **discontinued the Doing Business Report in 2020–21** over data-irregularity concerns in other economies. In **2024**, the World Bank launched its far more rigorous successor: **Business Ready (`B-READY`)**, assessing `180+` economies across **`10` business-lifecycle topics** (Business Entry, Location, Utility Services, Labour, Financial Services, International Trade, Taxation, Dispute Resolution, Market Competition, and Business Insolvency) through **three quantitative pillars**: **(i) Regulatory Framework**, **(ii) Public Services** (digital infrastructure supporting compliance), and **(iii) Operational Efficiency** (actual firm-level survey times/costs via World Bank Enterprise Surveys). After inaugural cohorts in 2024 (`50` economies) and 2025 (`+52` economies), **India is included in the Third `B-READY` Report scheduled for late 2026**!
- **Competitive Federalism — `BRAP` and Investment Friendliness Index:** Domestically, DPIIT drives state-level reform through the **Business Reforms Action Plan (`BRAP`)**, classifying States into *Top Achievers, Achievers, Aspirers,* and *Emerging Business Ecosystems* based on real-user feedback. Union Budget **2025–26** complemented this by announcing an **Investment Friendliness Index of States** to spur healthy competition on land, power, and single-window clearances.
- **Decriminalisation of Business Laws — Jan Vishwas Act (2023) & Jan Vishwas 2.0 Bill (2025):** Historically, Indian statutes criminalised hundreds of minor procedural lapses (such as late filing of registers or trivial packaging discrepancies) with imprisonment clauses, breeding fear and "inspector raj" rent-seeking.
  - **Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023** (assented **11 August 2023**): India's first omnibus decriminalisation statute, **decriminalising `183` provisions across `42` Central Acts** administered by `19` Ministries—replacing jail terms with proportionate civil monetary penalties and adjudication officers.
  - **Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Bill, 2025 (`Jan Vishwas 2.0`)** (introduced in Lok Sabha on **18 August 2025** and referred to a Select Committee): Expands trust-based governance across **`16` Central Acts** (`10` Ministries), amending **`355` provisions**—**decriminalising `288` provisions** for Ease of Doing Business and amending **`67` provisions** (under NDMC Act & Motor Vehicles Act) for Ease of Living, while introducing **non-punitive advisories/warnings for `76` first-time technical contraventions**!
- **National Single Window System (`NSWS`, launched September 2021):** A unified digital portal operated by DPIIT integrating approvals across **`32+` Central Ministries/Departments** and **`29+` States/UTs** via a Know-Your-Approvals (`KYA`) diagnostic engine—dramatically reducing duplicate filings for factory licences, environmental clearances (`PARIVESH`), and industrial land allotments.

## 4. Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs) and the Maharatna–Navratna Architecture

No account of India's industrial transformation is complete without examining the rise, crisis, institutional reform, and strategic repositioning of **Central Public Sector Enterprises (`CPSEs`)**. Under **`Section 2(45)` of the Companies Act, 2013**, a **Government Company** is defined as any company in which **not less than `51 per cent`** of the paid-up share capital is held by the Central Government, or by any State Government(s), or partly by the Centre and partly by one or more States—and explicitly includes any subsidiary company of such a Government Company. In India's official statistical architecture, the **Public Enterprises Survey** (compiled annually by the Department of Public Enterprises; its **65th edition for FY `2024–25`** records **`475` total CPSEs**, comprising **`291` operating CPSEs**, `81` under construction, `75` under closure/liquidation, and **`66` listed CPSEs**) covers Central Government companies and statutory corporations where the Centre (or another CPSE) holds over `50%` equity—notably **excluding** departmentally run undertakings (such as Indian Railways and India Post), public sector banks, and public sector insurance corporations.

### Historical Mission ("Temples of Modern India") versus Post-1980s Structural Sickness

At the dawn of the First Five-Year Plan (`1951`), India possessed merely **5 CPSEs** with a tiny cumulative investment of **`₹29 crore`**. Over the next four decades—guided by **IPR 1948**, **IPR 1956**, and Prime Minister **Jawaharlal Nehru's** evocative characterisation of great dams, steel plants, and public industrial complexes as the **"temples of resurgent India"** (popularly remembered as **"Temples of Modern India"**)—the State built an extraordinary industrial backbone encompassing **SAIL, BHEL, ONGC, IOCL, NTPC, Coal India, HAL, BEL, and ISRO-linked aerospace ecosystems**.

#### Five Foundational Contributions of the Early Public Sector

1. **Filling the Capital-Goods & Infrastructure Void:** Domestic private capital in the 1950s–60s could not mobilise the massive lump-sum outlays, long gestation horizons (10–15 years), and low initial financial returns required for integrated steel mills, heavy electrical turbines, refineries, offshore oil exploration, and national power grids. CPSEs absorbed that foundational risk.
2. **Balanced Regional Industrialisation:** By deliberately locating mega-plants in mineral-rich yet industrially backward states—**Bhilai** (then Madhya Pradesh, now Chhattisgarh), **Rourkela** (Odisha), **Durgapur** (West Bengal), **Bokaro** (then Bihar, now Jharkhand), **Barauni** (Bihar), and **Neyveli** (Tamil Nadu)—public enterprises planted growth poles, railways, schools, hospitals, and townships across non-metropolitan India.
3. **Strategic Self-Reliance & National Security:** Building domestic capabilities in defence aerospace (`HAL`, `BEL`, `Mazagon Dock`, `GRSE`), atomic power (`NPCIL`), energy security (`ONGC`, `IOCL`, `Coal India`), and space (`NSIL`) insulated India's foreign policy and sovereignty during geopolitical embargoes.
4. **Model Employment & Social Mobility:** CPSEs pioneered fair wages, housing colonies, worker welfare, and constitutional reservations (`SC/ST/OBC`) inside modern formal industry, expanding India's post-independence middle class.
5. **Countervailing Concentration of Private Monopolies:** State presence in basic raw materials (steel, coal, power, petrochemicals) prevented private cartels from monopolising foundational industrial inputs.

#### Anatomy of Post-1980s Public Sector Sickness

By the late 1970s and 1980s, however, a deep duality had opened across the CPSE universe. While a core cohort of energy, power, and resource majors remained solidly profitable, dozens of public enterprises—especially state-run textile mills (`NTC`), sick private firms nationalised to "save jobs", watches/machine tools (`HMT`), fertilisers, scooters, hotels (`ITDC`), and later telecom (`MTNL`/`BSNL`) and aviation (`Air India`)—sank into chronic losses, draining Union Budgets year after year. Five systemic pathologies explain this deterioration:

- **János Kornai's "Soft Budget Constraint":** Hungarian economist János Kornai coined this classic diagnosis for socialist/public enterprises: because managers and unions know the State will *always* bail out a failing PSU via budgetary loans, equity infusions, or sovereign guarantees rather than allow bankruptcy, financial discipline evaporates and losses compound endlessly.
- **Multi-Principal Interference and the "3C Paralysis":** Unlike private firms accountable to a single commercial Board and market discipline, CPSE executives faced conflicting mandates from administrative ministries (demanding populist pricing, politically directed plant locations, or excess hiring) alongside ex-post scrutiny by the **Comptroller and Auditor General (`CAG`)**, **Central Vigilance Commission (`CVC`)**, and **Central Bureau of Investigation (`CBI`)**—the infamous **"3C syndrome"** that punishes bona fide commercial risk-taking while rewarding bureaucratic inaction!
- **Administered Pricing & Quasi-Fiscal Burdens:** Governments routinely forced oil marketing CPSEs, coal companies, and power utilities to sell below market cost to shield consumers from inflation—eroding internal reserves and distorting balance sheets.
- **Overstaffing, Technological Obsolescence & Inability to Exit:** Rigid civil-service-style pay scales, unionised overmanning, slow public procurement rules, and political resistance to shutting unviable factories prevented CPSEs from adapting once post-1991 private and foreign rivals entered with agile technology.
- **Chronically Low Return on Equity (`RoE`):** Even excluding loss-makers, massive locked-up sovereign capital (including vast tracts of idle urban industrial land!) historically earned far below the government's own cost of borrowing—representing a huge opportunity cost of scarce funds badly needed for public schools, primary health centres, and rural roads.

### Department of Public Enterprises (DPE) and the July 2021 Finance Ministry Shift

To professionalise policy coordination across all CPSEs, the Government originally established the **Bureau of Public Enterprises (`BPE`) in `1965`** within the **Ministry of Finance** (on the recommendation of the Estimates Committee of Parliament). Following administrative reorganisation in September 1985, BPE was shifted to the Ministry of Industry and elevated in **May 1990** into a full-fledged **Department of Public Enterprises (`DPE`)** under the Ministry of Heavy Industries and Public Enterprises.

In a landmark governance reform notified on **6 July 2021**, the Union Government **transferred the Department of Public Enterprises (`DPE`) back into the Ministry of Finance**—making DPE the **sixth Department of the Ministry of Finance** (joining `DEA`, `DoE`, `DoR`, `DFS`, and `DIPAM`)! Why did this institutional shift matter so much? Because previously, **DPE** (managing CPSE capex, MoUs, governance, and surplus land) sat inside the Heavy Industries Ministry while **DIPAM** (managing CPSE disinvestment, dividends, and share buybacks) sat inside the Finance Ministry—causing friction and fragmented strategy. Unifying DPE and DIPAM inside the Ministry of Finance enabled seamless capital allocation, accelerated surplus land monetisation through the **National Land Monetisation Corporation (`NLMC`)**, and aligned CPSE capital expenditure directly with the Union Budget's macroeconomic strategy!

### The Three-Tier "Ratna" Classification: Maharatna, Navratna and Miniratna

Rather than treating hundreds of diverse CPSEs with identical bureaucratic micromanagement, the Government launched an autonomy-linked meritocratic tiering architecture starting in **1997**—granting progressively greater financial, joint-venture, M&A, and HR independence to high-performing, commercially sound public enterprises.

#### 1. Maharatna Status (Introduced February 2010 — Currently `14` CPSEs)

Launched vide **DPE Office Memorandum dated 4 February 2010**, the **Maharatna** scheme was engineered to empower India's largest, globally competitive CPSEs to expand into true Indian Multinational Corporations (`MNCs`) capable of acquiring overseas energy blocks, bidding for global megaprojects, and forming international joint ventures without waiting months for Cabinet clearance.

**Six Cumulative Quantitative & Qualitative Eligibility Criteria (Classic UPSC Prelims List!):**
1. Must already hold **Navratna status**.
2. Must be **listed on an Indian stock exchange** with minimum prescribed public shareholding under **SEBI regulations**.
3. **Average annual turnover of more than `₹25,000 crore`** during the last **3 years**.
4. **Average annual net worth of more than `₹15,000 crore`** during the last **3 years**.
5. **Average annual net profit after tax (`PAT`) of more than `₹5,000 crore`** during the last **3 years**.
6. Must possess a **significant global presence / international operations**.

**Approval Mechanism & Delegated Financial Autonomy:** Maharatna upgradation is approved by the **Union Finance Minister**, based on the recommendations of the **Inter-Ministerial Committee (`IMC`)** headed by the **Finance Secretary** and the **Apex Committee** headed by the **Cabinet Secretary**. Once conferred, a Maharatna Board can make **equity investments to establish financial JVs, wholly owned subsidiaries, or undertake domestic/overseas Mergers & Acquisitions (`M&A`) up to `₹5,000 crore` or `15 per cent` of its net worth (whichever is lower) in a single project**, subject to an overall cumulative ceiling of **`30 per cent` of net worth** across all projects! *(Moreover, Maharatna Boards enjoy virtually uncapped autonomy for domestic capital expenditure on new projects/modernisation without GoI equity support, plus broad HR powers to create board-minus-one posts.)*

#### 2. Navratna Status (Introduced July 1997 — `29` CPSEs as of 2025–2026)

Introduced in **July 1997** (originally identifying nine "jewels" with comparative advantage), the **Navratna** tier has expanded steadily—rising sharply from `16` in 2023 to **`29` Navratna CPSEs** by 2025–2026 following waves of upgradation across green energy, railways, shipbuilding, and refining (including **IREDA, Mazagon Dock Shipbuilders, NHPC, SJVN, SECI, RailTel, IRCTC, IRFC, Numaligarh Refinery [NRL], Chennai Petroleum [CPCL], and Garden Reach Shipbuilders [GRSE]**, alongside long-standing pillars like **BEL, CONCOR, EIL, NALCO, NBCC, NLC India, NMDC, SCI, RVNL, ONGC Videsh, RCF, IRCON, RITES, NFL, CWC, HUDCO, RINL, and MTNL**).

**Strict Multi-Parameter Eligibility Criteria:**
1. Must already be a **Miniratna Category-I** AND a **Schedule 'A' CPSE** (classified by DPE on size, turnover, and strategic importance).
2. Must have earned an **"Excellent" or "Very Good" rating** under the **MoU system** in **three of the last five years**.
3. Must attain a **composite score of `60` or above out of `100`** across **six specific quantitative performance parameters**:
   - *(i)* Net Profit to Net Worth
   - *(ii)* Manpower Cost to Total Cost of Production/Services
   - *(iii)* Profit Before Depreciation, Interest and Taxes (`PBDIT`) to Capital Employed
   - *(iv)* Profit Before Interest and Taxes (`PBIT`) to Turnover
   - *(v)* Earnings Per Share (`EPS`)
   - *(vi)* Inter-Sectoral Performance

**Delegated Autonomy:** Navratna Boards may invest **up to `₹1,000 crore` or `15 per cent` of net worth (whichever is lower) in a single project** (equity in JVs, wholly owned subsidiaries, domestic/overseas M&A), capped at **`30 per cent` of net worth** cumulatively—and enjoy unlimited capital-expenditure autonomy for domestic new projects/modernisation without GoI equity! *(Note: In 2025–2026, DPE also actively evaluated modernising the Ratna framework by updating financial thresholds and exploring intermediate tiers for rapidly scaling CPSEs.)*

#### 3. Miniratna Category-I and Category-II (Introduced October 1997; Consolidated DPE Guidelines 16 June 2026)

To extend commercial agility beyond elite mega-firms to profitable mid-sized CPSEs (**over `60–70` enterprises** across Cat-I and Cat-II, such as **Airports Authority of India, Cochin Shipyard, BSNL, Hindustan Copper, MOIL, MIDHANI, NSIC, Antrix, SPMCIL, ITDC**), DPE created **Miniratna Category-I and Category-II** (with consolidated autonomy guidelines formally re-codified by DPE on **16 June 2026**). Crucially, **Miniratna status is conferred directly by the concerned Administrative Ministry** (unlike Maharatna/Navratna which require Finance Ministry/Apex approval!).

- **Common Mandatory Conditions (Both Cat-I & Cat-II):** Continuous profits in the **last 3 years**, **positive net worth**, **no default** in repayment of GoI loans/interest, **no dependence on budgetary support or government guarantees**, and Board restructuring inducting at least three non-official Independent Directors.
- **Miniratna Category-I Extra Bar:** Must have recorded a **pre-tax profit of `₹30 crore` or more in at least ONE of the last 3 years**. **Autonomy:** Capex up to **`₹500 crore` or equal to net worth** (whichever is lower).
- **Miniratna Category-II:** Continuous 3-year profit + positive net worth (no `₹30 cr` bar). **Autonomy:** Capex up to **`₹300 crore` or `50 per cent` of net worth** (whichever is lower).

### Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) System and Corporate Governance Reforms

Granting autonomy without accountability invites managerial complacency; conversely, imposing rigid administrative micromanagement destroys commercial speed. To square this circle, India adopted the **Memorandum of Understanding (`MoU`) System** in **1987–88** based on the **Arjun Sengupta Committee Report (1984)** (inspired by the French *Contrat de Programme* and Korean performance-evaluation models).

- **How the MoU System Works:** An MoU is a mutually negotiated annual **performance contract** signed between the management of a CPSE and its Administrative Ministry—defining clear quantitative financial, operational, capex, and market-value targets at the start of the financial year in exchange for arm's-length managerial autonomy. Performance is evaluated rigorously by DPE and overseen by the **High Powered Committee (`HPC`)** chaired by the **Cabinet Secretary**, awarding composite grades (*Excellent, Very Good, Good, Fair, Poor*) that directly govern Ratna eligibility and executive Performance-Related Pay (`PRP`).
- **Modern Governance & Capital-Discipline Architecture (2016–2026):**
  1. **Board Professionalisation & SEBI Alignment:** DPE's Corporate Governance Guidelines (2010, aligned with `Companies Act 2013` and `SEBI LODR`) mandate independent Audit Committees, non-official **Independent Directors** (`1/3rd` of Board with non-executive Chair; `1/2` with executive Chair), women directors, and whistle-blower policies—though persistent vacancies in non-official Independent Directors remain a recurring governance critique!
  2. **Public Enterprises Selection Board (`PESB`):** Recommends meritocratic top management (CMDs and Functional Directors) for CPSEs, increasingly opening select strategic roles to private-sector lateral talent.
  3. **Consistent Capital Management & Dividend Policy (`DIPAM` / `CMCDC`):** Revised DIPAM guidelines mandate profitable CPSEs to distribute a minimum annual dividend of **`30%` of PAT or `4%` of net worth** (whichever is higher), issue bonus shares when reserves exceed `5×` paid-up equity, and undertake share buybacks when sitting on idle cash—dramatically improving CPSE capital discipline and stock-market valuation.
  4. **National Land Monetisation Corporation (`NLMC`, approved March 2022):** A `100%` Government-owned specialist real-estate company under **DPE (Ministry of Finance)** mandated to monetise **surplus, non-core urban and industrial land** lying idle across operating and closing CPSEs (unlocking `₹10,048 crore` in approved non-core land monetisation pipelines in FY26 alone!).

## 5. Disinvestment, Strategic Privatisation and Asset Monetisation

Across three and a half decades since 1991, few economic policies in India have aroused more ideological passion—or delivered more lessons in state capacity—than **disinvestment and privatisation**. At its heart lies a timeless public-finance and governance question: *When should the Indian State own commercial businesses, when should it exit and transfer management to private risk-takers, and when should it retain ownership of core infrastructure while leasing operational rights to private investors?* Distinguishing sharply across the **three concepts on the public-asset spectrum**, tracing India's institutional evolution from the Rangarajan Committee (1993) to the **New PSE Policy (2021)** and **National Monetisation Pipeline 2.0 (2026)**, and evaluating the pragmatic 2024–2026 shift toward **"wealth creation and dividend harvesting"** are essential for both UPSC Prelims and Mains GS Paper III.

### The Conceptual Spectrum: Minority Disinvestment, Strategic Privatisation and Asset Monetisation

Aspirants frequently blur these three terms—an error that directly costs marks in UPSC Prelims MCQs and Mains answers. Each occupies a distinct legal and economic point on the ownership–control continuum:

1. **Token / Minority Disinvestment:** The sale of a **minor equity stake** by the Government in a CPSE such that the **Government retains at least `51 per cent` shareholding AND uninterrupted management control**. The enterprise legally remains a Government Company (`Section 2(45)`). Common instruments include:
   - **Offer for Sale (`OFS`)** on stock exchanges (fastest route for listed CPSEs like Coal India, NHPC, GIC, LIC, Cochin Shipyard, Hindustan Copper).
   - **Initial Public Offering (`IPO`)** listing unlisted state giants for the first time (e.g., **LIC's historic `₹20,557 crore` IPO in May 2022** for `3.5%` stake; IRCTC, IREDA, RailTel).
   - **Exchange-Traded Funds (`ETFs`)** bundling baskets of CPSE shares (**CPSE ETF** & **Bharat-22 ETF**)—though later discouraged because continuous ETF dumping depressed CPSE stock prices!
   - **Share Buybacks** (CPSEs using surplus cash reserves to repurchase GoI shares) and **CPSE-to-CPSE Strategic Transfers / Cross-Holdings** (e.g., ONGC acquiring GoI's `51.11%` stake in HPCL for `₹36,915 crore` in 2018; PFC acquiring GoI's `52.63%` stake in REC for `₹14,500 crore` in 2019—criticised by economists as **"fiscal illusion"** because public assets merely move from one state pocket to another without private efficiency gains!).
2. **Strategic Disinvestment (Privatisation):** Defined officially by **DIPAM** as the **sale of a substantial portion of the Government shareholding of a CPSE (up to `50%` or higher, or even lower with a binding shareholders' agreement), explicitly accompanied by the TRANSFER OF MANAGEMENT CONTROL** to a strategic private buyer! Once management control shifts out of state hands (and government equity drops below `51%`), the company ceases to be a CPSE, escapes CAG/CVC/Article 12 bureaucratic fetters, and operates purely on private commercial principles. *(Remember the logical rule: **Every strategic disinvestment is privatisation, whereas minority disinvestment is NOT privatisation!**)*
3. **Asset Monetisation (Brownfield Value Recycling):** Fundamentally distinct from *both* disinvestment and privatisation! In **Asset Monetisation**, the Government **does NOT sell permanent ownership or equity** of public infrastructure. Instead, it grants **structured, time-bound contractual rights (typically 15–30 years)** to private operators or institutional investors to operate, maintain, and collect user revenues from **existing, de-risked, revenue-generating brownfield public assets** (operational NHAI highways, PGCIL power transmission lines, GAIL gas pipelines, airports, ports, rail stations, telecom towers) in exchange for **upfront capital payments or periodic revenue shares**—using instruments like **Toll–Operate–Transfer (`ToT`)**, **Infrastructure Investment Trusts (`InvITs`)**, **Real Estate Investment Trusts (`REITs`)**, and **Operate–Maintain–Transfer (`OMT`)**. Crucially, **ultimate sovereign title stays with the State**, and the asset **reverts back to the Government** upon concession expiry!

```compare
# Minority Disinvestment vs Strategic Privatisation vs Asset Monetisation
Minority Disinvestment | Strategic Disinvestment (Privatisation) | Asset Monetisation (NMP 1.0 & 2.0)
Ownership Transfer :: Partial equity sold; Govt retains ≥51% ownership | Majority / substantial equity sold (Govt <51% or 0%) | ZERO permanent ownership transfer; sovereign title stays with State
Management Control :: Remains strictly with Government (still a CPSE) | EXPLICITLY TRANSFERRED to private strategic buyer | Operational/revenue rights leased for 15–30 yrs; reverts to State
Primary Instruments :: OFS, IPO, CPSE/Bharat-22 ETFs, Buybacks, Cross-holdings | Competitive two-stage bidding (EOI + RFP) on Enterprise Value | Toll–Operate–Transfer (ToT), InvITs, REITs, OMT, PPP Concessions
Fiscal & Economic Goal :: Raise capital receipts, broaden retail equity & meet SEBI 25% MPS | Exit commercial businesses, halt fiscal bleed & unlock private efficiency | Recycle brownfield cash flows upfront to fund greenfield NIP/GatiShakti capex
Classic Indian Examples :: LIC IPO (2022) & 6.5% OFS (2026); Coal India, NHPC, Cochin Shipyard OFS | Air India (Tata Sons, 2022); NINL (Tata Steel, 2022); BALCO, HZL (2001–02) | NHAI Highways ToT & NHAI InvIT; PowerGrid InvIT; Airport PPP leases
```

### Institutional Architecture, Committees and Supreme Court Jurisprudence

India's disinvestment machinery evolved through trial, committee consensus, institutional upgrading, and pivotal Supreme Court constitutional tests:

#### Evolution of Institutional Machinery (1991–2026)

- **1991–92 Inauguration & Rangarajan Committee (1993):** Disinvestment began in `1991–92` (raising `₹3,038 crore`) as an urgent fiscal-deficit cushion by bundling minority shares of `31` PSUs to public financial institutions. To inject strategic rigour, the Government appointed the **Committee on Disinvestment of Shares in Public Sector Enterprises (1993)** under **Dr. C. Rangarajan**. It recommended capping state disinvestment at `49%` in reserved strategic sectors, allowing up to `74%` (or `100%`) in non-strategic sectors, establishing an autonomous permanent commission, and dedicating proceeds to retiring public debt and social infrastructure rather than financing routine revenue deficits.
- **Disinvestment Commission (1996, G.V. Ramakrishna) & First Strategic Wave (1999–2004):** Constituted as an advisory body in August 1996 under **G.V. Ramakrishna**, the Commission examined `58` PSUs and pushed India toward **strategic sales** over token minority tinkering. Between **1999 and 2004**, the dedicated **Ministry of Disinvestment** (headed by **Arun Shourie**) executed India's first high-impact strategic privatisation wave—selling management control in **Modern Foods, BALCO, CMC, HTL, VSNL, IBP, IPCL, Hindustan Zinc (`HZL`), Paradeep Phosphates, and Maruti Suzuki**—before the UPA era (`2004–2014`) shifted decisively back to profitable **minority IPOs/OFS** (`NTPC`, `Coal India`, `PGCIL`) while placing strategic privatisation on pause.
- **DIPAM (April 2016) & Three-Tier Approval Architecture:** Originally created as the Department of Disinvestment (`1999`), the agency was restructured and renamed the **Department of Investment and Public Asset Management (`DIPAM`)** in **April 2016** inside the Ministry of Finance—signalling a holistic mandate spanning **strategic disinvestment, minority stake sales, CPSE capital restructuring (dividends/buybacks/splits), and asset monetisation**. Today, strategic disinvestment follows a transparent multi-layered pipeline:
  1. **NITI Aayog** identifies CPSEs eligible for strategic disinvestment under the New PSE Policy.
  2. **Core Group of Secretaries on Disinvestment (`CGD`)**, chaired by the **Cabinet Secretary**, vets proposals and monitors execution.
  3. **Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (`CCEA`)** grants *"in-principle"* approval.
  4. **Alternative Mechanism (`AM`)**—an empowered ministerial group comprising the Finance Minister, Road Transport & Highways Minister, and Administrative Minister—approves transaction structure, reserve price, and winning bids transparently via two-stage open competitive bidding (`EOI` → `Virtual Data Room` → `RFP`).
- **National Investment Fund (`NIF`, Created November 2005; Revamped January 2013):** Created outside the Consolidated Fund of India in the **Public Account of India** to ring-fence disinvestment proceeds from routine revenue consumption. Originally, its corpus was permanent (`75%` of annual income for social sector schemes like health/education/employment; `25%` for reviving viable CPSEs). In **January 2013**, the Union Cabinet **restructured NIF** so that disinvestment proceeds credited to NIF can be deployed directly for **capital-creating sovereign purposes**: (i) **recapitalising Public Sector Banks (`PSBs`)** and public insurance companies, (ii) subscribing to rights/preferential equity issues of profitable CPSEs (preventing dilution below `51%`), (iii) financing capital expenditure of **Indian Railways, NHAI, metro rail, and NIIF**, and (iv) supporting strategic energy/infrastructure projects—ensuring one-time capital receipts build lasting capital assets!

> [!case]
> **Landmark Supreme Court Disinvestment Rulings — *BALCO* (2001) versus *CPIL* (2003):**
> - ***BALCO Employees' Union v. Union of India* (10 December 2001, 3-Judge Bench led by Justice B.N. Kirpal):** Employees challenged the `51%` strategic sale of Bharat Aluminium Company (`BALCO`) to Sterlite Industries, alleging undervaluation and violation of workers' rights. **Holding:** The Supreme Court dismissed the challenge, establishing the **foundational constitutional doctrine on privatisation**: disinvestment is a **purely economic policy decision** belonging to the Executive; **courts will NOT interfere with economic wisdom, methodology, or valuation** unless the action is patently *mala fide*, arbitrary, discriminatory, or contrary to a statute; further, PSU employees have **no vested Fundamental Right** to compel the State to retain perpetual majority ownership!
> - ***Centre for Public Interest Litigation (`CPIL`) v. Union of India* (16 September 2003, Justice S. Rajendra Babu & G.P. Mathur):** Petitioners challenged the executive decision to privatise **HPCL** and **BPCL** without Parliamentary approval. **Holding:** Carefully distinguishing *BALCO* (which was incorporated under the Companies Act), the Court noted that HPCL and BPCL had been nationalised through **specific Acts of Parliament** (ESSO Acquisition Act 1974; Burmah Shell Acquisition Act 1976) whose Preambles vested oil distribution in the State for the common good. Therefore, **a PSU created or nationalised by an Act of Parliament CANNOT be privatised by executive order alone without Parliamentary repeal or amendment!** *(This constitutional hurdle was later cleared when Parliament enacted the **Repealing and Amending Act, 2016**, repealing those legacy nationalisation statutes!)*

### New Public Sector Enterprise (PSE) Policy for Atmanirbhar Bharat (2021)

Announced under the Atmanirbhar Bharat package and formally unveiled in **Union Budget 2021–22** (notified **4 February 2021**), the **New Public Sector Enterprise (`PSE`) Policy** marks the sharpest ideological break from IPR 1956 in independent India's history. Guided by the principle that **the State has *"no business to be in business"*** where competitive private markets exist—and that public funds should concentrate on health, education, defence, and core public goods—the Policy classifies all commercial CPSEs (excluding non-profit Section 8 entities, regulatory authorities, and development/welfare agencies like FCI, AAI, or tribal/SC-ST finance corporations) into exactly **two overarching categories**:

| Policy Bucket | Sectors Covered | Sovereign Presence & Operational Mandate |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **A. Four Strategic Sectors** | **1.** Atomic Energy, Space and Defence<br>**2.** Transport and Telecommunications<br>**3.** Power, Petroleum, Coal and Other Minerals<br>**4.** Banking, Insurance and Financial Services | Maintain a **"bare minimum" presence** of public sector enterprises (typically `1` to `4` strong consolidated national champions per sector); **all remaining CPSEs** in these four sectors to be **privatised, merged, subsidiarised with another CPSE, or closed**! |
| **B. Non-Strategic Sectors** | All remaining commercial & industrial sectors (hotels, tourism, steel, cement, engineering, consumer goods, chemicals, textiles, fertilisers, consultancy, pharma) | **Zero permanent state commercial ownership:** All CPSEs in non-strategic sectors to be **strategically privatised** wherever feasible, **otherwise closed/wound up** and their surplus land monetised via **NLMC**! |

### National Monetisation Pipeline (NMP 1.0 to NMP 2.0, 2021–2030)

Complementing equity disinvestment with non-dilutive infrastructure financing, the Government launched the **National Monetisation Pipeline (`NMP`)**—crafted by **NITI Aayog** in partnership with infrastructure ministries—anchored on the motto **"Create through Monetisation"**. Rather than selling greenfield projects (which carry high land-acquisition and construction risks deterring institutional funds), NMP packages **operational, de-risked, cash-generating brownfield public assets** to attract long-term patient global capital (sovereign wealth funds, global pension funds, insurance pools) and recycles the upfront proceeds directly into building brand-new greenfield **National Infrastructure Pipeline (`NIP`) / PM GatiShakti** assets!

```flow
# National Monetisation Pipeline (NMP) Brownfield Asset Recycling Loop
1. De-Risked Brownfield Public Asset | Operational NHAI highways, PGCIL transmission grids, GAIL pipelines, ports, mines, rail corridors & airports already generating stable cash flows
2. Structured Concession (NO Ownership Sale!) | Asset monetised via ToT, InvITs, REITs, or PPP leases for 15–30 years; sovereign title stays permanently with Government of India
3. Patient Institutional Capital Unlocked | Global pension funds, sovereign wealth funds & domestic insurers pay upfront capital / concession fees for predictable inflation-linked yields
4. Greenfield Infrastructure Reinvestment | Ministries & CPSEs plough upfront receipts directly into new PM GatiShakti / NIP greenfield capex without widening fiscal deficit || 5. Asset Reversion | At concession end, upgraded modernised infrastructure reverts debt-free to public hands
```

#### NMP 1.0 Scorecard (FY22–FY25) and Launch of NMP 2.0 (23 February 2026)

- **NMP 1.0 (`FY2021–22` to `FY2024–25`, launched August 2021):** Targeted **`₹6.0 lakh crore`** across **`13` central ministries/sectors** (with the top five—Roads, Railways, Power, Oil & Gas Pipelines, and Telecom—accounting for `₹4.3 lakh crore` or `72%` of the pipeline). According to **NITI Aayog's official February 2026 NMP Report**, **NMP 1.0 achieved `₹5.3 lakh crore`**—an impressive **`~89–90 per cent` of its four-year target** (`₹3.87 lakh crore` during FY22–FY24 + `₹1.43 lakh crore` in FY25), spearheaded by **Highways (NHAI ToT & InvIT), Coal, Mines, Petroleum & Natural Gas, and Ports**, while **Railways** (passenger train/station PPP frictions) and **Telecom** (BSNL/MTNL tower/fibre lags) fell behind schedule. Crucially, NMP 1.0 catalysed deep institutional InvIT markets in India!
- **NMP 2.0 (`FY2025–26` to `FY2029–30`, launched 23 February 2026):** First signalled in **Union Budget 2025–26** as the **Asset Monetisation Plan 2025–30** to plough back **`₹10 lakh crore`** into new infrastructure projects, **National Monetisation Pipeline 2.0 (`NMP 2.0`)** was formally launched by Finance Minister **Nirmala Sitharaman** and **NITI Aayog** on **23 February 2026**. Expanding ambitiously across central ministries and CPSEs aligned with *Viksit Bharat @2047*, **NMP 2.0 projects an aggregate five-year monetisation potential of `₹16.72 lakh crore`** (over **`2.6×` NMP 1.0**!), explicitly incorporating **`₹5.8 lakh crore`** in private-sector investment alongside public-asset recycling!

### Empirical Evaluation (2021–2026): Why Strategic Sales Lagged and the Pivot to "Wealth Creation"

A candid, data-backed evaluation of India's post-2021 disinvestment record reveals a striking contrast between **bold policy intent** (the 2021 New PSE Policy) and **complex execution reality**—producing an important strategic recalibration since 2024.

#### Landmark Strategic Wins versus High-Profile Bottlenecks

> [!example]
> **Tale of Two Realities — Air India & NINL Triumphs versus BPCL, CONCOR & IDBI Bank Delays:**
> - **Air India Strategic Sale (Completed 27 January 2022):** After an unsuccessful 2018 attempt (when the State tried keeping a 24% stake), DIPAM switched to a clean **100% Enterprise Value (`EV`)** bid transferring Air India, 100% of Air India Express (`AIXL`), and 50% of AISATS to **Talace Pvt Ltd (wholly owned subsidiary of Tata Sons)** at **`₹18,000 crore` EV** (`₹2,700 crore` cash to GoI + `₹15,300 crore` debt retained), while isolating remaining legacy debt and `₹14,718 crore` non-core assets inside a clean SPV, **Air India Asset Holding Ltd (`AIAHL`)**. The deal permanently ended a **`~₹20 crore/day`** taxpayer haemorrhage and catalysed historic aircraft orders (`470+` jets) and private fleet revival!
> - **Neelachal Ispat Nigam Ltd (`NINL`, Completed July 2022):** India's first strategic disinvestment of a public-sector steel plant—`93.71%` equity held jointly by 4 CPSEs (`MMTC`, `NMDC`, `BHEL`, `MECON`) and 2 Odisha State PSUs (`IPICOL`, `OMC`) sold to **Tata Steel Long Products** at **`₹12,100 crore` Enterprise Value**, restarting a closed loss-making mill and protecting thousands of jobs! Followed subsequently by **Ferro Scrap Nigam Ltd (`FSNL`, FY25, `₹320 cr` to Konoike Transport)** and **Indian Medicines Pharmaceutical Corp. (`IMPCL`, `98.11%` strategic sale in FY27)**.
> - **Why Big-Ticket Strategic Sales Stalled (`BPCL`, `IDBI Bank`, `CONCOR`, `SCI`, `BEML`):**
>   - **BPCL:** Cancelled in May 2022 after bidders exited due to global fossil-fuel transition risks and unpredictable government retail fuel-price freezes.
>   - **IDBI Bank (`60.72%` combined GoI + LIC stake):** Stretched across FY24–FY27 due to rigorous **RBI "fit-and-proper"** screening of prospective global/domestic bank owners, valuation gaps, and investor caution.
>   - **CONCOR & SCI:** Snagged by complex **Indian Railways land-licensing fee disputes** (CONCOR) and protracted statutory demerger of non-core real estate into `SCI Land & Assets Ltd` (SCI), alongside trade-union pushback, parliamentary politics, and risk aversion among civil servants fearing ex-post "3C" valuation probes.

#### The 2024–2026 Pragmatic Pivot: From Rigid Targets to "Wealth Creation & Dividend Maximisation"

Between FY2020–21 and FY2023–24, India repeatedly fell sharply short of headline annual Budget disinvestment targets (e.g., `₹1.75 lakh crore` BE in FY22 vs `₹13,534 crore` actual; `₹51,000 crore` BE in FY24 vs `₹16,507 crore` actual). Recognising that announcing rigid annual targets telegraphs distress to stock markets—depressing CPSE share valuations right before sales!—the Finance Ministry executed a **quiet paradigm shift starting in Budget 2024–25**:

1. **Scrapping Standalone Disinvestment Targets:** Beginning FY25, the Union Budget merged disinvestment and asset monetisation into one flexible **`Miscellaneous Capital Receipts`** line item—allowing DIPAM to time market transactions opportunistically without fire-sale pressure!
2. **Focus on "CPSE Wealth Creation" and Record Dividend Harvesting:** Through disciplined capex, stronger governance, calibrated OFS spacing, and strict **CMCDC dividend norms**, listed CPSE market capitalisation soared across 2023–2026 while **non-tax CPSE dividend receipts to the Centre doubled**—climbing steadily from **`₹39,750 crore` (FY21)** → **`₹64,030 crore` (FY24)** → **`₹74,129 crore` (FY25)** → an all-time record **`₹78,438 crore` in FY2025–26**!
3. **Balanced 2025–2027 Receipt Surge:** In **FY `2025–26`**, DIPAM comfortably beat its `₹33,837 crore` Revised Estimate for Miscellaneous Capital Receipts—realising **`₹45,306 crore`** (`₹16,886 cr` disinvestment + `₹28,420 cr` asset monetisation) alongside **`₹78,438 cr` in dividends** (`₹1.237 lakh crore` total receipts!). For **FY `2026–27`**, the Centre raised Miscellaneous Capital Receipts BE to **`₹80,000 crore`**—already mobilising **`₹55,757 crore` in disinvestment receipts** by November 2026 (anchored by a landmark **`6.5%` OFS in LIC yielding `₹31,515 crore`**, alongside timely OFS tranches in Coal India, NHPC, GIC, Hindustan Copper, Central Bank of India, IRFC, Cochin Shipyard, and NLC India!).

> [!debate]
> **The Enduring Mains Debate — Should India Privatise Profit-Making CPSEs or Retain Them as Dividend Cash Cows?**
> - **Case FOR Strategic Privatisation (Even of Profitable Non-Strategic CPSEs):** (i) **Opportunity Cost & Governance Gains:** Private ownership unlocks higher Total Factor Productivity (`TFP`), agile innovation, and freedom from CAG/CVC/ministerial rigidity (as demonstrated historically by Hindustan Zinc and Maruti Suzuki post-privatisation, whose market value and tax contributions multiplied dramatically!). (ii) **Sovereign Focus:** Public capital and administrative bandwidth should prioritise public health, foundational literacy, climate adaptation, and frontier R&D rather than running airlines, hotels, or retail fuel stations. (iii) **Fiscal Sustainability:** Strategic sales retire public debt permanently and reduce contingent sovereign liabilities.
> - **Case FOR Selective Retention & Value Maximisation:** (i) **Strategic & Energy Security:** During global shocks (COVID-19, Russia–Ukraine energy spike, West Asia crises), national champions like ONGC, IOCL, HAL, Coal India, and NTPC provide strategic price-buffering, emergency supply continuity, and counter-cyclical capex that profit-maximising private firms will not absorb. (ii) **Avoiding Private Monopolies / Cronyism:** Privatising natural monopolies without strong independent regulators merely replaces an inefficient *public* monopoly with a rent-extracting *private* oligopoly! (iii) **Reliable Non-Tax Revenue & Social Equity:** Profitable CPSEs delivered **`₹78,438 crore`** in annual dividends in FY26 alone while upholding constitutional reservation policies in formal employment.
> - **Balanced Mains Synthesis:** Privatise **chronically sick and non-strategic commercial CPSEs decisively** (following the Air India/NINL SPV-carveout template), **retain lean, professionally governed, board-autonomous national champions** strictly in core strategic domains (defence, atomic energy, grid/energy security), and scale **brownfield Asset Monetisation (`NMP 2.0`)** to finance greenfield public infrastructure without losing sovereign ownership!

## 1. Evolution of India's Industrial Policies (1948–2026) and Industrial Output Measurement

- **IPR 1948 (6 Apr 1948, S.P. Mookerjee):** Established India's **mixed economy** with a **4-fold classification**: (1) **Exclusive State Monopoly (`3`)** — arms/ammunition, atomic energy, railways; (2) **State-Led New Units (`6`)** — coal, iron & steel, aircraft, shipbuilding, telecom apparatus, mineral oils (existing private units got 10-yr cushion); (3) **Regulated Private (`18`)** — autos, cement, sugar, textiles, fertilisers, chemicals; (4) **Residual Private/Cooperative**.
- **IDRA 1951 (8 May 1952):** Statutory backbone of **Licence Raj** (`Entry 52`, Union List). Mandated registration & Central **Industrial Licences** for new units, new articles, substantial expansion, or relocation in Scheduled Industries; allowed investigation, takeover & price control (`Sec. 18G`); set up **Central Advisory Council (`CAC`)** & Development Councils.
- **IPR 1956 (30 Apr 1956 — "Economic Constitution of India"):** Linked to **Socialistic Pattern of Society** (1954 / Avadi 1955) & **2nd Plan (Mahalanobis capital-goods model)**. **3 Schedules:** **Sch. A (`17` exclusive State)**, **Sch. B (`12` progressively State-owned, private supplements)**, **Sch. C (residual private under `IDRA`)**. Anchored backward-region plants (Bhilai, Rourkela, Durgapur) & small industries.
- **Peak Controls (1969–73):** **Dutt Committee / ILPIC (1969)** exposed pre-emptive licence-hoarding → **MRTP Act, 1969** (Jun 1970; asset cap `₹20 cr` → `₹100 cr` in 1985; MRTP Commission); **1970 Policy** (Core Sector & **Joint Sector** `≥26%` PFI equity); **FERA, 1973** (`40%` foreign equity cap, criminal penalties; IBM & Coca-Cola exited 1977).
- **1977, 1980 & 1985–86 Shifts:** **1977 Janata Policy** jumped **SSI reservation (`180 → 504` items; peaked `>800`)**, created **Tiny Sector (`≤₹1L`)**, launched **District Industries Centres (`DICs`)**. **1980 Policy** pushed economic federalism, **Nucleus Plants** & `5%/yr` automatic expansion. **1985–86** introduced **broad-banding** & `₹100 cr` MRTP limit.
- **New Industrial Policy (`NIP`, 24 Jul 1991 — Rao & Manmohan Singh):** Dismantled Licence–Permit–Quota Raj:
  - **Delicensing:** Abolished for all except **`18`** (simple `IEM` filing) → now **only `4` active compulsory-licensing industries** (DPIIT): *(i)* Tobacco cigars/cigarettes (no fresh licences since 1999), *(ii)* Electronic aerospace & defence equipment, *(iii)* Industrial explosives, *(iv)* Specified hazardous chemicals (hydrocyanic acid, phosgene, isocyanates/MIC). *(Potable alcohol moved to States per SC's **Bihar Distillery 1997**; final `20` SSI-reserved items ended **10 Apr 2015** → **zero** reserved today!)*
  - **PSU De-reservation:** Shrunk Sch. A **`17 → 8` (1991) → `2` today**: **Atomic Energy** & **Railway Operations** (private freight/rolling stock/stations allowed).
  - **MRTP → Competition Act 2002 (Raghavan Panel):** Created **CCI** (cartels, dominance abuse, mergers; **2023 Amend**: `>₹2,000 cr` deal-value threshold).
  - **FDI & FEMA:** `51%` automatic FDI in 34 priority sectors (now `100%` automatic in most mfg); **FIPB** abolished May 2017; civil **FEMA 1999** replaced FERA; scrapped PMP & convertibility clause.
- **IIP (NSO, MoSPI — Monthly Volume Index):** Rebased **`2011–12` → `2022–23`** on **1 Jun 2026** (`TAC-IIP`); switched value deflator from WPI to **Output PPI** on **29 Jun 2026** (`234/463` groups). **4 Sectors (`NIC-2025`, `463` groups / `1,042` products):** **Manufacturing `76.06%`** (`77.63%` old), **Mining `11.05%`** (`14.37%` old; added minor/rare earths), **Electricity & Gas `10.87%`** (`7.99%` old), **NEW 4th — Water Supply, Sewerage & Waste `2.02%`**. Use-based (`2022–23`): Primary `31.14%`, Intermediate `22.42%`, Consumer Non-Durables `16.15%`, Durables `11.31%`, Infra/Construction `10.91%`, Capital Goods `8.08%`.
- **Index of Core Industries (`ICI` — OEA, DPIIT, Commerce Ministry!):**
  - **Old `2011–12` (`8` Core = `40.27%` of IIP — R–E–S–C–C–N–C–F):** Refinery (`28.04%`) > Electricity (`19.85%`) > Steel (`17.92%`) > Coal (`10.33%`) > Crude Oil (`8.98%`) > Natural Gas (`6.88%`) > Cement (`5.37%`) > Fertilisers (`2.63%`).
  - **NEW `2022–23` (20 Jul 2026 — `9` Core = `32.88%` of IIP — E–R–S–C–C–I–C–N–F):** Added **Iron Ore** as **9th Core**; **Electricity (`30.93%`) overtook Refinery (`22.57%`) as #1**; Raw Coal only; Gross Steel! Exact weights: **Electricity `30.93%` > Refinery `22.57%` > Steel `17.58%` > Crude Oil `7.43%` > Coal `5.60%` > Iron Ore `4.91%` (NEW) > Cement `4.41%` > Natural Gas `3.84%` > Fertilisers `2.73%`**.

## 2. Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs): Backbone of Employment and Inclusive Growth

- **Macro Weight (Economic Survey 2025–26):** **`31.1%` GDP**, **`35.4%` manufacturing output**, **`48.58%` exports**, **`7.47+ cr` units** (`9.78 cr` Udyam + UAP by Oct 2026; `37.6%` women-owned), **`32.8–43+ cr` livelihoods** — **2nd-largest employer after agriculture**.
- **Classification Evolution:** **MSMED Act 2006** (investment-only, lower Services caps) → **2020 Composite (`S.O. 2119(E)`, 1 Jul 2020)** (Mfg = Services; Inv + Turnover via ITR/GSTN; **EXPORTS EXCLUDED**; traders added Jul 2021 for PSL) → **Budget 2025–26 (`S.O. 1364(E)`, 21 Mar 2025; w.e.f. `1 Apr 2025` — `2.5×` Inv & `2×` Turnover):** **Micro** `≤₹2.5 cr / ≤₹10 cr`; **Small** `≤₹25 cr / ≤₹100 cr`; **Medium** `≤₹125 cr / ≤₹500 cr`. Crossing **EITHER** triggers upward shift (**3-yr non-tax benefit retention**); falling below **BOTH** triggers downward shift.
- **Bottlenecks:** **"Missing Middle" & "Dwarfism" (Economic Survey 2018–19):** Firms `>10 yrs` old (`<100` workers) = `>50%` factories yet only `14.1%` jobs & `7.6%` NVA due to threshold cliffs. **Credit gap:** `₹25–30L cr` (**U.K. Sinha Panel 2019**). **Delayed payments:** `15/45-day` cap (`Sec. 15` MSMED Act, `3×` Bank Rate interest); **`Sec. 43B(h)` IT Act (Finance Act 2023, eff. Apr 2024)** disallows buyer tax deductions for overdue payments to registered Micro & Small suppliers (not Medium).
- **Policy Ecosystem (2024–2026):** **Udyam + Udyam Assist (`UAP`, SIDBI)** formalises IMEs for PSL. **TReDS** (5 RBI platforms, non-recourse factoring; mandatory buyer turnover cut **`₹500 → ₹250 cr` on 7 Nov 2024**; `₹7L+ cr` unlocked; Budget 2026–27 CPSE mandate + CGTMSE cover). **CGTMSE (>1 cr guarantees):** Budget 2025–26 doubled MSE cover **`₹5 → ₹10 cr`** (eff. 1 Apr 2025), Startups `₹20 cr`, exporter MSMEs `₹20 cr`, transgender MSEs 85% cover; `₹5L` micro credit cards; `₹100 cr` **MCGS-MSME**. **MUDRA (SIDBI refinance only):** Shishu `≤₹50k`, Kishore `₹5L`, Tarun `₹10L`, **Tarun Plus `₹20L` (2024)** for repaid Tarun borrowers. **Equity:** `₹50,000 cr` **SRI Fund** (`₹15,442 cr` in `682` MSMEs) + Budget 2026–27 **`₹10,000 cr` SME Growth Fund**. **Schemes:** **PM Vishwakarma** (`₹13,000 cr`, 18 artisan trades, `₹15k` toolkit, `₹1L+₹2L` @ `5%`), **RAMP** (`₹6,062 cr`, World Bank), **Public Procurement (`25%` MSE: `4%` SC/ST + `3%` Women) & GeM**, **SAMADHAAN (`161` MSEFCs)**, **CHAMPIONS**, **ONDC**.

## 3. Make in India, Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) Schemes and the Manufacturing Ecosystem

- **Mfg Share & 2025–26 Pickup:** Stalled at `14–17%` GDP vs `25%` target (NMP 2011 / Make in India 2014) due to logistics/power cross-subsidies, land/labour rigidities, inverted tariffs, `~0.65%` R&D & Rodrik's premature deindustrialisation. **Economic Survey 2025–26:** Real Mfg GVA **`+7.72%` (Q1)** & **`+9.13%` (Q2 FY26)**; **Med/High-Tech = `46.3%` MVA**; **UNIDO CIP `37th`**; **GII `38th`**; **ISM (`₹76,000 cr`)** approved **`10` chip projects across `6` States (`₹1.60L cr`)**.
- **Make in India (`2014`, 4 pillars) → Make in India 2.0 (`27` sectors: `15` Mfg + `12` Services) → National Manufacturing Mission (Budget 2025–26):** Small/medium/large focus across **5 focal areas** (Ease/cost, future workforce, MSMEs, tech, quality) + **Clean Tech Manufacturing** (solar PV, EV batteries, motors, electrolysers, wind turbines, HV grid) + Footwear/Leather Focus Scheme (`22L` jobs) & Toys Plan.
- **PLI (`14` Sectors; Approved Outlay `₹1.91L cr` [initially `₹1.97L cr`]; DPIIT nodal):** Outcome-based **`4–6%` incremental-sales cash payout for 5 yrs** (NO upfront subsidy; export-delinked for WTO `ASCM`). **Scorecard (30 Jun 2026, PIB):** `791` approvals, **`₹2.58L cr` capex**, **`₹23.79L cr` output/sales**, **`₹15.53L cr` exports**, **`14.57L` jobs** (`8.81L` direct + `5.75L` indirect), **`₹36,754 cr` disbursed**. **Stars:** **LSEM Electronics** (`₹20,580 cr` capex ~3× target, `₹11.61L cr` output, `₹19,091 cr` disbursed, #2 mobile maker & Apple hub), **Pharma** (`₹46,744 cr` capex, `₹4.02L cr` sales), **Food** (`3.29L` direct jobs), **Telecom**, **Autos**, **White Goods**. **Laggards & Critiques:** **ACC Batteries, MMF Textiles, Specialty Steel**, upstream Solar wafers; **Rajan–Lamba DVA critique** countered via **`₹22,919 cr` Electronics Component Scheme (`ECMS`, Mar 2025)**; WTO `Art. 3.1(b)` watch.
- **Corridors, SEZs, Parks & Regulation:**
  - **NICDC (`11` Corridors):** **28 Aug 2024** Cabinet cleared **`12` new greenfield smart cities (`₹28,602 cr`, `10` States, `6` corridors)** ("plug-and-play", "walk-to-work"; 12 SPVs, 9 EPC, 11 EC by Feb 2026).
  - **SEZs:** Asia's 1st EPZ **Kandla (1965)** → **SEZ Act 2005** (foreign territory, positive 5-yr `NFE`, `Sec. 10AA` sunset Mar 2020, WTO `DS541`) → **Baba Kalyani (2018)** ("3Es", export-delinked perks, DTA flexibility) → **DESH Bill 2022** → **Jun 2025 SEZ Rules** cut chip/electronics land **`50 → 10 ha`** + DTA sales (Micron Sanand, Aequs Dharwad) → **Feb 2026 SEZ 2.0 Panel**.

## 4. Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs) and the Maharatna–Navratna Architecture

- **Scope:** `Sec. 2(45)` Companies Act 2013 (`≥51%` Govt equity + subsidiaries). **65th Public Enterprises Survey (FY25):** `475` CPSEs (`291` operating, `66` listed) — **EXCLUDES** Railways/Posts, PSBs (SBI), & public insurers (LIC).
- **Arc:** Nehru's **"Temples of Modern India"** (capital goods, regional growth poles, defence) → post-1980s sickness via **Kornai's Soft Budget Constraint**, administered pricing, overstaffing & **"3C paralysis"** (CAG/CVC/CBI).
- **DPE:** BPE (1965, MoF) → DPE (1990, Heavy Industries) → **shifted back to Ministry of Finance on `6 Jul 2021`** (6th MoF Dept).
- **Three-Tier Ratnas:**
  - **Maharatna (Feb 2010 — `14` CPSEs):** Navratna + Listed + 3-yr avg **Turnover `>₹25,000 cr`**, **Net Worth `>₹15,000 cr`**, **PAT `>₹5,000 cr`** + Global presence. **Autonomy:** `₹5,000 cr` or `15%` NW (`30%` overall); approved by FM (IMC + Apex Panel). **All 14:** Oil/Gas (`6`: ONGC, OIL [13th, 2023], IOCL, BPCL, HPCL, GAIL) + Power/NBFC (`4`: NTPC, PGCIL, PFC, REC) + Metals/Coal/Engg (`3`: CIL, SAIL, BHEL) + Defence (`1`: **HAL — 14th, 12 Oct 2024**).
  - **Navratna (Jul 1997 — `29` CPSEs):** Miniratna-I + Sch. 'A' + `3/5` yrs Excellent/Very Good MoU + **Score `≥60/100`** on `6` parameters. **Autonomy:** `₹1,000 cr` or `15%` NW (`30%` overall).
  - **Miniratna I & II (Oct 1997; DPE consolidation 16 Jun 2026):** Conferred by Admin Ministry; 3 yrs continuous profit, positive NW, no GoI default/budget support. **Cat-I:** `≥₹30 cr` pre-tax profit in `≥1` yr (`₹500 cr` / `100%` NW); **Cat-II:** (`₹300 cr` / `50%` NW).
- **Governance:** **MoU System (1987–88, Arjun Sengupta Panel 1984)** via Cabinet Secy HPC; **PESB**; Independent Directors; **CMCDC** (`≥30%` PAT or `4%` NW dividend); **NLMC (2022, DPE)** monetising surplus CPSE land (`₹10,048 cr` approved FY26).

## 5. Disinvestment, Strategic Privatisation and Asset Monetisation

- **Three Concepts:** (1) **Minority Disinvestment:** Govt keeps **`≥51%` & control** (OFS, IPO, ETFs, Buybacks, CPSE cross-holdings like ONGC–HPCL). (2) **Strategic Disinvestment (Privatisation):** Substantial/majority sale **+ TRANSFER OF MANAGEMENT CONTROL**. (3) **Asset Monetisation:** Leasing **operational brownfield assets** (`ToT`, `InvITs`, `REITs`, `OMT`, `PPP`) for 15–30 yrs **WITHOUT selling sovereign ownership** (reverts to State).
- **Institutions & Courts:** **Rangarajan (1993)** → **Disinvestment Commission (1996, G.V. Ramakrishna)** → Shourie strategic wave (1999–2004: BALCO, HZL, VSNL, Maruti) → **DIPAM (Apr 2016, MoF)** + NITI Aayog + CGD + CCEA + Alternative Mechanism. **NIF (2005, Public Account; revamped 2013):** funds PSB recap, Railways/infra capex & CPSE equity. ***BALCO* (Dec 2001):** economic policy immune from judicial review absent *mala fides*. ***CPIL* (Sep 2003):** PSUs nationalised by **Parliamentary Acts** (HPCL/BPCL) need **Parliamentary repeal** (done via **Repealing Act 2016**).
- **New PSE Policy (Budget 2021–22, 4 Feb 2021):** **4 Strategic Sectors** ("bare minimum" CPSEs): *(1)* Atomic Energy, Space & Defence; *(2)* Transport & Telecom; *(3)* Power, Petroleum, Coal & Minerals; *(4)* Banking, Insurance & Financial Services. **Non-Strategic:** Privatise or close!
- **NMP 1.0 → NMP 2.0:** **NMP 1.0 (`FY22–25`):** `₹6.0L cr` target → **`₹5.3L cr` (`~89–90%`) achieved** (Highways/Coal/Mines/Oil/Ports led; Railways/Telecom lagged). **NMP 2.0 (`FY26–30`, launched `23 Feb 2026`):** Expanded from Budget 2025–26's `₹10L cr` plough-back to **`₹16.72L cr` aggregate potential** (incl. `₹5.8L cr` private investment; `>2.6×` NMP 1.0).
- **Record & 2024–26 Pivot:** **Wins:** **Air India (27 Jan 2022, Tata Sons `₹18,000 cr` EV: `₹2,700 cr` cash + `₹15,300 cr` debt; AIAHL SPV)**, **NINL (Jul 2022, Tata Steel `₹12,100 cr` EV)**, **FSNL (`₹320 cr`)**, **IMPCL**. **Delays:** BPCL (cancelled 2022), IDBI Bank (`60.72%`), CONCOR, SCI. **2024–26 Shift:** Replaced standalone disinvestment targets with **`Miscellaneous Capital Receipts`** (`₹45,306 cr` realised FY26; `₹80,000 cr` BE FY27 with `₹55,757 cr` disinvestment realised by Nov 2026 incl. **`6.5%` LIC OFS = `₹31,515 cr`**) + **"Wealth Creation & Dividends"** (record **`₹78,438 cr` FY26 CPSE dividends**!).
