## 1. Quick anchor

Part III of the Constitution (Articles 12 to 35) guarantees six groups of rights that a person can enforce against the State. They are justiciable: a person whose right is violated can go straight to the Supreme Court under Article 32 or to a High Court under Article 226.

## 2. The six groups

| Group | Articles | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| Right to equality | 14 to 18 | Equality before law, no discrimination, equal opportunity in public employment, abolition of untouchability and titles |
| Right to freedom | 19 to 22 | Six freedoms, protection in respect of conviction, life and personal liberty, education, protection against arrest and detention |
| Right against exploitation | 23 and 24 | Ban on forced labour and on child labour in hazardous work |
| Freedom of religion | 25 to 28 | Conscience, religious affairs, taxes for religion, religious instruction |
| Cultural and educational rights | 29 and 30 | Protection of minorities and their institutions |
| Constitutional remedies | 32 | The right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement |

The right to property was a Fundamental Right until the 44th Amendment Act, 1978. It is now a legal right under Article 300A.

## 3. Who holds them, and against whom

- Article 12 defines "the State": the Union and state governments and legislatures, local authorities, and "other authorities" such as statutory corporations.
- Article 13 makes any law that abridges a Fundamental Right void to that extent. This is the textual basis of judicial review.
- Articles 15, 16, 19, 29 and 30 are available only to citizens. The rest, including Articles 14 and 21, protect every person.

## 4. Article 21 and how it grew

Article 21 says no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.

- *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978): the procedure must be just, fair and reasonable. Articles 14, 19 and 21 are read together.
- *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017): a nine-judge bench held that privacy is part of life and personal liberty.

## 5. Remedies and writs

Dr Ambedkar called Article 32 the heart and soul of the Constitution. The Supreme Court can issue five writs:

1. **Habeas corpus**: produce a detained person before the court.
2. **Mandamus**: command a public authority to do its duty.
3. **Prohibition**: stop a lower court from exceeding its jurisdiction.
4. **Certiorari**: quash an order already passed by a lower court or tribunal.
5. **Quo warranto**: ask a person by what authority they hold a public office.

## 6. Limits

- The rights are not absolute. Article 19 itself lists the grounds for reasonable restrictions.
- During a national emergency, Article 19 is suspended automatically only when the emergency is declared on the ground of war or external aggression (Article 358).
- Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended in any emergency. The 44th Amendment added this safeguard.
- Parliament can amend Part III, but not so as to damage the basic structure (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973).

## The trap

Examiners test the citizen-only list and the emergency rule most often. Article 14 and Article 21 protect foreigners too. Article 19 does not.

<!-- distilled -->

- Part III, Articles 12 to 35. Six groups of rights, enforceable under Articles 32 and 226.
- Property left Part III in 1978 (44th Amendment) and is now Article 300A.
- Citizen-only: Articles 15, 16, 19, 29, 30.
- Article 21: procedure must be just, fair and reasonable (*Maneka Gandhi*, 1978). Privacy is included (*Puttaswamy*, 2017).
- Five writs: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto.
- Articles 20 and 21 can never be suspended. Article 19 is suspended only in a war or external-aggression emergency.
- Amendments cannot damage the basic structure (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973).
