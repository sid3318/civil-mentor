## 1. How the Constitution is organised

The Constitution is arranged in three layers. **Parts** group related provisions by subject: Part III on Fundamental Rights, for example. **Articles** are the individual provisions, numbered continuously from 1 to 395. **Schedules** are lists and tables attached at the end, which keep detail out of the main text: the list of states, the forms of oaths, the division of legislative powers.

The structure has changed a great deal since 1950. The Constitution began with 395 Articles in 22 Parts and 8 Schedules. Amendments have added about 75 Articles, three Parts and four Schedules. New provisions are given letter suffixes so that the original numbering survives: Article 21A follows Article 21, and Part IVA follows Part IV. One Part, Part VII on the old Part B states, was repealed in 1956.

Knowing the map has two uses. Prelims regularly asks which Schedule or Part contains what, and which amendment added it. Mains answers are stronger when they cite the right Article: "Article 21" rather than "the right to life".

Part IXB (Co-operative societies) came with the 97th Amendment (2011).

## 3. The twelve Schedules

| Tenth | Disqualification of legislators on grounds of defection |
| Eleventh | The 29 subjects that may be devolved to panchayats |

> **Remember:** a mnemonic for the order of the Schedules is "**T**EARS **O**F **O**LD **P**M": **T**erritory, **E**moluments, **A**ffirmations (oaths), **R**ajya Sabha seats, **S**cheduled Areas, **O**ther tribal areas (Sixth), **F**ederal lists (Seventh), **O**fficial languages, **L**and reforms (Ninth), **D**efection, **P**anchayats, **M**unicipalities.

> **Prelims pointer:** The oaths of the **President, Vice-President and Governors** are not in the Third Schedule; they are in the Articles themselves (60, 69 and 159). The Third Schedule covers ministers, legislators, judges and the CAG.

### Four schedules that matter beyond facts

**The Seventh Schedule: the federal bargain.** It distributes legislative power between the Centre and the states in three lists. The **Union List** (originally 97 subjects, now about 100) covers matters of national importance, such as defence, foreign affairs, banking and atomic energy. The **State List** (originally 66, now about 61) covers matters of local importance, such as public order, police, public health and agriculture. The **Concurrent List** (originally 47, now about 52) covers subjects on which both can legislate, with Union law prevailing in a conflict: criminal law, marriage, forests and education, among others. The 42nd Amendment (1976) moved five subjects from the State List to the Concurrent List: education, forests, weights and measures, protection of wild animals and birds, and the administration of justice. Residuary powers, over subjects in none of the lists, belong to Parliament (Article 248). The centre–state relations note covers how these lists work in practice.

**The Eighth Schedule: languages.** It began with 14 languages and now has **22**: Assamese, Bengali, Bodo, Dogri, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, Konkani, Maithili, Malayalam, Manipuri, Marathi, Nepali, Odia, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Santhali, Sindhi, Tamil, Telugu and Urdu. Sindhi was added by the 21st Amendment (1967); Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali by the 71st (1992); and Bodo, Dogri, Maithili and Santhali by the 92nd (2003). The 96th Amendment (2011) changed "Oriya" to "Odia". Inclusion matters because these languages are represented in the Official Language Commission, and candidates may write some public examinations in them. Demands for more languages to be included are pending. (The official language and classical languages are covered in their own notes.)

**The Ninth Schedule: a shield and its limits.** The **1st Amendment (1951)** created it, with Article 31B, to protect land reform laws from being struck down for violating Fundamental Rights, especially the right to property. Laws placed in it were immune from judicial review on that ground. It began with 13 Acts and has grown to well over 280, including some that had little to do with land reform. In ***I.R. Coelho* (2007)** the Supreme Court held that laws added to the Ninth Schedule after **24 April 1973**, the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment, can be challenged if they violate Fundamental Rights that form part of the basic structure. The Schedule is no longer a blank shield.

**The Tenth Schedule: anti-defection.** The **52nd Amendment (1985)** added it to curb legislators switching parties for office or money, the "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" culture of the 1960s. A member is disqualified for voluntarily giving up party membership or for defying the party whip in a vote. The Presiding Officer decides questions of disqualification. The **91st Amendment (2003)** removed the exception for a "split" by one-third of a legislature party, leaving only a merger supported by two-thirds. In ***Kihoto Hollohan* (1992)** the Supreme Court upheld the Schedule but made the Presiding Officer's decisions subject to judicial review. The Parliament note covers the law and its controversies in full.

> **Mains angle:** The Schedules make good examples of how the Constitution balances competing values. The Seventh Schedule shows a federation with a strong Centre. The Fifth and Sixth Schedules show self-rule for tribal communities. The Ninth Schedule shows social reform pitted against judicial review, resolved by the basic structure doctrine. The Tenth Schedule shows party discipline pitted against a legislator's conscience. A question on any of these is best answered by citing the Schedule, the amendment that created it, and the leading judgment.
