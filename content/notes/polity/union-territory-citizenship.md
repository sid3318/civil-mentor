## 1. Union of States & Territorial Architecture (Articles 1–4)

Every sovereign republic must settle two foundational questions before designing its legislatures, courts, or charters of rights: what constitutes its geographical territory, and who belongs to its political community. The Constitution of India answers the first question in **Part I** (`Articles 1` to `4`, titled *The Union and its Territory*) and the second in **Part II** (`Articles 5` to `11`, titled *Citizenship*). Together, these eleven Articles anchor the spatial and civic boundaries of the Indian Republic.

Unlike federations born out of treaties between pre-existing sovereign colonies, independent India inherited a fractured subcontinent comprising nine British Indian Governors' Provinces, over 550 princely states of wildly varying sizes, and colonial enclaves held by France and Portugal. The framers therefore engineered a territorial architecture that guaranteed permanent national unity while retaining unmatched constitutional flexibility to redraw internal provincial borders as political, linguistic, and developmental realities matured.

```keyfacts
# Part I & Part II Constitutional Anchors at a Glance
Articles 1–4 | Part I: Union & Territory | Defines India as a 'Union of States'; empowers Parliament to admit, form, or alter states by simple majority
28 States & 8 UTs | Current Territorial Map | Reorganised from 14 States + 6 UTs (1956) through 2019 J&K Act & 2020 DNH–DD merger
Articles 5–11 | Part II: Citizenship | Identifies citizens on 26 January 1950; grants plenary legislative power to Parliament under Article 11
Citizenship Act, 1955 | Statutory Framework | Amended 9 times (1957–2019); sets 5 modes of acquisition, 3 modes of loss, Section 6A (Assam) & OCI
```

### Article 1: Name, Polity, and the Territory of India

`Article 1` serves three distinct constitutional functions across its three clauses: it specifies the official name of the country, declares the nature of its polity, and classifies the geographical extent of Indian territory. Clause (1) reads: *"India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States."* Clause (2) states that the States and the territories thereof shall be as specified in the **First Schedule**. Clause (3) defines the *territory of India* as comprising the territories of the States, the Union territories specified in the First Schedule, and such other territories as may be acquired.

#### "India, that is Bharat": Constitutional Nomenclature and Contemporary Debate

In the Constituent Assembly, naming the new republic sparked lively ideological debate on 18 September 1949. Members such as H.V. Kamath, Seth Govind Das, and Hargovind Pant pressed for traditional names like *Bharat*, *Bharatvarsha*, or *Hindustan* to lead the opening clause, arguing that *India* was a colonial legacy. Others pointed out that *India* enjoyed immediate international legal continuity in the United Nations, treaties, and diplomatic accords.

The Drafting Committee adopted a balanced synthesis — **"India, that is Bharat"** — incorporating both the modern international name and the ancient civilisational appellation on an equal constitutional footing. Neither name enjoys legal precedence over the other; both are interchangeable designations of the same sovereign Republic.

> [!news]
> **"India" vs "Bharat" Controversy (September 2023):** During the G20 Leaders' Summit in New Delhi in September 2023, official dinner invitations issued from Rashtrapati Bhavan used the title *"President of Bharat"* instead of *"President of India"*, triggering public debate over a possible formal renaming. Constitutionally, `Article 1(1)` places both designations in apposition ("India, that is Bharat"), making either title legally valid in official communication; formally deleting the word "India" from `Article 1`, however, would require a constitutional amendment under `Article 368`.

#### "Union of States" vs "Federation of States": Dr. Ambedkar's Two Reasons

A remarkable textual feature of the Indian Constitution is that the word **"federation"** appears nowhere in its text, even though its institutional structure is indisputably federal. While the Union Constitution Committee had initially drafted *"Federation of States"*, the Drafting Committee deliberately replaced it with **"Union of States"** (echoing the Canadian British North America Act, 1867, which also termed a federal polity a Union). Introducing the Draft Constitution on 4 November 1948, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar offered **two decisive reasons** for this phrasing:

1. **Not the result of an agreement among States:** Unlike the United States — where thirteen independent colonies voluntarily compacted in 1787 to form a federal union — the Indian federation was not created through a treaty or compact among pre-existing sovereign states. Provinces in British India were administrative units created by the Centre, and princely states merged completely into the Republic's sovereignty.
2. **No right of secession:** Because the federation is not a contractual compact among states, **no State has the right to secede** from the Union. The federation is a Union precisely because it is indestructible. The country may be divided into different states for administrative convenience, yet its people remain one integral whole living under a single sovereignty.

> "The Drafting Committee wanted to make it clear that though India was to be a Federation, the Federation was not the result of an agreement by the States to join in a Federation and that the Federation not being the result of an agreement no State has the right to secede from it. The Federation is a Union because it is indestructible. Though the country and the people may be divided into different States for convenience of administration the country is one integral whole, its people a single people living under a single imperium derived from a single source." — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, Vol. VII, 4 November 1948

#### "Union of India" vs "Territory of India"

Examiners routinely test the precise conceptual distinction between two expressions used in `Article 1`:

- **Union of India** is the narrower expression. It includes **only the States** (currently **28 States**), because only the States are members of the federal system and share a constitutional distribution of legislative and executive powers with the Centre under the Seventh Schedule.
- **Territory of India** is the wider expression under `Article 1(3)`. It encompasses **three categories**:
  1. **Territories of the States** (the 28 federating units);
  2. **Union Territories** specified in the First Schedule (currently **8 UTs**, administered directly by the Central Government through the President under Part VIII, `Articles 239–241`); and
  3. **Territories that may be acquired by the Government of India at any time** in the future.

Being a sovereign state, India is inherently competent to acquire foreign territory through all modes recognised by public international law: **cession** (following treaty, purchase, gift, lease, or plebiscite), **occupation** (of *terra nullius* hitherto unoccupied by a recognised ruler), or **conquest and subjugation**. India exercised this power when it integrated **Dadra and Nagar Haveli**, **Goa, Daman and Diu**, **Puducherry**, and **Sikkim** after 1950. Notably, special administrative frameworks also operate within the territory of India under **Part XXI** (`Articles 371` to `371J`, covering twelve states) and the **Fifth and Sixth Schedules** governing Scheduled and Tribal Areas.

### Article 2: Admission or Establishment of New States

`Article 2` provides: *"Parliament may by law admit into the Union, or establish, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit."* Unlike `Article 3`, which rearranges units already inside India, `Article 2` deals exclusively with **external territories that are not already part of the Union of India**. It vests Parliament with two distinct discretionary powers:

1. **Power to admit into the Union new States:** This applies to political entities that are **already in existence** as organised states or territories outside India and seek entry into the Indian Union.
2. **Power to establish new States:** This applies to territories that are **not previously organised as states** (for instance, newly acquired territories or unorganised tracts) which Parliament elevates into new states.

Crucially, the phrase *"on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit"* grants Parliament broad discretion to design transitional or special arrangements for incoming territories. In *R.C. Poudyal v. Union of India* (1993), a Constitution Bench upheld special seat reservations in the Sikkim Legislative Assembly (including the *Sangha* seat for Buddhist monasteries under `Article 371F`), clarifying that while `Article 2` confers wide latitude to impose special terms, those terms cannot destroy the **basic structure** of the Constitution.

#### The Sikkim Precedent: 35th and 36th Constitutional Amendments

The most significant historical invocation of `Article 2` — and its limits — was the integration of **Sikkim**. Until 1947, Sikkim was a princely state ruled by the hereditary **Chogyal** (monarch). After British paramountcy lapsed, Sikkim became a **protectorate of India** under the Indo-Sikkim Treaty of 1950, with India controlling its defence, external affairs, and communications while the Chogyal managed internal administration.

Following pro-democracy agitations in 1973–74, the newly elected Sikkim Assembly expressed a desire for closer association with India. Because `Article 2` only contemplated admitting full states rather than a hybrid "associate" status, Parliament enacted the **35th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1974**, inserting **`Article 2A`** and a new **Tenth Schedule** to confer the unprecedented status of an **"Associate State"** on Sikkim (complete with two parliamentary representatives).

This experiment proved short-lived. In a special referendum held in April 1975, over 97 per cent of Sikkim's electorate voted to abolish the Chogyal's monarchy and merge completely with India. Parliament accordingly passed the **36th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975** (effective **26 April 1975**), which:
- Made Sikkim the **22nd full-fledged State** of the Indian Union in the First Schedule;
- Repealed **`Article 2A`** and the **Tenth Schedule** inserted by the 35th Amendment (the Tenth Schedule remained empty until the 52nd Amendment of 1985 filled it with the Anti-Defection Law);
- Inserted **`Article 371F`** containing special constitutional safeguards for Sikkim's demographic, legislative, and land-rights protections.

### Article 3: Internal Readjustment of States and Union Territories

While `Article 2` looks outward, **`Article 3`** governs the **internal readjustment of territories of the existing constituent States and Union Territories** of India. It empowers Parliament by law to perform five distinct territorial operations:

- **`Article 3(a)`:** Form a new State by separation of territory from any State, OR by uniting two or more States or parts of States, OR by uniting any territory to a part of any State;
- **`Article 3(b)`:** Increase the area of any State;
- **`Article 3(c)`:** Diminish the area of any State;
- **`Article 3(d)`:** Alter the boundaries of any State; and
- **`Article 3(e)`:** Alter the name of any State.

```compare
# Article 2 vs Article 3 — External Entry vs Internal Reorganisation
Article 2 | Article 3
Scope :: Relates to admission or establishment of new States NOT already part of the Union of India | Relates to formation of or changes in existing States and Union Territories already part of India
Nature of Power :: External territorial accession or elevation of acquired/foreign territories | Internal political map redrawing (split, merger, area change, boundary or name alteration)
Presidential Recommendation :: Not expressly required by Article 2's text | Strictly mandatory prior Presidential recommendation before introducing the Bill in either House
State Legislature Consultation :: Not applicable (territory is outside existing States) | President must refer the Bill to affected State Legislature(s) for non-binding views within a specified time
Classic Examples :: Integration of Sikkim (35th & 36th Amendments, 1974–75) | Andhra (1953), States Reorganisation Act (1956), Telangana (2014), J&K (2019), Keralam (2026)
```

#### The Proviso and Procedural Safeguards (5th Amendment, 1955 & Babulal Parate, 1960)

The **Proviso to Article 3** imposes **two mandatory procedural conditions** before any Bill affecting an existing State can proceed:

1. **Prior recommendation of the President:** A Bill contemplating any of the five changes under `Article 3` **cannot be introduced in either House of Parliament** except on the prior recommendation of the President. Consequently, a Private Member cannot unilaterally move a state-reorganisation or state-renaming Bill without Presidential sanction.
2. **Reference to the affected State Legislature:** Before recommending the Bill, if its proposal affects the area, boundaries, or name of any existing State, the President **must refer the Bill to the Legislature of that State** for expressing its views within a period specified in the reference (or within such further period as the President may allow). Notably, the **5th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1955** amended this proviso to empower the President to fix a specific time limit for the State Legislature's response — preventing states from stalling reorganisation Bills indefinitely through inaction.

Crucially, however, this consultation is **purely recommendatory and non-binding**:
- Neither the President nor Parliament is bound by the views of the State Legislature. Parliament may accept or reject the state's opinion even if communicated within the stipulated period — as vividly illustrated during the bifurcation of Andhra Pradesh in **2014**, when Parliament enacted the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act despite the State Legislative Assembly passing a resolution rejecting the draft Bill.
- If the State Legislature fails to convey its views within the prescribed or extended time limit, the constitutional requirement stands fulfilled and the Bill may be introduced in Parliament.
- In ***Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay* (1960)**, a Constitution Bench ruled that **no fresh reference to the State Legislature is required** every time Parliament moves or adopts an amendment to the Bill during parliamentary debates, even if the amendment substantially alters the original proposal (in that case, shifting from a proposed three-unit division of Bombay to a composite bilingual Bombay State), so long as the amendment is germane to the subject matter of the referred Bill.

#### Union Territories under Article 3: The 18th Amendment (1966) and *In Re: Article 370* (2023)

Two critical **Explanations** were appended to `Article 3` by the **18th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1966** (enacted alongside the reorganisation of Punjab to clarify the creation of Chandigarh UT and transfers to Himachal Pradesh UT):

- **Explanation I:** Clarifies that in **clauses (a) to (e)** of `Article 3`, the term *"State"* **includes a Union Territory** — meaning Parliament can carve out, merge, diminish, expand, or rename Union Territories using `Article 3`. **However, in the Proviso, the word "State" does NOT include a Union Territory.** Therefore, when a reorganisation Bill affects only a Union Territory (even one with a Legislative Assembly, such as Delhi, Puducherry, or Jammu and Kashmir), the President is under **no constitutional obligation** to refer the Bill to that UT's legislature!
- **Explanation II:** Clarifies that Parliament's power under `Article 3(a)` expressly includes the power to form a new **State or Union Territory** by uniting a part of any State or Union Territory to any other State or Union Territory.

> [!case]
> ***In Re: Article 370 of the Constitution* (11 December 2023 — 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** While upholding the abrogation of Jammu and Kashmir's special status, the Supreme Court directly interpreted `Article 3` in the context of the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** (which converted a full State into two UTs — Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh — during President's Rule). The Court delivered three crucial holdings on `Article 3`:
> 1. **Carving out Ladakh UT upheld:** Reading `Article 3(a)` together with `Explanation I`, the Court affirmed that Parliament is constitutionally empowered to carve out a Union Territory (Ladakh) by separating territory from an existing State.
> 2. **Consultative role during President's Rule:** Because the J&K Assembly was dissolved under `Article 356`, Parliament could validly exercise the State Legislature's consultative function under `Article 356(1)(b)`; moreover, state views under the `Article 3` proviso are strictly recommendatory.
> 3. **Downgrading an entire State into a UT left open:** Relying on the Solicitor General's formal assurance that J&K's statehood would be restored at the earliest, the Bench **expressly declined to adjudicate** whether `Article 3` permits Parliament to extinguish the character of statehood altogether by converting an entire federating State into one or more Union Territories — observing that such a question must be tested in an appropriate future case against the principles of federalism and representative democracy.

#### "Indestructible Union of Destructible States": India vs the USA

Because Parliament can redraw the political map of India unilaterally without the consent of the affected States, the territorial integrity and continued existence of no Indian State is constitutionally guaranteed. In sharp contrast, the United States Constitution (Article IV, Section 3) prohibits Congress from forming a new state within the jurisdiction of any existing state, or by the junction of two or more states, **without the consent of the legislatures of the states concerned**. Constitutional scholars therefore summarise the divergence through a celebrated aphorism: **India is an "indestructible Union of destructible States," whereas the United States is an "indestructible Union of indestructible States."**

```compare
# Indestructible Union of Destructible States: India vs USA
India (Articles 1–4) | United States (Article IV, Sec. 3)
Origin of Federation :: Union created from above; not a compact among sovereign states | Compact forged by 13 pre-existing sovereign colonies in 1787
Right to Secede :: Explicitly barred ("Union of States" — indestructible Union) | Barred judicially after Civil War (Texas v. White, 1869)
Territorial Guarantee to States :: None — States are destructible; boundaries, area, and names alterable by Parliament | Guaranteed — States are indestructible; federal government cannot redraw maps unilaterally
Role of State Legislature :: Mandatory referral for views, but views are strictly NON-BINDING | Express consent of affected State Legislature(s) is constitutionally MANDATORY
Legislative Threshold :: Simple majority of Parliament outside Article 368 (ordinary law) | Federal legislation coupled with binding consent of concerned state legislatures
```

> [!prelims]
> **High-Yield Prelims Traps on Articles 1–3:**
> - **Union Territories & the Proviso:** Because `Explanation I` excludes UTs from the Proviso to `Article 3`, Parliament does **not** need to refer a Bill to the Delhi, Puducherry, or J&K Legislative Assembly before altering a UT's boundaries, name, or status — nor is a constitutional amendment under `Article 368` required to restore full statehood to Jammu and Kashmir (a simple law under `Articles 3` and `4` suffices).
> - **Joint Sitting Permitted:** Because an `Article 3` Bill is an ordinary Bill outside `Article 368`, a deadlock between Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha over creating or renaming a state **can be resolved through a Joint Sitting under `Article 108`**.
> - **Original Jurisdiction Bar?:** Disputes purely over the political merits of a reorganisation law do not invalidate parliamentary competence, though constitutional courts can review procedural compliance (such as whether Presidential recommendation was obtained).

### Article 4: Laws Outside Article 368 and Consequential Schedule Amendments

`Article 4` supplies the procedural engine that makes India's territorial framework extraordinarily flexible. Under **`Article 4(1)`**, any law enacted under `Article 2` or `Article 3` **shall contain such provisions for the amendment of the First Schedule** (names and territorial extents of States and UTs) and the **Fourth Schedule** (allocation of seats in the Rajya Sabha) as may be necessary to give effect to the law, along with supplemental, incidental, and consequential provisions — such as adjusting representation in the Lok Sabha and State Legislatures, bifurcating High Courts, dividing state assets and liabilities, and allocating All-India Services cadres.

Under **`Article 4(2)`**, *"No such law as aforesaid shall be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of Article 368."* This single sentence carries immense practical consequences:
- Laws admitting, establishing, bifurcating, merging, or renaming states require **only a simple majority** (a majority of members present and voting in each House of Parliament) — the exact threshold used for ordinary statutes.
- They bypass both the **special majority** (`Article 368(2)`) and **ratification by half the State Legislatures** ordinarily needed for amendments affecting federal features like the Fourth Schedule.
- The President's assent follows the ordinary legislative pathway under `Article 111`, rather than the mandatory assent rule inserted into `Article 368` by the 24th Amendment.

```flow
# Legislative & Executive Pathways for Territorial Alteration in India
What kind of territorial change is proposed? | Choose the constitutional route based on whether territory stays within India or crosses international borders
Internal Reorganisation (Art. 3) | Split, merge, resize, or rename existing States/UTs || Cession of Indian Territory (Art. 368) | Transfer de jure Indian land to a foreign state (Berubari, 1960) || Boundary Dispute Settlement (Art. 73) | Demarcate uncertain border with no de jure cession (Maganbhai, 1969)
President's Prior Recommendation & Referral | Mandatory referral to affected State Legislature (non-binding views; not required for UTs) || Constitutional Amendment Bill (Art. 368) | Introduced in either House; no Presidential recommendation or state referral needed || Executive Action by Union Government | Implemented by diplomatic agreement / tribunal award via Art. 73 executive power
Simple Majority in Both Houses (Art. 4) | Ordinary legislative process; Joint Sitting (Art. 108) allowed; amends 1st & 4th Schedules || Special Majority in Each House (Art. 368) | >50% total membership + 2/3 present & voting; NO Joint Sitting; amends 1st Schedule || No Constitutional Amendment Required | Parliamentary statute needed ONLY if private citizen rights or domestic laws are altered
```

## 2. Cession of Territory & Border Settlements

Does Parliament's power under `Article 3(c)` to *"diminish the area of any State"* also empower it to **cede Indian territory to a foreign country** by simple majority? This profound constitutional question tested the outer limits of Part I within the very first decade of the Republic and produced a crisp three-part constitutional jurisprudence distinguishing internal reorganisation, foreign territorial cession, and international boundary settlement.

### Can Parliament Cede Indian Territory under Article 3? The *Berubari Union* Reference (1960)

The controversy originated from a flawed border demarcation during Partition. When Sir Cyril Radcliffe drew the boundary between West Bengal and East Pakistan in 1947, **Berubari Union No. 12** — a territory of roughly 8.75 square miles in Jalpaiguri district of West Bengal — fell inside India on the map, though Pakistan soon disputed the interpretation of the Radcliffe Award. Simultaneously, India and East Pakistan inherited a chaotic mosaic of **Cooch-Behar enclaves** (*chhitmahals*) — tiny pockets of one country entirely surrounded by the territory of the other.

To resolve these festering border irritants, Prime Ministers Jawaharlal Nehru and Feroz Khan Noon signed the **Nehru–Noon Agreement on 10 September 1958**, agreeing to divide Berubari Union No. 12 roughly in half between India and Pakistan and to exchange the Cooch-Behar enclaves without compensation for extra area. Facing fierce opposition in West Bengal, President Dr. Rajendra Prasad invoked the Supreme Court's advisory jurisdiction under **`Article 143(1)`** to clarify whether the agreement could be implemented by executive action, by an ordinary law under `Article 3`, or only by a formal constitutional amendment under `Article 368`.

In ***In Re: The Berubari Union and Exchange of Enclaves* (1960)**, an **8-Judge Constitution Bench** unanimously laid down a landmark constitutional rule:
- **No cession by executive action alone:** While the Union executive holds treaty-making power under `Article 73`, it cannot unilaterally alienate sovereign national territory specified in the First Schedule.
- **`Article 3(c)` does NOT permit foreign cession:** The power to *"diminish the area of any State"* operates **strictly internally** — where territory carved out of one Indian State is merged into another Indian State or converted into a Union Territory, keeping the total territory of India intact. It does not authorise shrinking the sovereign boundaries of India itself.
- **Cession requires an `Article 368` amendment:** Because ceding territory reduces the sovereign territory specified in `Article 1` and the First Schedule, Parliament can validly implement a territorial cession treaty **only by enacting a Constitutional Amendment under `Article 368`** (by special majority).

Acting on the Supreme Court's opinion, Parliament enacted the **9th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1960** to amend the First Schedule and authorise the transfer of southern Berubari and enclave exchanges with Pakistan. However, subsequent litigation and deteriorating Indo-Pakistan relations stalled full operationalisation of the Berubari transfer, and after Bangladesh's liberation in 1971, the issue merged into a broader Indo-Bangladesh border framework (with India retaining South Berubari in exchange for leasing the **Tin Bigha Corridor** to Bangladesh).

### Boundary Settlement vs Territorial Cession: *Ram Kishore Sen* (1966) and *Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel* (1969)

Within a decade of *Berubari*, two further Supreme Court verdicts refined the constitutional boundary between true *cession* and mere *border demarcation*:

1. ***Ram Kishore Sen v. Union of India* (1966):** Following the 9th Amendment, petitioners challenged the transfer of a portion of Chilahati village (Jalpaiguri) that India had administered de facto since 1947 despite the Radcliffe Award placing it in East Pakistan. The Supreme Court clarified that handing over territory which legally belonged to Pakistan under the original Partition award — and over which India held only **adverse possession** rather than *de jure* constitutional title — did not constitute cession of Indian territory requiring an additional `Article 368` amendment.
2. ***Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v. Union of India* (1969):** After armed border clashes in the **Rann of Kutch** in 1965, India and Pakistan referred their disputed Gujarat–West Pakistan boundary to an international arbitration tribunal (**Indo-Pakistan Western Boundary Case Tribunal**). On 19 February 1968, the Tribunal awarded roughly 350 square miles (three sectors and two inlets in the Rann of Kutch) to Pakistan while upholding India's claim to roughly 90 per cent of the disputed marshland. Petitioners moved the Supreme Court under `Article 32`, arguing that implementing the award amounted to territorial cession requiring an `Article 368` amendment under *Berubari*.

A **5-Judge Constitution Bench** headed by Chief Justice M. Hidayatullah dismissed the petitions and drew a sharp, enduring legal distinction: **the settlement of a boundary dispute between India and a neighbouring country does NOT involve cession of territory** when the boundary was historically uncertain and the settlement merely ascertains and demarcates where the true line runs. Accordingly, **no Constitutional Amendment under `Article 368` is required** — such a boundary settlement can be implemented **purely by executive action** under `Article 73`, unless implementing the award modifies domestic statutes or infringes the private legal rights of Indian citizens (in which case ordinary legislation suffices).

### The 100th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2015: India–Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement

The constitutional doctrine of *Berubari* found its most monumental modern application in the **100th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2015**, which finally resolved a 68-year-old territorial anomaly inherited from the 1947 Partition.

Along the India–Bangladesh border lay **162 enclaves (*chhitmahals*)** — territories wholly surrounded by the neighbouring nation — along with **6.1 kilometres of undemarcated border** and several pockets held in **adverse possession** (territories administratively controlled by one country though legally belonging to the other). Specifically, **111 Indian enclaves** (covering ~17,160 acres) lay deep inside Bangladesh, while **51 Bangladeshi enclaves** (covering ~7,110 acres) lay inside West Bengal, India. Roughly **51,000 residents** of these enclaves lived in virtual statelessness for decades, cut off from electricity, schools, hospitals, courts, land registries, and voting booths because neither country's officials could legally cross foreign soil to govern them.

Although Prime Ministers Indira Gandhi and Sheikh Mujibur Rahman had signed the **Land Boundary Agreement (LBA) on 16 May 1974**, it remained unratified by India for four decades due to unresolved demarcations and political opposition. In **September 2011**, India and Bangladesh signed a comprehensive **Protocol to the 1974 LBA** setting out a clean, permanent exchange. Because transferring 111 enclaves against 51 involved a net cession of roughly 10,000 acres of *de jure* Indian territory, *Berubari* made an `Article 368` amendment mandatory.

Passed unanimously by both Houses of Parliament in May 2015 (and brought into force upon exchange of instruments of ratification on **31 July 2015**, with the enclave swap taking physical effect at midnight on **31 July–1 August 2015**), the **100th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2015**:
- **Amended the First Schedule** of the Constitution with respect to the territories of **four Indian States: Assam, West Bengal, Meghalaya, and Tripura** (note: Mizoram was NOT among the four states amended by the 100th Amendment — a frequent Prelims trap!);
- Transferred **111 Indian enclaves** to Bangladesh and acquired **51 Bangladeshi enclaves** into India;
- Regularised all areas in **adverse possession** on an "as-is-where-is" basis and completed demarcation of the remaining 6.1 km of border;
- Granted enclave residents the **humanitarian right to choose their citizenship**: while the overwhelming majority opted to become citizens of the surrounding country where their homes and farms stood, **922 residents** of former Indian enclaves inside Bangladesh chose to retain Indian citizenship and were relocated and rehabilitated by the Government of India in Cooch Behar district, West Bengal.

> [!remember]
> **Three Territorial Rules — Never Confuse Them in Prelims!**
> 1. **Internal redrawing of States/UTs** (`Art. 3`) → **Ordinary Law by Simple Majority** (`Art. 4`, outside `Art. 368`).
> 2. **Ceding *de jure* Indian territory to a foreign state** (*Berubari*, 1960) → **Constitutional Amendment by Special Majority** under `Art. 368` (examples: **9th Amendment 1960**; **100th Amendment 2015**).
> 3. **Settling a disputed international boundary with no cession of *de jure* territory** (*Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel*, 1969) → **Executive Action alone** (`Art. 73`) — neither `Art. 368` amendment nor parliamentary law needed!

> [!mains]
> **Strategic & Federal Value-Addition (100th Amendment):** In GS-2 answers on *India and its Neighbourhood* or *Cooperative Federalism*, cite the 100th Amendment as a textbook model on three counts: (1) **Human Rights & Statelessness**, ending the legal limbo of over 50,000 *chhitmahal* residents; (2) **Border Security**, rationalising a porous, zigzagging frontier notorious for smuggling and illegal migration; and (3) **Cooperative Federalism**, demonstrating how Centre–State consensus-building with West Bengal, Assam, Meghalaya, and Tripura enabled unanimous parliamentary passage across party lines.

## 3. Evolution & Reorganisation of States and Union Territories (1947–2026)

India's contemporary map of **28 States and 8 Union Territories** is the product of nearly eight decades of constitutional statecraft, mass linguistic movements, security imperatives in borderlands, and post-2000 developmental aspirations. Understanding this evolution requires tracking four consecutive phases: the integration of princely states (1947–49), the original four-fold classification (1950), the linguistic reorganisation milestone (1953–56), and the continuous territorial adjustments from 1960 to 2026.

### Integration of Princely States: Sardar Patel, V.P. Menon, and the Three Holdouts

At the dawn of independence, the political geography of undivided India consisted of two sharply distinct categories: **British Indian Provinces** (directly governed by the British Crown) and **565 Princely States** (covering roughly 40 per cent of the land area and nearly a quarter of the population, ruled by hereditary monarchs under British *paramountcy*). Section 7 of the **Indian Independence Act, 1947** formally declared the lapse of British paramountcy, leaving princely rulers with three theoretical options: accede to India, accede to Pakistan, or declare independence — a formula that threatened the "Balkanisation" of the subcontinent.

Of the **552 princely states** situated within the geographical boundaries of India, **549** acceded peacefully before 15 August 1947 through the tireless diplomacy of the newly established **States Department** (June 1947), headed by Deputy Prime Minister **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** as Minister and **V.P. Menon** as Secretary. Patel and Menon combined patriotic appeal, strategic pragmatism, and firm leverage:
- Rulers initially signed an **Instrument of Accession** surrendering only **three core subjects** — **Defence, External Affairs, and Communications** — alongside a **Standstill Agreement** preserving administrative continuity;
- In return, rulers were assured personal titles, private properties, and tax-free annual **Privy Purses** (constitutionalised in `Article 291`, later abolished by the **26th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971**);
- Subsequently, smaller states were consolidated into viable provincial unions (such as Saurashtra, Madhya Bharat, Rajasthan, Travancore-Cochin, and PEPSU) or merged into adjoining Governors' Provinces through Instruments of Merger.

Three recalcitrant princely states resisted accession before Independence Day and had to be integrated through distinct political, military, and diplomatic means:

1. **Junagadh (Referendum / Plebiscite, February 1948):** Located on the Kathiawar coast of Gujarat with no land contiguity to Pakistan, Junagadh had an overwhelming Hindu majority (over 80 per cent) ruled by a Muslim Nawab, **Mahabat Khan III**, who announced accession to Pakistan on 15 August 1947. Following popular protests, the formation of a provisional government (*Arzi Hukumat* led by Samaldas Gandhi), and the Nawab's flight to Karachi, the Dewan (Shah Nawaz Bhutto) invited India to assume administration. India held a **plebiscite in February 1948**, in which over 99 per cent of voters opted for integration with India.
2. **Hyderabad (Police Action — Operation Polo, September 1948):** India's largest and wealthiest princely state, entirely landlocked in the Deccan with an ~85 per cent Hindu population, was ruled by Nizam **Mir Osman Ali Khan**, who aspired to independent status. Although Hyderabad signed a one-year Standstill Agreement in November 1947, armed militia called the **Razakars** (led by Kasim Razvi) unleashed communal violence and border raids while the Nizam sought international backing. Between **13 and 17 September 1948**, the Indian Army executed **"Operation Polo"** (officially termed a *Police Action* under Major General J.N. Chaudhuri), swiftly securing the Nizam's surrender and integrating Hyderabad into the Indian Union.
3. **Jammu and Kashmir (Instrument of Accession, October 1947):** A Muslim-majority border state ruled by Hindu Dogra monarch **Maharaja Hari Singh**, J&K initially sought independence and executed a Standstill Agreement with Pakistan (India did not sign one). On **22 October 1947**, Pakistan-backed tribal raiders invaded Kashmir, advancing rapidly toward Srinagar. Appealing urgently for Indian military assistance, Maharaja Hari Singh signed the **Instrument of Accession on 26 October 1947**, legally acceding Jammu and Kashmir to India on the exact same terms as all other princely states, whereupon Indian troops were airlifted to defend Srinagar. The state subsequently received temporary special provisions under **`Article 370`** until its abrogation in August 2019.

### The Original Four-Fold Classification of States (1950)

Because the merger of princely states had only just concluded, the Constitution adopted in November 1949 treated the existing territorial map as a **temporary arrangement**, dividing India's constituent units in the **First Schedule** into **four distinct categories — Parts A, B, C, and D**. As enacted on 26 November 1949, the First Schedule listed **29 units** (9 Part A, 9 Part B, 10 Part C, and 1 Part D). Immediately prior to commencement on 26 January 1950, **Cooch-Behar** was merged into West Bengal (1 January 1950) and **Vindhya Pradesh** was shifted from Part B to Part C via the Constitution (Amendment of the First and Fourth Schedules) Order, 1950, leaving **28 units** on Republic Day (and later Bilaspur merged into Himachal Pradesh in 1954).

### Linguistic Provinces Commissions and the Road to 1956

The four-fold classification was widely recognised as an awkward colonial-princely patchwork — for instance, Madras Presidency lumped together Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, and Kannada speakers, while Hyderabad split Telugu, Marathi, and Kannada populations across administrative borders. Even before independence, the Indian National Congress at its **Nagpur Session (1920)** had reorganised its own Pradesh Congress Committees along linguistic lines, and the **Nehru Report (1928)** had endorsed linguistic provinces. Yet the trauma of Partition in 1947 filled national leaders with deep apprehension that redrawing boundaries on language might unleash fresh centrifugal and secessionist forces.

#### 1. S.K. Dhar Commission (June–December 1948)

In June 1948, Constituent Assembly President Dr. Rajendra Prasad appointed the **Linguistic Provinces Commission** chaired by retired Allahabad High Court Judge **S.K. Dhar** (with J.N. Lal and Panna Lall) to examine the demand for linguistic states, especially in South India. Submitting its report in December 1948, the Dhar Commission **emphatically rejected language** as the criterion for state reorganisation, recommending instead **administrative convenience, geographical contiguity, financial self-reliance, and national unity**.

#### 2. JVP Committee (December 1948 – April 1949)

The Dhar Commission's report triggered widespread disappointment, especially across Andhra and Maharashtra. To review the question politically, the Congress Party at its Jaipur Session (December 1948) appointed a three-member **Linguistic Provinces Committee** — popularly called the **JVP Committee** after its members: **Jawaharlal Nehru**, **Vallabhbhai Patel**, and **Pattabhi Sitaramayya** (no formal chairman). Reporting in April 1949, the JVP Committee **formally rejected language as the primary basis for reorganisation**, stressing that India's top priorities were national security, unity, and economic consolidation. Crucially, however, it left a small door ajar: if public sentiment in any region proved *"insistent and overwhelming"*, democratic statesmanship would have to reconsider the issue.

#### 3. Potti Sriramulu and the Creation of Andhra State (October 1953)

That overwhelming public pressure erupted first in the Telugu-speaking districts of Madras State. On 19 October 1952, veteran Gandhian activist **Potti Sriramulu** launched an indefinite fast demanding a separate Andhra State. After a **56-day hunger strike**, Sriramulu died on **15 December 1952**, sparking spontaneous mass demonstrations and violence across coastal Andhra and Rayalaseema. Within four days, Prime Minister Nehru announced the creation of a separate Telugu state. On **1 October 1953**, **Andhra State** (with Kurnool as its capital) was formally inaugurated — becoming **India's first linguistic state**, carved out by separating the Telugu-speaking areas from Madras State (while Bellary district went to Mysore).

#### 4. Fazl Ali Commission / States Reorganisation Commission (December 1953 – September 1955)

The birth of Andhra opened the floodgates of linguistic demands across Maharashtra, Gujarat, Karnataka, Kerala, and Punjab. In December 1953, the Government of India appointed a three-member **States Reorganisation Commission (SRC)** chaired by **Justice Fazl Ali**, with **K.M. Panikkar** and **H.N. Kunzru** as members. After travelling across India and examining over 1.5 lakh memoranda, the Commission submitted its landmark report in **September 1955**.

The Fazl Ali Commission struck a masterly constitutional balance: it **broadly accepted language as a rational basis for state reorganisation**, yet **explicitly rejected the rigid doctrine of "one language–one state"** (retaining multiple Hindi-speaking states and rejecting a purely linguistic split of Bombay and Punjab at that time). It articulated **four overarching criteria** that must govern any territorial redrawing:
1. **Preservation and strengthening of the unity and security of the country** (paramount consideration);
2. **Linguistic and cultural homogeneity** (to facilitate grassroots education and administration);
3. **Financial, economic, and administrative considerations** (viability of each unit); and
4. **Planning and promotion of the welfare of the people** in each state as well as of the nation as a whole.

The Commission recommended abolishing the colonial Part A/B/C/D hierarchy and reorganising India into **16 States and 3 centrally administered territories**, alongside safeguards for linguistic minorities (such as primary education in the mother tongue and a Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities — later enacted as `Articles 350A` and `350B`!).

#### 5. States Reorganisation Act, 1956 & 7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956

Accepting the Fazl Ali Commission's blueprint with selective modifications (notably keeping Bombay bilingual temporarily and creating more UTs), Parliament enacted the **States Reorganisation Act, 1956** alongside the **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956**, both entering into force on **1 November 1956**. Together they transformed India's internal constitutional architecture:
- **Abolished the four-fold classification** (Parts A, B, C, and D), repealing **Part VII (`Article 238`)** and abolishing the princely office of **Rajpramukh**;
- Established a clean **two-fold structure**: **14 States and 6 Union Territories**:
  - **14 States:** Andhra Pradesh (merging Andhra State with the Telugu-speaking Telangana region of Hyderabad), Assam, Bihar, Bombay (enlarged with Saurashtra, Kutch, Marathwada, and Vidarbha), Jammu & Kashmir, Kerala (merging Travancore-Cochin with Malabar & Kasaragod), Madhya Pradesh (merging Madhya Bharat, Vindhya Pradesh, and Bhopal), Madras, Mysore (expanded to unite Kannada-speaking areas), Orissa, Punjab (merging PEPSU), Rajasthan (merging Ajmer), Uttar Pradesh, and West Bengal (receiving Purulia and Islampur from Bihar).
  - **6 Union Territories:** Andaman & Nicobar Islands, Delhi, Himachal Pradesh, Laccadive, Minicoy & Amindivi Islands, Manipur, and Tripura.
- Created **five statutory Zonal Councils** (Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, and Southern) under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 to promote inter-state cooperation and soften linguistic parochialism (a sixth, the North-Eastern Council, was later added by a separate 1971 Act).
- Inserted `Articles 350A` (primary instruction in mother tongue for linguistic minorities) and `350B` (Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities) and permitted a **common Governor** (`Art. 153` proviso) and **common High Court** (`Art. 231`) for two or more States.

### Mains Debate: Linguistic Federalism, Smaller States, and Contemporary Statehood Demands

For UPSC Mains (GS-1 Post-Independence Consolidation and GS-2 Indian Polity), the reorganisation of states is evaluated not merely as historical chronology, but as a living laboratory of **holding-together federalism**.

#### Assessing Linguistic Reorganisation (1956): Unity Strengthened, Not Fractured

Contrary to the post-Partition fears of the Dhar Commission and JVP Committee, linguistic reorganisation proved one of the most stabilising political choices in independent India's history. Historians such as Ramachandra Guha point out that by accommodating linguistic identities within federal statehood rather than suppressing them (as Pakistan did in East Bengal in 1971, or Sri Lanka did with Tamil), India defused secessionism before it could turn violent. Administration, courts, and schooling in vernacular languages dramatically broadened democratic participation, allowing non-English-speaking agrarian and backward classes to enter state politics.

At the same time, linguistic boundaries produced persistent frictions: **inter-state border disputes** (such as Maharashtra–Karnataka over Belagavi, or Assam–Mizoram and Assam–Meghalaya boundary clashes), **inter-state river water disputes** (Cauvery, Krishna, Mahadayi), protection of linguistic minorities inside majoritarian states, and periodic *"sons-of-the-soil"* nativism in regional employment.

> [!debate]
> **Smaller States vs Larger States: Does Bifurcation Improve Governance?**
> - **Arguments for Smaller States:** Bring administration physically closer to remote citizens; break the neglect of backward sub-regions overshadowed by dominant political elites (e.g., Uttarakhand and Chhattisgarh achieved rapid per-capita GSDP growth post-2000; Telangana surged in IT, agriculture, and welfare delivery after 2014); improve legislative accountability and police-to-population ratios.
> - **Arguments against Unchecked Bifurcation:** Smaller states frequently suffer fiscal fragility, narrow tax bases, and heavy dependence on Finance Commission transfers; splitting states triggers massive one-time capital construction costs (e.g., Amaravati after Andhra's bifurcation), multiplies inter-state river and asset disputes, and does not automatically cure governance deficits if institutional capacity and anti-corruption safeguards remain weak (e.g., political instability and uneven human development indicators in Jharkhand).

#### Contemporary Statehood and Autonomy Demands (2024–2026)

Since 2000, territorial mobilisation has shifted decisively from *language* to **relative economic underdevelopment, tribal land security, and democratic self-rule**:

- **Ladakh (Statehood & Sixth Schedule Demand, 2024–2026):** Although Leh initially welcomed UT status in 2019, fears over loss of legislative voice, land protection, jobs, and fragile Himalayan ecology united the **Leh Apex Body (LAB)** and **Kargil Democratic Alliance (KDA)** around a four-point agenda: (1) full Statehood; (2) inclusion under the **Sixth Schedule** (given Ladakh's >90 per cent Scheduled Tribe population); (3) a dedicated Public Service Commission; and (4) two Lok Sabha seats (one each for Leh and Kargil).
  - Following protracted talks with the MHA **High-Powered Committee (HPC)** chaired by MoS Home Nityanand Rai — and turbulent September 2025 protests in Leh that led to the National Security Act detention of climate activist Sonam Wangchuk (subsequently revoked by the Union Government after nearly six months) — the Centre formally ruled out both full statehood and Sixth Schedule status in 2026, proposing instead an empowered *sui generis* elected territorial body with legislative, executive, and financial powers via special constitutional provision (such as under `Article 371`), which remains under negotiation with local leadership.
- **Vidarbha (Maharashtra):** Grounded in persistent agrarian distress, farmer suicides, irrigation backlogs, and the unfulfilled promises of the **Nagpur Pact (1953)**, despite statutory regional development boards under `Article 371(2)`.
- **Gorkhaland (West Bengal):** Anchored in the distinct linguistic and ethnic identity of Nepali-speaking Gorkhas in Darjeeling, Kalimpong, and the Dooars; currently addressed through the semi-autonomous **Gorkhaland Territorial Administration (GTA)**.
- **Bodoland (Assam):** A notable illustration of **asymmetrical autonomy substituting for bifurcation** — the **Third Bodo Peace Accord (January 2020)** upgraded the Bodoland Territorial Council into the expanded **Bodoland Territorial Region (BTR)** within the Sixth Schedule, channelling militancy into constitutional sub-state governance without further partitioning Assam.
- **Other Regional Movements:** Proposals for a four-way split of Uttar Pradesh (*Purvanchal, Bundelkhand, Awadh Pradesh, Harit Pradesh* — endorsed by a 2011 UP Assembly resolution), *Saurashtra* (Gujarat), *Kongu Nadu* (Tamil Nadu), and *Greater Tipraland* (Tripura).

**Balanced Mains Way Forward:** Rather than reacting piecemeal to agitational politics or electoral cycles, India needs a **Second States Reorganisation Commission** mandated to frame objective, transparent criteria — demographic scale, fiscal sustainability, ecological carrying capacity, and administrative viability. Equally vital, however, is deepening **sub-state asymmetrical federalism** through empowered Fifth/Sixth Schedule councils, effective `Article 371` regional development boards, genuine fiscal devolution to Panchayats and Municipalities (`73rd` & `74th` Amendments), and targeted infrastructure investment in backward districts — proving that good governance does not always require drawing new borders.

## 4. Constitutional Provisions on Citizenship (Part II, Articles 5–11)

While Part I defines the physical body of the Republic, **Part II** (`Articles 5` to `11`) defines its political soul: the **citizens** who constitute *"We, the People of India."* In modern constitutional theory, citizenship is not merely a passport status; as political theorist Hannah Arendt memorably described it, citizenship is *"the right to have rights"* — the legal bond conferring full civic equality, political participation, and reciprocal duties toward the state.

### Meaning and Constitutional Significance: Citizens vs Aliens

Like any modern state, India distinguishes between two categories of inhabitants within its territory: **citizens** and **aliens** (nationals of foreign states). Citizens are full members of the Indian polity who owe permanent allegiance to the Republic and enjoy all civil and political rights. Aliens are further divided into two legally distinct classes:

- **Friendly Aliens:** Subjects of countries that maintain cordial diplomatic relations with India. They enjoy basic civil and human rights (such as `Articles 14, 20, 21, 21A, 22, 23, 24, 25–28`) but lack political rights.
- **Enemy Aliens:** Subjects of countries that are at war with India. They enjoy a narrower set of protections — notably, under **`Article 22(3)(a)`**, enemy aliens are expressly denied protection against arbitrary arrest and preventive detention safeguards guaranteed under `Article 22(1)` and `(2)`.

#### Rights, Privileges, and Constitutional Offices Reserved Exclusively for Citizens

A perennial UPSC Prelims staple is distinguishing entitlements available to **all persons** (citizens and foreigners alike) from those reserved **exclusively for citizens of India**:

1. **Five Citizen-Only Fundamental Rights:**
   - **`Article 15`:** Prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth;
   - **`Article 16`:** Equality of opportunity in matters of public employment;
   - **`Article 19`:** Six democratic freedoms (speech and expression, assembly, association, movement, residence, and profession);
   - **`Article 29`:** Protection of distinct language, script, and culture of minorities;
   - **`Article 30`:** Right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions.
2. **Electoral and Political Rights:**
   - **Right to vote** in elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies (`Article 326`);
   - **Right to contest elections** for membership of Parliament (`Article 84`) and State Legislatures (`Article 173`).
3. **Exclusive Eligibility for High Constitutional Offices:**
   - **President of India** (`Article 58`);
   - **Vice-President of India** (`Article 66`);
   - **Judges of the Supreme Court** (`Article 124`) and **High Courts** (`Article 217`);
   - **Governor of a State** (`Article 157`);
   - **Attorney General of India** (`Article 76`); and
   - **Advocate General of a State** (`Article 165`).
4. **Correlative Civic Duties:** Citizens alone are bound by the **Fundamental Duties** enumerated in **`Article 51A`** (Part IVA), alongside legal obligations to pay taxes, uphold constitutional integrity, and defend the nation when called upon.

> [!prelims]
> **Two Classic UPSC Traps on Citizens vs Aliens & Legal Persons:**
> 1. **Naturalised Citizen as President (India vs USA):** In **India**, *both* a **citizen by birth** and a **naturalised citizen** are constitutionally eligible for the office of President (`Article 58` requires only that the candidate be a "citizen of India"). In the **United States**, by contrast, Article II restricts the Presidency exclusively to a **natural-born citizen** — a naturalised citizen can never become US President!
> 2. **Corporations Are NOT Citizens:** In ***State Trading Corporation of India Ltd. v. Commercial Tax Officer* (1963)**, a 9-Judge Bench held that **juristic/legal persons such as companies and corporations are NOT "citizens"** under Part II or the Citizenship Act, 1955, and therefore cannot directly invoke citizen-only rights like `Article 19` (though in *R.C. Cooper*, 1970 and *Bennett Coleman*, 1973, the Court permitted Indian citizen-shareholders to challenge state action that directly infringes their own rights alongside the company's).

### Clause-by-Clause Architecture of Articles 5 to 11

Two structural facts frame all of Part II:
- **Early Commencement:** Under `Article 394`, **`Articles 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9` came into force immediately on 26 November 1949** (two months before Republic Day) so that electoral rolls and citizenship transitions following Partition could operate smoothly.
- **Strictly Transitional Scope:** The Constituent Assembly — overwhelmed by the unprecedented cross-border migrations of Partition — deliberately refrained from drafting a permanent citizenship code inside the Constitution. **`Articles 5` to `8` identify ONLY the four categories of persons who became Indian citizens at the commencement of the Constitution (26 January 1950).** All permanent rules governing post-1950 acquisition, termination, and loss were entrusted to Parliament under `Article 11`.

| Article | Subject Matter | Exact Constitutional Rule & Cut-off Date |
|---|---|---|
| **`Article 5`** | Citizenship by Domicile | Domiciled in India on 26 Jan 1950 **PLUS** (a) born in India, OR (b) either parent born in India, OR (c) ordinarily resident in India for **≥ 5 years** immediately preceding commencement |
| **`Article 6`** | Migrants **from Pakistan** to India | Person, parent, or grandparent born in undivided India **PLUS**: if migrated **before 19 July 1948** → ordinarily resident in India since migration; if **on or after 19 July 1948** → **6 months' residence** + registration by appointed officer |
| **`Article 7`** | Migrants **to Pakistan** who returned | Migrated to Pakistan **after 1 March 1947** → NOT a citizen. **Proviso:** If returned under a **permit for resettlement or permanent return**, treated under `Art. 6(b)` (6 months' residence + registration) |
| **`Article 8`** | Persons of Indian Origin abroad | Person, parent, or grandparent born in undivided India, ordinarily residing outside India → **registered** by Indian diplomatic/consular representative abroad |
| **`Article 9`** | Voluntary foreign citizenship | Voluntarily acquiring citizenship of any foreign state **automatically disqualifies** a person from being an Indian citizen under Arts. 5, 6, or 8 |
| **`Article 10`** | Continuance of citizenship | Every person who is or is deemed a citizen continues to be so, **subject to any law made by Parliament** |
| **`Article 11`** | Parliamentary regulation | **Plenary power** of Parliament to make any provision regarding acquisition, termination, and all other matters relating to citizenship (overrides prior provisions in Part II) |

#### Article 5: Citizenship by Domicile at Commencement

`Article 5` conferred citizenship on every person who had their **domicile in the territory of India** at the commencement of the Constitution AND fulfilled **any one** of three connecting conditions: (a) was born in the territory of India; OR (b) either of whose parents was born in the territory of India; OR (c) had been ordinarily resident in the territory of India for **not less than five years** immediately preceding such commencement.

In law, **domicile** is distinct from mere residence: it requires two concurrent elements — **factum** (actual physical residence) and **animus manendi** (the settled intention to remain permanently or indefinitely in that country). In ***Pradeep Jain v. Union of India* (1984)**, the Supreme Court clarified a vital constitutional principle: unlike the United States, **India recognises only ONE domicile — the "domicile of India"**; there is no separate constitutional concept of "state domicile" (even though states loosely use the term "domicile" for residential eligibility in education or employment).

#### Article 6: Rights of Citizenship of Persons Who Migrated from Pakistan

Drafted specifically for Partition refugees streaming across the newly created Radcliffe border into India, `Article 6` applies to persons who migrated to India from territories now included in Pakistan, provided that **the person, or either parent, or any grandparent was born in undivided India** (as defined in the Government of India Act, 1935). It splits such migrants along a single pivotal administrative date — **19 July 1948**, the date on which the Dominion of India introduced the **permit system** to regulate cross-border movement from West Pakistan:

- **Pre-Permit Migrants (`Article 6(b)(i)` — before 19 July 1948):** Automatically deemed citizens of India at commencement if they had been **ordinarily resident in India** since the date of their migration. No registration formality was required.
- **Post-Permit Migrants (`Article 6(b)(ii)` — on or after 19 July 1948):** Could become citizens only if they had been **resident in India for at least six months** immediately preceding the date of application and were formally **registered** as citizens by an officer appointed by the Government of the Dominion of India before commencement.

#### Article 7: Rights of Citizenship of Certain Migrants to Pakistan (Returnees)

Opening with a sweeping *non-obstante* clause (*"Notwithstanding anything in Articles 5 and 6"*), `Article 7` addressed people who initially moved from India to Pakistan during the upheaval preceding Partition. Any person who migrated to Pakistan **after 1 March 1947** (chosen because communal violence and cross-border movement intensified in March 1947) was **disqualified from Indian citizenship**, even if they had previously satisfied `Article 5` or `Article 6`.

However, the **Proviso to Article 7** carved out a compassionate legal exception for returnees: if such a person returned to India under a valid **permit for resettlement or permanent return** issued by or under the authority of any law, they were legally deemed to have migrated to India **after 19 July 1948** for the purposes of `Article 6(b)` — enabling them to acquire Indian citizenship after **six months' residence** and official registration.

#### Article 8: Persons of Indian Origin Residing Outside India

To ensure that members of the historic Indian diaspora living overseas (for instance, in East Africa, Malaya, Fiji, or Europe) were not cut adrift at independence, `Article 8` conferred citizenship on any person who — or either of whose parents or any of whose grandparents — was born in undivided India (as defined in the GoI Act, 1935), and who was ordinarily residing in any country outside undivided India, provided they got **registered as a citizen of India by the diplomatic or consular representative of India** in their country of residence (either before or after 26 January 1950).

#### Articles 9, 10, and 11: Foreign Citizenship, Continuance, and Parliamentary Supremacy

The closing trio of Part II establishes single citizenship, shields citizens from arbitrary executive stripping, and empowers Parliament for the future:

- **`Article 9` (No Dual Citizenship):** Expressly bars dual nationality at commencement — *"No person shall be a citizen of India by virtue of Article 5, or be deemed to be a citizen of India by virtue of Article 6 or Article 8, if he has voluntarily acquired the citizenship of any foreign State."* Notably, the Supreme Court held in ***State of Uttar Pradesh v. Shah Mohammad* (1969)** that `Article 9` applies only to foreign citizenship acquired **before 26 January 1950**; voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship *after* commencement is governed by **Section 9 of the Citizenship Act, 1955**, where the Central Government alone determines the disputed fact of voluntary acquisition.
- **`Article 10` (Continuance of Citizenship Rights):** Guarantees that every citizen shall continue to enjoy citizenship status **subject to the provisions of any law made by Parliament**. This embeds a crucial rule-of-law safeguard: citizenship cannot be extinguished by an unguided executive order; only a parliamentary statute can alter or terminate it.
- **`Article 11` (Plenary Power of Parliament):** Declares that *"Nothing in the foregoing provisions of this Part shall derogate from the power of Parliament to make any provision with respect to the acquisition and termination of citizenship and all other matters relating to citizenship."* This clause renders Parliament's legislative authority over citizenship **plenary** (subject, of course, to Fundamental Rights and the Basic Structure) — empowering it even to override commencement-era cut-off dates when creating tailored statutory regimes like Section 6A for Assam.

## 5. Citizenship Act, 1955: Acquisition, Loss & Section 6A (Assam)

Exercising its plenary mandate under `Article 11`, Parliament enacted the **Citizenship Act, 1955** (`Act No. 57 of 1955`), which serves as India's comprehensive statutory code governing post-1950 acquisition, termination, and diaspora registration. Over seven decades, the Act has been amended **nine times** — in **1957, 1960, 1985, 1986, 1992, 2003, 2005, 2015, and 2019**.

Two overarching statutory shifts deserve immediate attention for UPSC:
1. **Abolition of Commonwealth Citizenship (2003):** Originally, the 1955 Act granted reciprocal *Commonwealth Citizenship* to nationals of Commonwealth countries. The **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003** repealed this colonial-era concept entirely, simultaneously introducing the statutory category of **"illegal migrant"** (`Section 2(1)(b)`), creating **Overseas Citizenship of India (OCI)**, and inserting **`Section 14A`** authorising compulsory registration of citizens and a National Register of Indian Citizens (NRIC/NRC).
2. **Definition of "Illegal Migrant" (`Section 2(1)(b)`):** An *illegal migrant* is defined as a foreigner who either: (i) enters India **without a valid passport or other prescribed travel documents**; OR (ii) enters with valid documents but **remains beyond the permitted period of time**. Illegal migrants are barred from acquiring citizenship by registration or naturalisation (subject to the CAA 2019 proviso) and face detention or deportation under foreigners laws.

### Five Modes of Acquiring Indian Citizenship (Sections 3–7)

The Citizenship Act, 1955 prescribes **five exhaustive modes** of acquiring Indian citizenship: **Birth, Descent, Registration, Naturalisation, and Incorporation of Territory**.

| Mode (Section) | Core Principle & Eligibility | Statutory Cut-offs / Key Thresholds | Key Exceptions & Conditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. By Birth** (`Sec. 3`) | Shifted progressively from *jus soli* (soil) toward *jus sanguinis* (bloodline) | **1950–30 Jun 1987:** Any birth in India; **1 Jul 1987–2 Dec 2004:** Either parent a citizen; **On/after 3 Dec 2004:** Both parents citizens, OR one citizen & other NOT an illegal migrant | Excludes children of **foreign diplomats** posted in India and **enemy aliens** |
| **2. By Descent** (`Sec. 4`) | Born **outside India** to Indian parent(s) | **1950–92:** Father only; **10 Dec 1992–2 Dec 2004:** Either parent; **On/after 3 Dec 2004:** Must register at Indian consulate **within 1 year** + no foreign passport | Dual-national minor loses Indian citizenship if foreign citizenship is not renounced within **6 months of turning 18** |
| **3. By Registration** (`Sec. 5`) | Central Government registration across **7 statutory categories** (not illegal migrants) | **7 years** ordinary residence (PIO or spouse of Indian citizen); **12 months** for former Indian citizens or **5-year OCI cardholders** | Mandatory **Oath of Allegiance** (Second Schedule) |
| **4. By Naturalisation** (`Sec. 6` & 3rd Sch.) | Foreigners (not illegal migrants) meeting all Third Schedule criteria | **12 months** continuous immediately prior + **11 years** in preceding 14 years *(reduced to **5 years** for CAA 2019 beneficiaries)* | Good character; **Eighth Schedule language**; renounce foreign citizenship; **waiver** for distinguished service |
| **5. By Incorporation of Territory** (`Sec. 7`) | Foreign territory becomes part of India | Central Government specifies eligible persons via Official Gazette order | Applied to Puducherry (1962), Goa/Daman & Diu (1962), Dadra & Nagar Haveli (1962), Sikkim (1975) |

#### 1. Citizenship by Birth (`Section 3`): The Progressive Tightening from *Jus Soli* to *Jus Sanguinis*

No provision of the Citizenship Act reveals India's changing demographic and security anxieties more clearly than the evolution of `Section 3`. Originally, independent India embraced inclusive **jus soli** (citizenship by place of birth), reflecting its civic-nationalist ethos. Rising concerns over cross-border irregular migration from neighbouring states — particularly following the Assam Agitation — prompted Parliament to tighten the rule twice, via the **1986** and **2003** Amendments:

- **Born on or after 26 January 1950, but before 1 July 1987:** Citizen of India by birth **irrespective of the nationality of their parents** (pure *jus soli*).
- **Born on or after 1 July 1987, but before 3 December 2004** *(1986 Amendment, effective 1 July 1987)*: Citizen by birth only if **either parent** is a citizen of India at the time of birth.
- **Born on or after 3 December 2004** *(2003 Amendment, effective 3 December 2004)*: Citizen by birth **only if BOTH parents are Indian citizens**, OR **one parent is an Indian citizen and the other is NOT an illegal migrant** at the time of birth.
- **Two Permanent Exclusions:** Regardless of birth date, children born in India to **foreign diplomatic envoys** enjoying immunity from suit and legal process, or to **enemy aliens** (if the birth occurs in a place under enemy occupation), **never** acquire Indian citizenship by birth.

#### 2. Citizenship by Descent (`Section 4`): Gender Equality and Consular Registration

`Section 4` governs persons born **outside India** to Indian parents, tracing an arc from patriarchal framing to gender neutrality and strict anti-dual-nationality controls:

- **Born on or after 26 January 1950, but before 10 December 1992:** Citizen by descent **only if the father** was an Indian citizen at the time of birth (mothers could not transmit citizenship abroad!).
- **Born on or after 10 December 1992, but before 3 December 2004** *(1992 Amendment)*: Corrected the gender anomaly — citizen by descent if **either parent** (father or mother) was an Indian citizen at the time of birth.
- **Born on or after 3 December 2004** *(2003 Amendment)*: A person born outside India shall **not** be a citizen by descent unless: (a) their birth is **registered at an Indian consulate within one year** of birth (or later with Central Government permission); AND (b) the parents submit a written declaration that the minor **does not hold the passport of another country**.
- **Age-18 Renunciation Rule:** A minor who is an Indian citizen by descent and also holds foreign citizenship by birth abroad **automatically ceases to be an Indian citizen** if they do not renounce the foreign nationality within **six months of attaining full age (18 years)**.

#### 3. Citizenship by Registration (`Section 5`): All Seven Statutory Categories

On application, the Central Government may register as a citizen of India any person (**not being an illegal migrant**) falling within **any one of seven specified categories**:

1. **`Section 5(1)(a)`:** A **Person of Indian Origin (PIO)** who is ordinarily resident in India for **seven years** before making an application for registration;
2. **`Section 5(1)(b)`:** A **Person of Indian Origin** who is ordinarily resident in any country or place outside undivided India;
3. **`Section 5(1)(c)`:** A person who is **married to a citizen of India** and is ordinarily resident in India for **seven years** before making an application;
4. **`Section 5(1)(d)`:** **Minor children** of persons who are citizens of India;
5. **`Section 5(1)(e)`:** A person of full age and capacity whose **parents are registered** as citizens of India under `Section 5(1)(a)` or naturalised under `Section 6(1)`;
6. **`Section 5(1)(f)`:** A person of full age and capacity who, or either of whose parents, was **earlier a citizen of independent India**, and has been ordinarily resident in India for **twelve months** immediately before making an application;
7. **`Section 5(1)(g)`:** A person of full age and capacity who has been registered as an **Overseas Citizen of India (OCI) Cardholder for five years**, and has been ordinarily resident in India for **twelve months** immediately before making an application (streamlined by the 2015 Amendment to allow up to 30 days' total break across the 12 months in special circumstances).

For the purposes of `Section 5`, a person is deemed to be of **Indian origin** if they, or **either of their parents**, were born in undivided India or in any other territory that became part of India after 15 August 1947 (note: unlike `Article 6` and `Article 8` which extend to *grandparents*, `Section 5` stops at *parents*!). Every person registered under `Section 5` must take the **Oath of Allegiance** set out in the Second Schedule before registration takes effect.

#### 4. Citizenship by Naturalisation (`Section 6` & Third Schedule)

The Central Government may grant a **Certificate of Naturalisation** to any foreigner (not being an illegal migrant) who meets **all seven qualifications** laid down in clauses (a) to (g) of the **Third Schedule** of the Act:
1. **Reciprocity (`clause (a)`):** Not a subject or citizen of any country where Indian citizens are legally prevented from becoming citizens by naturalisation;
2. **Renunciation Undertaking (`clause (b)`):** Undertakes to renounce the citizenship of their existing country if their application for Indian citizenship is accepted;
3. **Immediate 12-Month Residence or Service (`clause (c)`):** Has either resided in India or been in the service of a Government in India (or partly one and partly the other) continuously throughout the **12 months immediately preceding** the date of application;
4. **Aggregate 11-Year Prior Residence or Service (`clause (d)`):** During the **14 years immediately preceding those 12 months**, has resided in India or served the Government for periods amounting in aggregate to **not less than 11 years** — making an effective minimum of **12 years** (reduced under the **CAA 2019** proviso to **not less than 5 years** in place of 11 years for eligible persecuted minorities, yielding a 6-year effective minimum);
5. **Good Character (`clause (e)`):** Is of good character;
6. **Linguistic Proficiency (`clause (f)`):** Has an adequate knowledge of at least one language specified in the **Eighth Schedule** of the Constitution (currently 22 languages);
7. **Future Intention (`clause (g)`):** Intends, upon receiving a certificate of naturalisation, to reside in India, or to enter/continue in service under a Government in India, an international organisation of which India is a member, or a society/company/body of persons established in India.

**Distinguished Service Waiver (`Section 6(1)` Proviso):** Crucially, the Government of India may **waive all or any of the above conditions** for a person who, in its opinion, has rendered **distinguished service to the cause of science, philosophy, art, literature, world peace, or human progress** (used, for instance, in granting naturalisation to renowned figures such as Mother Teresa and singer Adnan Sami). Naturalised citizens must likewise subscribe to the **Oath of Allegiance**.

#### 5. Citizenship by Incorporation of Territory (`Section 7`)

Whenever any foreign territory becomes part of the territory of India, `Section 7` empowers the Central Government, by order notified in the Official Gazette, to specify which persons connected with that territory shall become citizens of India from the notified date. India invoked this provision to confer collective Indian citizenship via the **Citizenship (Pondicherry) Order, 1962**, the **Goa, Daman and Diu (Citizenship) Order, 1962**, the **Dadra and Nagar Haveli (Citizenship) Order, 1962**, and the **Sikkim (Citizenship) Order, 1975**.

### Three Modes of Losing Indian Citizenship (Sections 8–10)

The Citizenship Act, 1955 specifies **three distinct pathways** by which Indian citizenship — whether acquired under the Constitution or under the Act — can be lost: **Renunciation, Termination, and Deprivation**.

| Mode (Section) | Nature of Loss | Trigger & Procedure | Critical Statutory Nuances & Exceptions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Renunciation** (`Sec. 8`) | **Voluntary act** by citizen | Citizen of full age & capacity files formal declaration; citizenship ceases upon **registration** of declaration | **War Bar:** Registration may be withheld during a war in which India is engaged; **Minors:** Minor children also lose citizenship, but may **resume it within 1 year of turning 18** |
| **2. Termination** (`Sec. 9`) | **Automatic operation of law** | Indian citizen **voluntarily** (consciously, without compulsion) acquires citizenship of another country | Needs no government order — operates automatically; **War Bar:** Does **not** apply during a war in which India is engaged |
| **3. Deprivation** (`Sec. 10`) | **Compulsory termination** by Central Government order | Order passed on **any of 5 statutory grounds** (fraud, disloyalty, war trade, imprisonment, 7-yr absence) | Applies primarily to citizens by **registration/naturalisation** (and domicile/`Art. 5(c)` in specified grounds), NOT broadly to citizens by birth; Centre must be satisfied deprivation is **conducive to the public good** (except 7-yr absence) |

#### All Five Grounds of Compulsory Deprivation (`Section 10`)

Under `Section 10`, the Central Government may compulsorily deprive a citizen of Indian citizenship if satisfied of any of **five exhaustive grounds**:

1. **Fraud or Concealment:** The citizen obtained registration or naturalisation by means of **fraud, false representation, or concealment of any material fact**;
2. **Disloyalty to the Constitution:** The citizen has shown, by act or speech, **disloyalty or disaffection towards the Constitution of India** as by law established;
3. **Trading with the Enemy during War:** The citizen has, during any war in which India may be engaged, **unlawfully traded or communicated with an enemy** or been associated with any business carried on in a manner assisting an enemy;
4. **Imprisonment within Five Years:** The citizen has, **within five years** after registration or naturalisation, been **sentenced to imprisonment in any country for a term of not less than two years**;
5. **Continuous Absence Abroad for Seven Years:** The citizen has been **ordinarily resident out of India for seven years continuously**, UNLESS they were: (i) a student at an educational institution abroad; OR (ii) in the service of a Government in India or an international organisation of which India is a member; OR (iii) registered annually at an Indian consulate declaring their intention to retain Indian citizenship.

> [!prelims]
> **Precision Memory Anchors on Sections 3–10:**
> - **Two Different Age-18 Timelines:** A minor who loses citizenship because a parent *renounced* it (`Sec. 8`) has **1 year after turning 18** to declare resumption of Indian citizenship. By contrast, a minor who acquired Indian citizenship by *descent* (`Sec. 4`) alongside a foreign nationality must renounce the foreign citizenship within **6 months of turning 18** or forfeit Indian citizenship!
> - **War Exception Applies to TWO Modes:** Both **Renunciation** (Centre may withhold registration) and **Termination** (automatic termination does not operate) carry an explicit wartime suspension clause to prevent citizens from dodging wartime obligations or treason jurisdiction.
> - **Deprivation Scope:** A person who is a citizen strictly **by birth** under `Section 3` cannot be deprived of citizenship under `Section 10` on grounds such as 7 years' continuous residence abroad or 2 years' imprisonment!

### Special Provisions for Assam: Section 6A and the October 2024 Constitution Bench Verdict

Assam occupies a singular place in India's citizenship jurisprudence due to its geography and history. Partition severed East Bengal from Assam, and over the subsequent decades — peaking massively during the **1971 Bangladesh Liberation War** when an estimated ten million refugees crossed into eastern India — demographic anxieties over undocumented migration reshaped Assamese politics. Following a six-year mass movement (**Assam Agitation, 1979–1985**) led by the **All Assam Students' Union (AASU)** and the **All Assam Gana Sangram Parishad (AAGSP)**, the Union Government (under Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi), the Government of Assam, and agitation leaders signed the historic **Assam Accord on 15 August 1985**.

To give statutory effect to the Accord's citizenship settlement, Parliament enacted the **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1985**, inserting **`Section 6A`** — a special regime applicable **exclusively to Assam** for persons of Indian origin entering from the *"specified territory"* (territories included in Bangladesh immediately before 1985). `Section 6A` divided migrants into **three distinct legal cohorts** using two pivotal cut-off dates (**1 January 1966** and **25 March 1971**):

1. **Entered Assam BEFORE 1 January 1966:** All persons of Indian origin who migrated to Assam from Bangladesh prior to 1 January 1966 and have been ordinarily resident in Assam since entry are **deemed to be citizens of India** from 1 January 1966.
2. **Entered Assam ON OR AFTER 1 January 1966, and BEFORE 25 March 1971:** Upon being **detected as a foreigner** by a Foreigners Tribunal, such persons must **register themselves** with the designated authority. Upon registration, they enjoy all rights and obligations of Indian citizens **EXCEPT the right to vote** (their names are deleted from electoral rolls) for a **period of ten years** from the date of detection; upon expiry of those ten years, they are deemed full citizens of India with restored voting rights.
3. **Entered Assam ON OR AFTER 25 March 1971** *(the eve of Pakistan's Operation Searchlight in East Pakistan)*: Treated strictly as **illegal migrants**, ineligible for citizenship under `Section 6A`, and liable to detection, deletion from electoral rolls, and deportation.

#### From *Sarbananda Sonowal* (2005) to *In Re: Section 6A* (17 October 2024)

The legal enforcement of Assam's 1971 cut-off generated two landmark Supreme Court battles. First, in ***Sarbananda Sonowal v. Union of India* (2005)**, a 3-Judge Bench struck down the **Illegal Migrants (Determination by Tribunals) Act, 1983 (IMDT Act)** — a statute enacted exclusively for Assam that placed the burden of proving foreign nationality on the complainant/State rather than on the accused individual (unlike the Foreigners Act, 1946 applied across the rest of India). The Court held that unabated large-scale illegal migration amounted to **"external aggression"** under **`Article 355`**, rendering the weak IMDT Act unconstitutional and shifting detection proceedings back to Foreigners Tribunals under the Foreigners Act.

Second, starting in 2009 (*Assam Public Works* and *Assam Sanmilita Mahasangha*), petitioners challenged the **constitutional validity of `Section 6A` itself**, arguing that fixing a generous **25 March 1971** cut-off exclusively for Assam — compared to the **19 July 1948** constitutional cut-off (`Article 6`) for the rest of India — discriminated arbitrarily against Assam (`Article 14`), threatened the indigenous Assamese language and culture (`Article 29(1)`), violated the Union's protective duty under `Article 355`, and breached the constitutional text of `Articles 6` and `7`.

> [!case]
> ***In Re: Section 6A of the Citizenship Act, 1955*** (`2024 INSC 789`, **17 October 2024** — 5-Judge Constitution Bench, **4–1 Majority**):
> - **Bench & Three Opinions:** **Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud** (concurring separately), **Justice Surya Kant** (authoring the lead majority opinion for himself, **Justice M.M. Sundresh**, and **Justice Manoj Misra**), and **Justice J.B. Pardiwala** (sole dissent).
> - **Majority Holding (4–1 — Upholding Validity):**
>   1. **Parliamentary Competence (`Article 11`):** Parliament holds plenary power under `Article 11` to frame special statutory cut-off dates post-1950; `Section 6A` supplements rather than violates `Articles 6` and `7`, which only governed citizenship at the Constitution's commencement.
>   2. **Rationality under `Article 14`:** Singling out Assam had an intelligible differentia and rational nexus. Unlike West Bengal or Tripura, Assam's smaller population size meant cross-border inflows created a disproportionate demographic share, making a negotiated political compromise (**Assam Accord**) essential. The **25 March 1971** date was eminently rational because Pakistan launched *Operation Searchlight* on that night, triggering the Liberation War.
>   3. **Cultural Rights under `Article 29(1)`:** `Article 29(1)` protects a community's right to *conserve* its distinct language and culture; the mere presence of diverse ethnic or linguistic groups within a state does not ipso facto violate `Article 29(1)`, nor does the Constitution endorse ethnic homogeneity.
>   4. **Fraternity & Implementation Directives (`Article 355`):** Rejecting the claim that `Section 6A` violated `Article 355`, the majority observed that constitutional **fraternity** calls for live-and-let-live coexistence while insisting that post-25 March 1971 entrants remain illegal migrants. Crucially, the Bench found the existing statutory machinery for detecting and deporting post-1971 migrants **inadequate**, directing the Supreme Court to monitor the functioning of Foreigners Tribunals and enforcement mechanisms.
> - **Sole Dissent (Justice J.B. Pardiwala — Doctrine of Temporal Unreasonableness):** Held `Section 6A` **prospectively unconstitutional** from the date of judgment. Applying the doctrine of *temporal unreasonableness* and manifest arbitrariness, Justice Pardiwala reasoned that although `Section 6A` was justified as a transitional political settlement in 1985, the absence of a **sunset clause** coupled with nearly four decades of executive inertia had transformed it into an open-ended, self-defeating mechanism perpetuating unending litigation and demographic uncertainty.

## 6. CAA 2019, NRC, Single Citizenship & Diaspora Frameworks

Contemporary citizenship debates in India sit at the intersection of four powerful currents: humanitarian protection for persecuted minorities in the subcontinent, border security and irregular migration, federal diversity in the North-East, and economic engagement with one of the world's largest overseas diasporas.

### Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 (CAA) and the March 2024 Rules

Enacted as **`Act No. 47 of 2019`** (receiving Presidential assent on **12 December 2019** and formally commencing on **10 January 2020**), the **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 (CAA)** amended the Citizenship Act, 1955 to create a fast-track citizenship pathway for specified religious minorities from three neighbouring countries. Its implementing framework — the **Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024** — was formally notified by the Ministry of Home Affairs on **11 March 2024**, with the first batch of citizenship certificates issued in May 2024.

#### Core Statutory Provisions of CAA 2019

1. **Carve-out from "Illegal Migrant" (`Section 2(1)(b)` Proviso):** Exempts persons belonging to **six specified religious communities** — **Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, and Christians** — from **three neighbouring countries** (**Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan**) who entered India **on or before 31 December 2014** (having been exempted under the Passport (Entry into India) Act, 1920 or Foreigners Act, 1946 due to religious persecution or fear thereof) from being treated as *"illegal migrants"*.
2. **Special Conferment Provision (`Section 6B`):** Empowers the Central Government (or a notified authority) to grant a certificate of registration or naturalisation to eligible beneficiaries. Upon conferment, the person is **deemed to be a citizen of India from the date of their entry into India**, and all pending legal proceedings regarding illegal migration or citizenship stand **abated**.
3. **Relaxed Naturalisation Residency (Third Schedule Amendment):** Reduces the aggregate period of residence or Government service required under clause (d) of the Third Schedule from **"not less than eleven years" to "not less than five years"** (alongside the continuous **12 months** immediately preceding application under clause (c), effectively lowering the minimum statutory residence threshold from 12 years to 6 years).
4. **Territorial Exemptions Protecting the North-East (`Section 6B(4)`):** To safeguard indigenous demographics in sensitive borderlands, CAA 2019 **does NOT apply** to two categories of territories:
   - **Sixth Schedule Tribal Areas** of **Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram, and Tripura** (e.g., Karbi Anglong, Dima Hasao, Bodoland Territorial Region in Assam; Khasi, Jaintia, and Garo Hills in Meghalaya; Chakma, Mara, and Lai Autonomous Districts in Mizoram; and Tripura Tribal Areas Autonomous District Council); and
   - **Inner Line Permit (ILP) Areas** notified under the **Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation, 1873** — currently covering **Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram, and Manipur** (note: Manipur was brought under the ILP regime by a Presidential Order in December 2019 concurrently with CAA's passage!).
5. **OCI Cancellation Amendment (`Section 7D`):** Simultaneously expanded grounds for cancelling OCI registration (inserting `Section 7D(da)` for violation of any notified law) while adding a vital natural-justice proviso requiring a **reasonable opportunity of being heard**.

#### Implementation Architecture: Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024 (11 March 2024)

Notified under `Section 18` of the Act (inserting `Rules 10A, 11A, and 13A` into the Citizenship Rules, 2009), the March 2024 Rules operationalised `Section 6B` through a **completely centralised, online, two-tier machinery** that bypasses state government bureaucracies:
- **Electronic Application:** Applicants apply online via `indiancitizenshiponline.nic.in`, submitting documentary proof of origin in Afghanistan, Bangladesh, or Pakistan (Schedule 1A — including even expired passports, birth/school certificates, or parent/grandparent records) and proof of entry into India on or before 31 December 2014 (Schedule 1B), plus an eligibility certificate from a locally reputed community institution confirming membership in one of the six communities.
- **Tier 1 — District Level Committee (DLC):** Chaired by the Senior Superintendent or Superintendent of Post (Department of Posts) as the **Designated Officer**, with representatives of the District Collector and central agencies; verifies physical documents and administers the **Oath of Allegiance**.
- **Tier 2 — State/UT Empowered Committee (EC):** Headed by the **Director of Census Operations** in each State/UT, with officers from the **Subsidiary Intelligence Bureau (SIB)**, **Foreigners Regional Registration Office (FRRO)**, **National Informatics Centre (NIC)**, and **Post Master General**; scrutinises applications and grants digital/physical citizenship certificates.

### National Register of Citizens (NRC) vs National Population Register (NPR)

Public discourse routinely conflates three legally separate demographic instruments — the **NRC**, the **NPR**, and the **decennial Census** — making their exact statutory distinctions a prime UPSC testing ground.

- **National Register of Citizens (NRC) / NRIC:** Derives its statutory footing from **`Section 14A`** of the Citizenship Act (inserted by the **2003 Amendment**) read with the **Citizenship (Registration of Citizens and Issue of National Identity Cards) Rules, 2003**. Its sole legal purpose is to maintain an authenticated register of **Indian citizens** verified through documentary lineage/citizenship proof. To date, the NRC has been updated **ONLY in Assam** — first prepared after the 1951 Census and updated between 2013 and 2019 under the direct monitoring of the Supreme Court (*Assam Public Works*). When Assam's **final NRC list was published on 31 August 2019**, out of 3.30 crore applicants, approximately **3.11 crore were included** and **19.06 lakh applicants were excluded**; the process remains legally unresolved as formal Registrar General of India (RGI) notification and adjudication of appeals before Foreigners Tribunals are still pending, and no pan-India NRC has been notified.
- **National Population Register (NPR):** Prepared under the **same 2003 Citizenship Rules** (Rule 3 & Rule 4), the NPR is a comprehensive identity database of **all "usual residents"** of India — **both citizens and foreigners** — defined as anyone who has resided in a local area for the past **6 months or more**, or intends to reside there for the next **6 months or more**. First collected alongside the 2011 Census house-listing in **2010** and updated in **2015**, the NPR does **not** by itself confer or revoke citizenship; however, under Rule 4(3) of the 2003 Rules, the NPR is legally designated as the **database from which the Local Register of Indian Citizens (NRIC/NRC)** is verified by marking *"doubtful citizens"* — the root cause of political apprehension surrounding proposed NPR updates.
- **Census of India:** Conducted every ten years under a completely separate statute — the **Census Act, 1948**. It collects anonymised macroeconomic, demographic, and socio-economic data of the entire population; critically, **Section 15 of the Census Act, 1948** renders individual Census records strictly **confidential and inadmissible as evidence in any court of law**, unlike NRC/NPR data.

### Single Citizenship in India: Philosophy, Comparative Context, and Constitutional Exceptions

Although India adopted a federal polity with dual governments (Union and States), the framers deliberately rejected the **dual citizenship** model practised in federal democracies like the **United States** and **Switzerland** — where every individual holds both national citizenship and state/cantonal citizenship, enabling constituent states to favour their own citizens in voting, public offices, and professional licensing. Instead, India borrowed **Single Citizenship** from the **British Constitution**: every Indian is a citizen solely of India, carrying uniform civil and political rights across every State and Union Territory.

Dr. Ambedkar and the Constituent Assembly embraced single citizenship to cultivate **fraternity**, forge a unified national economy, and prevent provincial chauvinism from erecting internal barriers against interstate migrants. Yet, balancing national unity with the legitimate developmental needs of backward regions and vulnerable tribal populations, the Constitution itself carves out **four carefully circumscribed exceptions** to absolute uniformity of rights across States:

1. **Residence Requirement in Public Employment (`Article 16(3)`):** While `Article 16(2)` bars discrimination based on place of birth or residence in public employment, `Article 16(3)` empowers **Parliament — and ONLY Parliament, NEVER a State Legislature!** — to prescribe residence within a particular State or Union Territory as a qualification for certain classes of state or local public employment. Parliament originally enacted the *Public Employment (Requirement as to Residence) Act, 1957* (covering Andhra Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh, Manipur, and Tripura), which expired in **1974**. Today, special constitutional residence provisions continue under **`Article 371D`** (for **Andhra Pradesh** and **Telangana**, inserted by the 32nd Amendment, 1973) and **`Article 371J`** (for the **Kalyana-Karnataka / Hyderabad-Karnataka** region, inserted by the 98th Amendment, 2012).
2. **Residence-Based Preferences Outside Public Employment (`Article 15`):** `Article 15(1)` prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or **place of birth** — crucially, it **omits "residence"** from the list of prohibited grounds! Consequently, State Governments may constitutionally offer reasonable **residence-based (domicile) reservations** or fee concessions in undergraduate State institutions — upheld in ***D.P. Joshi v. State of Madhya Bharat* (1955)** and refined in ***Pradeep Jain v. Union of India* (1984)**. Most recently, in ***Tanvi Behl v. Shrey Goel*** (`2025 INSC 125`, **29 January 2025**), a 3-Judge Bench expressly invoked India's **single domicile** (*"We are all domiciled in the territory of India"*) to strike down **residence/domicile-based reservation in Postgraduate (PG) medical courses** as unconstitutional under `Article 14` (while preserving reasonable residence quotas in MBBS seats and reasonable institutional preference in PG admissions).
3. **Protection of Scheduled Tribes (`Article 19(5)`):** Parliament and State Legislatures may impose reasonable restrictions on the freedoms of movement (`Art. 19(1)(d)`) and residence/settlement (`Art. 19(1)(e)`) in the **interests of any Scheduled Tribe** — sustaining the **Inner Line Permit (ILP)** regime in North-Eastern states and restrictions on non-tribal land acquisition in Fifth and Sixth Schedule areas to safeguard indigenous land, culture, and livelihoods.
4. **Erstwhile Special Status of Jammu & Kashmir (`Article 35A` — Historical Context):** Prior to August 2019, `Article 35A` (inserted via Presidential Order of 1954 under `Article 370`) authorised the J&K Legislature to define *"permanent residents"* of the state and confer upon them exclusive rights regarding state public employment, acquisition of immovable property, settlement, and state scholarships. Following the abrogation of `Article 370`'s operative provisions in **August 2019** (C.O. 272 & 273), `Article 35A` ceased to exist and was replaced by statutory **J&K Domicile Rules (2020)** issued by the Central Government (extending domicile eligibility to residents of 15 years, students who studied 7 years and appeared in Class 10/12 exams in J&K, and children of long-serving Central officials).

> [!prelims]
> **UPSC Trap — Who Can Prescribe Residence Qualifications for Jobs vs Admissions?**
> - For **State Public Employment** (`Article 16(3)`): **ONLY Parliament** can prescribe a residence requirement — State Legislatures lack constitutional authority to enact local-job residence quotas unilaterally (which is why state statutes mandating 75–80% local quotas in private or public jobs repeatedly face judicial invalidation!).
> - For **State Educational Admissions** (`Article 15`): Because *"residence"* is absent from `Article 15`, **State Governments/Legislatures** can legally frame reasonable residence-based admissions policies (subject to `Article 14` reasonableness and non-exclusion at super-speciality medical levels).

### Diaspora Architecture: NRI vs PIO (Merged 2015) vs OCI Cardholders

With an overseas diaspora exceeding 35 million persons of Indian origin and Non-Resident Indians, India had to balance its strict **constitutional bar against dual citizenship** (`Article 9` & `Section 9`) with the growing cultural, philanthropic, and investment aspirations of global Indians.

#### Evolution: L.M. Singhvi Committee (2000) to the 2015 PIO–OCI Merger

In **September 2000**, the Government of India constituted a **High Level Committee on the Indian Diaspora** chaired by **Dr. L.M. Singhvi**. Submitting its report in January 2002, the Committee recommended celebrating **Pravasi Bharatiya Divas** on **9 January** (commemorating Mahatma Gandhi's return from South Africa in 1915) and introducing a statutory "dual-citizenship-like" facilitation card for persons of Indian origin in select countries. Acting on these recommendations:
- The **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003** introduced the **Overseas Citizenship of India (OCI)** scheme initially for PIOs of **16 specified countries** (excluding Pakistan and Bangladesh);
- The **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2005** expanded OCI globally to PIOs of **all countries except Pakistan and Bangladesh** (launching operationally on **2 December 2005**);
- Meanwhile, an earlier executive **Person of Indian Origin (PIO) Card Scheme** (launched in 2002, valid for 15 years with mandatory FRRO reporting after 180 days) ran parallel to OCI, causing administrative confusion. The **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2015** formally **merged the PIO Card into the OCI Cardholder scheme** (`Sections 7A–7D`) with effect from **9 January 2015**, deeming all existing PIO cardholders to be OCI cardholders.

#### Eligibility, Rights, 2021 MHA Restrictions, and Cancellation Grounds of OCI (`Sections 7A–7D`)

**1. Eligibility (`Section 7A`):** The Central Government may register as an **OCI Cardholder**:
- Any foreign citizen who: (i) was a citizen of India on or after 26 January 1950; OR (ii) was eligible to become a citizen on 26 January 1950; OR (iii) belonged to a territory that became part of India after 15 August 1947;
- A child, grandchild, or **great-grandchild** of such a citizen;
- A minor child of any of the above, OR a minor child whose both parents (or one parent) are citizens of India;
- A **spouse of foreign origin** of an Indian citizen OR of an OCI cardholder, whose marriage has been registered and **subsisted for a continuous period of not less than two years** immediately preceding application (subject to security clearance).
- **Absolute Statutory Bar:** **No person** who — or either of whose parents, grandparents, or great-grandparents — is or had ever been a citizen of **Pakistan, Bangladesh**, or such other country as the Central Government may notify, is eligible for OCI registration.

**2. Statutory Rights vs Express Constitutional Disqualifications (`Section 7B`):**
- **Key Privileges:** Multiple-entry, **lifelong visa** to visit India; complete **exemption from FRRO police registration** regardless of length of stay; parity with **Indian nationals** in domestic airfares, entry fees to national monuments, museums, and wildlife sanctuaries, and inter-country adoption; parity with **NRIs** in financial, economic, and educational fields (except acquiring agricultural land, plantation properties, or farmhouses) and professional practice in India as doctors, dentists, nurses, pharmacists, advocates, architects, and chartered accountants under relevant laws.
- **Explicit Constitutional Bars (`Section 7B(2)`):** An OCI Cardholder is **strictly a foreign national, NOT a dual citizen**. They are expressly **denied**: (a) equality of opportunity in public employment (`Article 16`); (b) eligibility for President (`Art. 58`), Vice-President (`Art. 66`), Supreme Court Judge (`Art. 124`), or High Court Judge (`Art. 217`); (c) registration as a voter (`Sec. 16`, RPA 1950); (d) eligibility for membership of Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha, State Legislative Assembly, or State Legislative Council; and (e) appointment to public services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union or of any State (unless specially exempted by the Central Government).

**3. The 4 March 2021 MHA Notification (`S.O. 1050(E)`) & Judicial Check (*Anushka Rengunthwar*, 2023):**
Superseding earlier 2005, 2007, and 2009 notifications under `Section 7B(1)`, the Ministry of Home Affairs issued a consolidated Gazette notification on **4 March 2021** tightening regulatory oversight over OCI cardholders:
- **Four Activities Requiring Prior Special Permission/Permit** (from the competent authority, FRRO, or Indian Mission):
  1. Undertaking **research**;
  2. Undertaking **missionary, Tabligh, mountaineering, or journalistic activities**;
  3. Undertaking **internship or employment in foreign diplomatic missions** or foreign government organisations in India;
  4. Visiting any **Protected, Restricted, or Prohibited area** notified by the Government.
- **Parity Shift:** Clarified that in all matters not expressly specified in the notification, OCI cardholders enjoy parity with **foreign nationals** (rather than NRIs) under FEMA and other laws.
- **Higher Education Seats (`Anushka Rengunthwar v. Union of India`, 3 February 2023):** The 2021 notification barred OCI students from competing for general/all-India seats in exams like **NEET and JEE**, confining them strictly to **NRI or supernumerary seats**. On 3 February 2023, the Supreme Court held that stripping accrued rights retroactively was arbitrary and ruled that this restriction applies **ONLY prospectively** to foreign-born OCI applicants born **after 4 March 2021** — protecting existing cohorts of OCI students raised and schooled in India.

**4. All Seven Grounds for Cancellation of OCI Registration (`Section 7D`):**
The Central Government may cancel OCI registration by order if satisfied that:
1. Registration was obtained by **fraud, false representation, or concealment** of a material fact (`7D(a)`);
2. The cardholder has shown **disaffection towards the Constitution** of India (`7D(b)`);
3. During any war in which India is engaged, the cardholder **unlawfully traded or communicated with an enemy** or assisted an enemy business (`7D(c)`);
4. Within **five years** after OCI registration, the cardholder is **sentenced to imprisonment for not less than two years** (`7D(d)`);
5. *(Inserted by CAA 2019)* The cardholder has **violated any provision of the Citizenship Act or any other law** notified by the Central Government in the Official Gazette (`7D(da)`);
6. Cancellation is necessary in the interests of the **sovereignty and integrity of India, security of India, friendly relations with foreign states, or general public interest** (`7D(e)`);
7. For a foreign-origin spouse OCI cardholder, the qualifying **marriage has been dissolved** by a competent court or otherwise, OR the cardholder **solemnised another marriage** during the subsistence of that marriage (`7D(f)`).
- **Mandatory Natural-Justice Safeguard (CAA 2019 Proviso):** No cancellation order under `Section 7D` shall be passed unless the OCI cardholder has been afforded a **reasonable opportunity of being heard**.

## 1. Union of States & Territorial Architecture (Articles 1–4)
- **`Art. 1(1)`:** *"India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States."* Both names constitutionally equivalent (Sept 2023 G20 "President of Bharat" debate).
- **Ambedkar's 2 reasons for "Union of States" (4 Nov 1948):** (1) Federation is NOT the result of an agreement among states (unlike USA); (2) States have **no right to secede** (indestructible Union).
- **"Territory of India" (wider, `Art. 1(3)`)** = States (28) + UTs (8) + acquired territories. **"Union of India" (narrower)** = States only.
- **`Art. 2`:** Admit or establish new States **outside India** (Sikkim: **35th Amend 1974** → Associate State via `Art. 2A` & old 10th Sch.; **36th Amend 1975** → repealed `Art. 2A`, inserted `Art. 371F`, 22nd full State on 26 Apr 1975).
- **`Art. 3`:** Internal reorganisation (new state, area, boundaries, name). **Proviso:** Prior Presidential recommendation + referral to affected State Legislature for **non-binding views** within fixed time (`5th Amend 1955`). No fresh referral if Parliament amends Bill (*Babulal Parate*, 1960).
- **`18th Amend` (1966) Explanations:** `Explanation I` — "State" includes UT in clauses (a)–(e), **NOT in Proviso** (UT legislatures need not be consulted). `Explanation II` — can unite parts of States/UTs into new State/UT.
- ***In Re: Art. 370* (11 Dec 2023):** Upheld carving out Ladakh UT (`Art. 3(a)` + `Explanation I`); **left open** whether `Art. 3` permits downgrading an entire State into UTs (relying on assurance to restore J&K statehood).
- **India vs USA:** India = *"indestructible Union of destructible states"*; USA = *"indestructible Union of indestructible states."*
- **`Art. 4`:** Laws under Arts. 2 & 3 amend First & Fourth Schedules and are **NOT `Art. 368` amendments** → **simple majority** (Joint Sitting under `Art. 108` allowed).

## 2. Cession of Territory & Border Settlements
- ***Berubari Union* (1960, 8-Judge Bench):** `Art. 3(c)` covers internal changes only; ceding *de jure* territory abroad requires an **`Art. 368` amendment** → **9th Amendment Act, 1960**.

## 4. Constitutional Citizenship (Part II, Articles 5–11)
- **Citizen-Only Rights & Offices:** FRs `15, 16, 19, 29, 30`; vote (`Art. 326`); contest MP/MLA seats; President, VP, SC/HC Judges, Governor, AG, Advocate General; Fundamental Duties (`Art. 51A`). Enemy aliens denied `Art. 22` safeguards.
- **Traps:** **Naturalised citizens** CAN become President of India (unlike USA!). Corporations are **NOT citizens** (*STC v. CTO*, 1963). India has **single domicile** (*Pradeep Jain*, 1984).
- **Arts. 5–9 commenced 26 Nov 1949; cover ONLY 26 Jan 1950:** `Art. 5` (domicile + birth/parent/5-yr residence); `Art. 6` (from Pakistan: before **19 July 1948** ordinary residence vs on/after **19 July 1948** 6-month residence + registration); `Art. 7` (to Pakistan after **1 March 1947** barred unless returned on permanent permit); `Art. 8` (PIOs abroad registered at consulate); `Art. 9` (voluntary foreign citizenship ends Indian citizenship); `Art. 10` (continuance subject to law); `Art. 11` (**plenary parliamentary power**).

## 5. Citizenship Act, 1955 & Section 6A (Assam)
- Amended **9 times** (1957–2019); **2003 Amend** repealed Commonwealth citizenship, defined *illegal migrant* (`Sec. 2(1)(b)`), added OCI & `Sec. 14A` (NRC).
- **5 Acquisition Modes:** (1) **Birth (`Sec. 3`):** 1950–30 Jun 1987 (pure *jus soli*); 1 Jul 1987–2 Dec 2004 (1 citizen parent); from **3 Dec 2004** (both citizens OR 1 citizen + other NOT illegal migrant); excludes diplomats/enemy aliens. (2) **Descent (`Sec. 4`):** father only (1950–92); either parent (from 10 Dec 1992); from 3 Dec 2004 register at consulate within **1 yr** + no foreign passport; renounce foreign citizenship within **6 months of turning 18**. (3) **Registration (`Sec. 5`):** 7 categories (7-yr residence for PIO/spouse; 12 months for former citizens/5-yr OCIs). (4) **Naturalisation (`Sec. 6`/3rd Sch.):** 12 months + **11 yrs** in 14 yrs (5 yrs under CAA); good character; **8th Schedule language**; distinguished-service waiver. (5) **Incorporation (`Sec. 7`):** Gazette order.
- **3 Loss Modes:** (1) **Renunciation (`Sec. 8`)** — withheld in war; minor may resume within **1 yr of turning 18**; (2) **Termination (`Sec. 9`)** — automatic on voluntary foreign citizenship (not in war); (3) **Deprivation (`Sec. 10`)** — 5 grounds: fraud, disloyalty, wartime enemy trade, **2-yr prison within 5 yrs**, **7 yrs continuous residence abroad**.
- **Section 6A (Assam Accord 1985):** Pre-1 Jan 1966 = citizens; 1 Jan 1966–24 Mar 1971 = register, **10-yr voting bar**, then full citizens; on/after **25 Mar 1971** = illegal migrants. *Sarbananda Sonowal* (2005) struck down IMDT Act (`Art. 355`).
- ***In Re: Section 6A* (17 Oct 2024, 5-Judge Bench, 4–1):** Upheld `Sec. 6A` under `Arts. 11, 14, 29(1) & 355` (Justice Surya Kant + Sundresh + Misra; CJI Chandrachud concurring); **Justice Pardiwala dissented** (*temporal unreasonableness*).

## 6. CAA 2019, NRC/NPR, Single Citizenship & Diaspora
- **CAA 2019 (`Sec. 6B`) & Rules (11 Mar 2024):** 6 non-Muslim groups (Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi, Christian) from **Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Pakistan** entering by **31 Dec 2014**; naturalisation cut **11→5 yrs** (+12 months); exempts **Sixth Schedule** (Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Tripura) & **ILP** (Arunachal, Nagaland, Mizoram, Manipur). Online processing via **DLC** & **State EC**.
- **Immigration & Foreigners Act, 2025 (eff. 1 Sept 2025) & Exemption Orders (Sept 2025 / 4 Nov 2026):** Replaced 4 laws; protects 6 persecuted minorities entering up to **31 Dec 2024** from prosecution (visa required for stay) **without changing CAA's 31 Dec 2014 cut-off**.
- **NRC vs NPR vs Census:** NRC (`Sec. 14A`, 2003 Rules) = citizens only (Assam 31 Aug 2019: ~19.06 lakh excluded); NPR (2003 Rules) = all usual residents (≥6 months); Census (`Census Act, 1948`) = confidential headcount.
- **Single Citizenship Exceptions:** `Art. 16(3)` (**Parliament only** sets job residence — `Arts. 371D, 371J`); `Art. 15` omits residence (MBBS domicile quotas allowed, **PG medical domicile quotas struck down in *Tanvi Behl*, 29 Jan 2025**); `Art. 19(5)` ST areas; former `Art. 35A` (abrogated 2019).
- **NRI vs PIO (merged 9 Jan 2015) vs OCI (`Secs. 7A–7D`):** NRI = Indian citizen; OCI = foreign national (NO Pakistan/Bangladesh lineage), lifelong visa, FRRO-exempt, NO vote/public office/farmland. **4 Mar 2021 MHA Notification** requires permit for research, missionary, Tabligh, mountaineering, journalism, diplomatic internships, protected areas; NEET/JEE NRI-seat bar prospective only for foreign-born post-4 Mar 2021 (*Anushka Rengunthwar*, Feb 2023). `Sec. 7D`: **7 cancellation grounds** + mandatory **hearing** (CAA 2019).
