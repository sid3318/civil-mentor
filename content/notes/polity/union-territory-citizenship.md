## 1. "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States"

Part I of the Constitution (Articles 1 to 4) deals with the Union and its territory, and Part II (Articles 5 to 11) with citizenship. Together they answer two basic questions of any state: what its territory is, and who belongs to it.

Article 1 opens with the words "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States". The Drafting Committee chose "Union of States" over "Federation of States" with care. As Ambedkar explained to the Assembly, the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement among the states, as the American one is, and so **no state has the right to secede** from it. The federation is a Union because it is indestructible. The country can be divided into states for convenient administration, but it remains one integral whole.

Article 1 also distinguishes two phrases. The **territory of India** includes three categories: the territories of the states, the Union territories, and any territories that India may acquire. The **Union of India** includes only the states, since only the states share powers with the Centre in the federal system. The Union territories and acquired territories are administered directly by the Centre.

> **In the news:** The words "India, that is Bharat" drew attention in September 2023, when invitations to a dinner during the G20 summit in New Delhi were issued in the name of the "President of Bharat". Article 1 uses both names, so either is constitutionally correct.

## 2. Making and remaking states: Articles 2, 3 and 4

The Constitution lets Parliament reorganise the states far more easily than most federations allow.

- **Article 2** lets Parliament admit into the Union, or establish, new states that were not part of India before, on such terms as it thinks fit. Sikkim, for example, became an associate state through the 35th Amendment (1974) and a full state through the 36th Amendment (1975).
- **Article 3** lets Parliament reorganise states already in the Union. It can form a new state by separating territory from a state, uniting two or more states or parts of states, or uniting territory with a state. It can increase or diminish the area of any state, and alter a state's boundaries or name.

Article 3 lays down two conditions. A bill for any of these purposes can be introduced in Parliament only with the **prior recommendation of the President**. Before recommending it, the President must refer it to the state legislature concerned for its views within a specified period. Parliament is **not bound by the state legislature's views**: it may accept or reject them, even if they arrive in time. And if the bill is later amended, a fresh reference is not needed. A state's territory and name can therefore be changed without its consent.

**Article 4** declares that laws made under Articles 2 and 3 are **not amendments of the Constitution** under Article 368. They can be passed by a simple majority, through the ordinary legislative process.

> **Remember:** Parliament's power to diminish a state's area does not cover ceding Indian territory to a foreign country. In the *Berubari Union* case (1960), the Supreme Court held that cession requires a constitutional amendment under Article 368, so the 9th Amendment (1960) was passed to transfer Berubari to Pakistan. Settling a boundary dispute, on the other hand, needs no amendment. The **100th Amendment Act, 2015**, gave effect to the Land Boundary Agreement with Bangladesh: India transferred 111 enclaves and received 51.

## 3. How the map was drawn after 1947

At independence India contained two kinds of political units: the provinces of British India and over 550 princely states. Of the princely states within India's boundaries, all but three, Hyderabad, Junagadh and Kashmir, signed the Instrument of Accession before 15 August 1947. Junagadh was integrated after a plebiscite, Hyderabad through police action, and Kashmir by an Instrument of Accession signed in October 1947. The integration was led by Sardar Patel and V.P. Menon.

The Constitution of 1950 grouped the states into four categories: Part A (the former governors' provinces), Part B (the former princely states with legislatures), Part C (the chief commissioners' provinces and some princely states) and Part D (the Andaman and Nicobar Islands).

### The demand for linguistic states

| Year | Step | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| 1948 | **S.K. Dar Commission** | Recommended reorganisation by administrative convenience, not language |
| 1948–49 | **JVP Committee** (Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, Pattabhi Sitaramayya) | Also rejected language as the basis |
| 1952–53 | **Potti Sriramulu's** death after a 56-day fast for a Telugu state | **Andhra State**, the first linguistic state, created on 1 October 1953 |
| 1953–55 | **Fazl Ali Commission** (Fazl Ali, K.M. Panikkar, H.N. Kunzru) | Broadly accepted language as a basis, but rejected "one language, one state"; national unity came first |
| 1956 | **States Reorganisation Act and the 7th Amendment** | The four-part classification abolished; **14 states and 6 Union territories** from 1 November 1956 |

Several states and territories have also changed their names: Madras became Tamil Nadu (1969), Mysore became Karnataka (1973), the Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands became Lakshadweep (1973), Pondicherry became Puducherry (2006), Uttaranchal became Uttarakhand (2007) and Orissa became Odisha (2011).

> **Mains angle:** Linguistic reorganisation is a classic GS-1 and GS-2 question. Its benefits: it made administration accessible in people's own languages, deepened democracy, and defused secessionist pressures by accommodating identity within the Union. Its risks: regionalism, disputes over boundaries and river waters, and demands for ever-smaller states. Recent creations, Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand, Jharkhand and Telangana, were justified on grounds of development and administration rather than language alone. The Union territories, including Delhi and Jammu and Kashmir, have a note of their own.

## 4. Citizenship under the Constitution

A **citizen** is a full member of the Indian state, with all civil and political rights. An **alien** is a citizen of another country; aliens are either friendly or enemy aliens. The distinction matters because several rights belong only to citizens:

- the Fundamental Rights in Articles 15, 16, 19, 29 and 30;
- the right to vote in elections to the Lok Sabha and the state assemblies (Article 326);
- the right to contest elections to Parliament and the state legislatures;
- eligibility for certain offices: President, Vice-President, judge of the Supreme Court or a High Court, Governor of a state, Attorney General and Advocate General.

### Articles 5 to 11

The Constitution itself identifies only the people who became citizens at its commencement on 26 January 1950. Everything else it leaves to Parliament.

| Article | Who became a citizen at the commencement |
|---|---|
| 5 | A person **domiciled in India** who was born in India, or either of whose parents was born in India, or who had been ordinarily resident in India for five years immediately before the commencement |
| 6 | A person who **migrated from Pakistan**: if before 19 July 1948, by having been ordinarily resident in India since migrating; if on or after that date, by registering after six months' residence. In either case the person, a parent or a grandparent must have been born in undivided India |
| 7 | A person who migrated **to Pakistan** after 1 March 1947 but later returned under a permit for resettlement or permanent return |
| 8 | A **person of Indian origin living abroad**, who registered as a citizen with an Indian diplomatic or consular representative in that country |

The remaining Articles set the rules around these:

- **Article 9:** a person who voluntarily acquires the citizenship of a foreign state is not an Indian citizen.
- **Article 10:** every citizen continues to be a citizen, subject to any law Parliament makes.
- **Article 11:** Parliament has the power to make any law on the acquisition and termination of citizenship. It used this power to enact the **Citizenship Act, 1955**.

## 5. The Citizenship Act, 1955

### Acquiring citizenship

The Act provides five ways to acquire citizenship.

1. **By birth.** A person born in India between 26 January 1950 and 1 July 1987 is a citizen, whatever the parents' nationality. A person born between 1 July 1987 and 3 December 2004 is a citizen if either parent was a citizen at the time of birth. A person born on or after 3 December 2004 is a citizen only if both parents are citizens, or if one is a citizen and the other is not an illegal migrant. The tightening reflects worries about illegal migration.
2. **By descent.** A person born outside India is a citizen by descent if a parent was an Indian citizen at the time of birth. For births on or after 3 December 2004, the birth must also be registered at an Indian consulate within a year (or later, with permission).
3. **By registration.** The central government may register as citizens certain categories, among them persons of Indian origin who have lived in India for seven years, persons married to Indian citizens who have lived in India for seven years, and minor children of Indian citizens.
4. **By naturalisation.** A foreigner who is not an illegal migrant may be naturalised after living in India for 12 months immediately before applying, and for 11 of the 14 years before that.
5. **By incorporation of territory.** When foreign territory becomes part of India, as Puducherry and Goa did, the Government of India specifies who among its people become citizens.

### Losing citizenship

Citizenship can be lost in three ways: by **renunciation**, a voluntary declaration by an adult citizen; by **termination**, which is automatic when a citizen voluntarily acquires another country's citizenship; and by **deprivation**, a compulsory loss ordered by the central government when citizenship was obtained by fraud, or when the citizen has shown disloyalty to the Constitution or traded with the enemy during a war, among other grounds.

> **Prelims pointer:** India does not allow dual citizenship. An Indian who voluntarily acquires another country's citizenship loses Indian citizenship by termination, which is automatic and needs no order. The **Overseas Citizenship of India** (OCI) is not citizenship. OCI cardholders get a lifelong multiple-entry visa and other benefits, but they cannot vote, hold constitutional offices or buy agricultural land. The old Person of Indian Origin (PIO) card was merged into OCI in 2015.

## 6. Who counts as a citizen today: the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019

The Act of 1955 defines an **illegal migrant** as a foreigner who entered India without valid travel documents, or who stayed beyond the permitted period. An illegal migrant cannot acquire citizenship by registration or naturalisation.

The **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019** (CAA) made an exception. Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis and Christians from **Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan** who entered India **on or before 31 December 2014** are no longer treated as illegal migrants. The residence required of them for naturalisation was cut from eleven years to **five**. The Act does not apply to the tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Tripura included in the Sixth Schedule, or to areas covered by the Inner Line Permit system. Its rules were notified on **11 March 2024**.

The CAA has been debated on several grounds:

- **For it:** it protects persecuted religious minorities from three neighbouring Muslim-majority countries; it is a humanitarian measure rooted in partition; and it takes away no one's citizenship.
- **Against it:** it uses religion as a basis for citizenship, which critics say violates Article 14 and the secular character of the Constitution; it leaves out persecuted groups such as Ahmadiyyas, Rohingyas and Tamils from Sri Lanka; and it unsettles the Assam Accord's cut-off date of 1971.

Petitions challenging the CAA's validity have been filed in the Supreme Court.

### Assam: Section 6A and the NRC

Assam has its own rules. The **Assam Accord** of 1985 led to Section 6A of the Citizenship Act. Under it, people of Indian origin who came to Assam from Bangladesh before 1 January 1966 are citizens. Those who came between 1 January 1966 and 24 March 1971 become citizens after registering and waiting ten years. Those who came later are to be detected and deported. On **17 October 2024** a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court **upheld Section 6A by 4 to 1**, holding that it was a legitimate political solution to a unique problem.

The **National Register of Citizens** (NRC) in Assam, updated under the Supreme Court's supervision, published its final list on 31 August 2019. Over 19 lakh applicants were left out.

> **Mains angle:** Citizenship questions sit at the meeting point of identity, security and rights. A good answer uses the constitutional design (single citizenship, with Parliament's power under Article 11) and the CAA and NRC debates (equality, secularism, statelessness, federal concerns in the North-East). It also covers the humanitarian obligations owed to refugees, noting that India has not signed the 1951 Refugee Convention. And it brings in the diaspora angle, where OCI is part of India's soft power. Conclude with the need for a clear refugee law and a fair, transparent process for identifying citizens.

- **Article 1:** "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States". It is a Union, not a federation by agreement, so no state may secede. The "territory of India" means the states, UTs and acquired territories; the "Union of India" means the states only.

- **Article 3:** reorganising states. Needs the President's prior recommendation; the state legislature's views are sought but not binding.
- **Article 4:** laws under Articles 2 and 3 are not amendments under Article 368 (simple majority).
- **Berubari (1960):** ceding territory needs an amendment (the 9th). The 100th Amendment (2015), the Bangladesh land boundary deal: 111 enclaves given, 51 received.
- **Linguistic states:** Dar Commission (1948) and JVP Committee rejected language. Andhra was created on 1 October 1953 (Potti Sriramulu). The Fazl Ali Commission (Panikkar, Kunzru) led to the States Reorganisation Act and 7th Amendment: 14 states and 6 UTs on 1 November 1956.
- **Later changes:** Maharashtra and Gujarat (1960); Nagaland (1963); Haryana (1966); Himachal Pradesh (1971); Manipur, Tripura and Meghalaya (1972); Sikkim (1975); Mizoram, Arunachal and Goa (1987); Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand (2000); Telangana (2014); J&K and Ladakh UTs (31 October 2019); Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu merged (26 January 2020). Now 28 states and 8 UTs.
- **Citizen-only rights:** Articles 15, 16, 19, 29, 30; the vote (Article 326); offices such as President, Vice-President, judges, Governor, Attorney General and Advocate General.
- **Articles 5–8:** citizenship at the commencement (domicile; migrants from Pakistan, with 19 July 1948 the key date; migrants to Pakistan who returned; people of Indian origin abroad). **Article 9:** voluntary foreign citizenship ends Indian citizenship. **Article 11:** Parliament's power, used in the Citizenship Act, 1955.
- **By birth:** before 1 July 1987, any birth in India; from 1 July 1987, one parent a citizen; from 3 December 2004, both parents citizens, or one a citizen and the other not an illegal migrant.
