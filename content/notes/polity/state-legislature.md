## 1. Organisation and Bicameralism in States (Articles 168–169)

Within India's federal parliamentary democracy, **State Legislatures** occupy the decisive operational tier where representative government meets citizens' daily lives. Under the Seventh Schedule, subjects that shape human development and local governance—public order and police, public health and sanitation, agriculture, irrigation, land tenures, local self-government, and state public services—lie squarely within **List II (State List)**, while education, forests, economic planning, and criminal justice fall within **List III (Concurrent List)**. Consequently, the twenty-eight State Legislatures and three Union Territory Legislative Assemblies enact the vast bulk of domestic statutes, sanction cumulative annual public expenditure exceeding ₹58 lakh crore, and hold State Councils of Ministers headed by Chief Ministers to continuous democratic account.

**Part VI, Chapter III (`Articles 168 to 212`)** of the Constitution sets out the organisation, composition, duration, presiding officers, legislative procedure, privileges, and financial control of State Legislatures. While structurally patterned on the Union Parliament (`Articles 79 to 122`), State Legislatures display **crucial constitutional asymmetries**—most notably optional bicameralism, an overriding mechanism without any Joint Sitting, a distinctive five-channel indirect composition for Legislative Councils, stricter State-elector domicile requirements, a dual-floor quorum rule, and gubernatorial reservation of Bills for Presidential consideration. Mastering these exact divergences between Parliament and State Legislatures is one of the highest-yield disciplines for both UPSC Prelims and Mains GS Paper II.

### Constitutional Architecture of the State Legislature (Article 168)

Under **`Article 168(1)`**, *"for every State there shall be a Legislature which shall consist of the Governor,"* together with **either two Houses** (in specified bicameral States) **or one House** (in all other unicameral States). Where a State Legislature has two Houses, one is known as the **Legislative Council (*Vidhan Parishad*)** and the other as the **Legislative Assembly (*Vidhan Sabha*)**; where there is only one House, it is known as the **Legislative Assembly (`Article 168(2)`)**.

Just as the President of India is an integral component of Parliament under `Article 79` despite not being a member of either House, the **Governor is an integral constitutional organ of the State Legislature** under `Article 168(1)`. This integration reflects India's republican adaptation of the Westminster parliamentary principle that law-making is the joint act of the Head of State and the elected Chambers, directly linking the State Executive and State Legislature:

- **Essential Assent to Legislation (`Articles 200–201`):** No Bill passed by the Legislative Assembly (or by both Houses in a bicameral State) can become a binding State Act without the assent of the Governor—or, where reserved, the assent of the President of India.
- **Sessions and Dissolution (`Article 174`):** The Governor summons each House to meet, prorogues either or both Houses, and dissolves the Legislative Assembly—acting on the binding aid and advice of the **Chief Minister and State Council of Ministers** (`Article 163`), except in narrow constitutional contingencies where ministerial majority is in genuine doubt (*Nabam Rebia*, 2016).
- **Addresses and Messages (`Articles 175–176`):** The Governor addresses the Legislature at the commencement of the first session after every general election and the first session of each year (setting out the elected government's policy agenda) and may send messages concerning pending Bills.
- **Financial Gatekeeping (`Articles 202, 203(3), 207`):** Money Bills, Financial Bills Category I, and Demands for Grants cannot be introduced or moved in the Legislative Assembly without the Governor's prior recommendation, and the Governor causes the Annual Financial Statement (State Budget) to be laid before the Legislature.
- **Ordinance-Making Power (`Article 213`):** When the Legislative Assembly (or either House in a bicameral State) is not in session and immediate legislative action is required, the Governor promulgates Ordinances possessing the full force and effect of an Act of the State Legislature, subject to subsequent legislative approval within six weeks of reassembly.
- **Executive Accountability Nexus (`Article 164(2)`):** Anchoring the parliamentary system at the State level, the **Chief Minister and the State Council of Ministers** are **collectively responsible solely to the Legislative Assembly**—never to the Legislative Council—even though a Chief Minister or minister may constitutionally belong to either House (or serve as a non-legislator for up to six consecutive months under `Article 164(4)`).

### Unicameral vs Bicameral States: Why Uniformity Was Rejected

Unlike the Union Parliament—where bicameralism is a permanent, non-negotiable pillar of Indian federalism (`Article 79`)—the Constitution deliberately refuses to impose uniformity across the States. During the **Constituent Assembly Debates** (January 1949), framers diverged sharply over provincial second chambers. Critics, echoing the legacy of the **Government of India Act, 1935** (which had introduced bicameral legislatures in six British provinces: Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Bihar, Assam, and the United Provinces), warned that Legislative Councils were expensive, conservative brakes on socio-economic reform—especially land reforms—and lacked any federal rationale because States are unitary polities internally.

Proponents countered that large, populous, and socially diverse States needed a revising chamber to temper hasty Assembly legislation and offer representation to local bodies, intellectuals, educators, and cooperators. Rather than settling the quarrel permanently from New Delhi, the Drafting Committee led by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar forged a flexible **"opt-in, opt-out" compromise**: large provinces desiring a second chamber received one in `Article 168`, smaller or fiscally constrained States remained unicameral, and **`Article 169`** empowered each State to initiate the creation or abolition of its own Legislative Council whenever local political consensus warranted it.

#### The Current Map of India's 28 States and 3 UT Legislatures

As of **November 2026**, **22 of India's 28 States** operate a **unicameral legislature** consisting solely of the Governor and the Legislative Assembly: Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Chhattisgarh, Goa, Gujarat, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Odisha, Punjab, Rajasthan, Sikkim, Tamil Nadu, Tripura, Uttarakhand, and West Bengal.

Only **6 States** maintain a **bicameral legislature** comprising the Governor, the Legislative Assembly (*Vidhan Sabha*), and the Legislative Council (*Vidhan Parishad*): **Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, and Uttar Pradesh**. Prior to August 2019, **Jammu and Kashmir** stood as the seventh bicameral State, with a 36-member Legislative Council constituted under the separate Constitution of Jammu and Kashmir; however, the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** abolished that Council with effect from **31 October 2019** and restructured Jammu and Kashmir into a Union Territory with a unicameral Legislative Assembly. Consequently, **no Union Territory in India possesses a Legislative Council**—the three Union Territories with legislatures (**National Capital Territory of Delhi**, **Puducherry**, and **Jammu and Kashmir**) are all unicameral.

### Creation and Abolition of Legislative Councils (Article 169)

**`Article 169`** crafts an unusual two-tier federal procedure that deliberately lowers the bar at the parliamentary stage while requiring a broad legislative consensus inside the State concerned. It permits Parliament to **abolish an existing Legislative Council** in a State having such a Council, or to **create a Legislative Council** in a State having no such Council—**if and only if** the concerned State initiates the process.

#### The Two-Stage Constitutional Procedure

1. **Stage 1 — State Legislative Assembly's Special Majority Resolution (`Article 169(1)`):** The **Legislative Assembly** of the State must pass a resolution for creation or abolition of the Council by a **Special Majority**, satisfying two cumulative thresholds simultaneously:
   - a **majority of the total membership** of the Assembly (i.e., strictly more than 50% of total sanctioned seats, irrespective of vacancies or absentees); **AND**
   - a **majority of not less than two-thirds (≥ 2/3rd) of the members of the Assembly present and voting**.
   *(Notice a vital detail: even when abolishing an existing Legislative Council, the **Legislative Council itself has NO vote or veto** on the resolution—the exclusive initiating authority belongs solely to the Legislative Assembly!)*
2. **Stage 2 — Parliamentary Enactment by Simple Majority (`Article 169(1)–(3)`):** Upon receipt of the State Assembly's resolution, **Parliament may by law** provide for the abolition or creation of the Council. This parliamentary Bill is passed like an **ordinary piece of legislation by a SIMPLE MAJORITY** (more than 50% of members present and voting) in both the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha, followed by Presidential assent.
3. **Consequential Provisions and Express Exclusion from `Article 368` (`Article 169(2)–(3)`):** Under `Article 169(2)`, such a parliamentary law contains consequential amendments to the Constitution—specifically adding or deleting the State's name in **`Article 168(1)(a)`**—alongside supplemental provisions amending the **Representation of the People Acts, 1950 and 1951** (fixing total Council seats and constituency allocations). Crucially, **`Article 169(3)`** declares unambiguously that ***"no such law as aforesaid shall be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of Article 368."***

```flow
# Creation or Abolition of a Legislative Council (Article 169)
State Legislative Assembly Initiates (Art. 169(1)) | Passes Resolution by SPECIAL MAJORITY (>50% of Total Membership AND ≥2/3rd of Present & Voting; Council has NO say)
Resolution Sent to Union Government & Parliament | Parliament is NOT Constitutionally Bound to Accept — May Enact, Delay Indefinitely, or Decline
Parliament Enacts Ordinary Law (Simple Majority) | Both Houses Pass Bill by Simple Majority → Presidential Assent (Expressly NOT an Art. 368 Amendment under Art. 169(3)!)
Consequential Amendments & Notification | Art. 168(1)(a) & Third Schedule of RPA 1950 Updated → Council Created or Abolished Upon Presidential/Statutory Commencement Date
```

#### Four Crucial Prelims Traps Inside Article 169

- **Trap 1 — Parliament Cannot Act Suo Motu:** Parliament **cannot** create or abolish a State's Legislative Council on its own motion without a prior Special Majority resolution from that State's Legislative Assembly. *(Exception in historical practice: during comprehensive State reorganisation under **`Articles 3 and 4`**—such as the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, the Bombay Reorganisation Act, 1960, or the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014 creating Telangana's Council—Parliament exercises plenary supplemental powers under `Article 4` to adjust legislative structures of reorganised States.)*
- **Trap 2 — Parliament Is NOT Bound by the Assembly's Resolution:** The word **"may"** in `Article 169(1)` (*"Parliament may by law provide..."*) vests complete constitutional discretion in Parliament. Passing an Assembly resolution neither creates nor abolishes a Council by itself, nor does it legally compel the Union Government to introduce a Bill or bind Parliament to pass it.
- **Trap 3 — Reversed Majority Symmetry:** Aspirants routinely confuse which body uses which majority! Remember: the **State Assembly uses SPECIAL MAJORITY**, whereas **Parliament uses SIMPLE MAJORITY** (mirroring `Articles 2–4` and `Fifth/Sixth Schedule` changes that amend the constitutional text outside `Article 368`).
- **Trap 4 — Presidential Assent & Notification:** Even after Parliament enacts a creation statute, if the Act stipulates that it shall come into force on a date notified by the Central Government (as happened with the Tamil Nadu Legislative Council Act, 2010), the Council does not become operational until that Gazette notification is actually issued.

### Historical Trajectory of Abolitions, Revivals and Pending Proposals (1950–2026)

India's seven-decade experience under `Article 169` reveals a turbulent pattern: Legislative Councils have repeatedly been created, abolished, revived, and targeted again depending less on dispassionate institutional design than on **which political party controlled the Assembly versus the Council**.

- **Early Post-Independence Map & Reorganisation (1950–1960):** At commencement in 1950, seven Part A/B States possessed Councils (Bihar, Bombay, Madras, Punjab, United Provinces/Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, and Mysore/Karnataka). Following linguistic reorganisation, **Andhra Pradesh** established a Council in **1958** (via the Legislative Councils Act, 1957), while the **Bombay Reorganisation Act, 1960** retained a Council for **Maharashtra** while giving newly carved **Gujarat** a unicameral Assembly.
- **Post-1967 Non-Congress Abolitions — West Bengal & Punjab (1969–1970):** After the 1967 elections fractured single-party dominance, newly elected non-Congress governments in West Bengal and Punjab confronted Congress-dominated Upper Houses. Both State Assemblies passed `Article 169` abolition resolutions, leading Parliament to enact the **West Bengal Legislative Council (Abolition) Act, 1969** (abolished **1 August 1969**) and the **Punjab Legislative Council (Abolition) Act, 1969** (abolished **7 January 1970**).
- **The Andhra Pradesh Roller-Coaster (1958 → 1985 → 2007 → 2020–2021):** No State illustrates partisan oscillation better than Andhra Pradesh. In **1985**, Chief Minister N.T. Rama Rao's Telugu Desam Party (TDP)—frustrated by a Congress-majority Council—secured abolition via the **Andhra Pradesh Legislative Council (Abolition) Act, 1985** (effective **1 June 1985**). Two decades later, following a July 2004 resolution under Congress CM Y.S. Rajasekhara Reddy, Parliament passed the **Andhra Pradesh Legislative Council Act, 2005**, formally reviving the Council in **March–April 2007** (with 90 seats, later bifurcated into **58 seats for residuary Andhra Pradesh**—raised from 50 by a 2015 amendment—and **40 seats for Telangana** in 2014). Yet on **27 January 2020**, Chief Minister Y.S. Jagan Mohan Reddy's YSRCP government passed a fresh `Article 169` resolution to **abolish** the Council after the TDP-majority Upper House referred its three-capital Decentralisation Bills to a Select Committee. Less than two years later—once the YSRCP gained numerical control of the Council—the very same Assembly on **23 November 2021** **withdrew its 2020 abolition resolution**, keeping the Council alive!
- **Tamil Nadu's Unresolved Flip-Flop (1986 → 2010 → 2011):** In **1986**, Chief Minister M.G. Ramachandran's AIADMK government secured abolition of India's oldest provincial upper chamber (dating back to the Madras Legislative Council of 1861) through the **Tamil Nadu Legislative Council (Abolition) Act, 1986** (effective **1 November 1986**). In April 2010, M. Karunanidhi's DMK government passed a revival resolution, prompting Parliament to enact the **Tamil Nadu Legislative Council Act, 2010** (May 2010) for a 78-member House. Before notification, however, J. Jayalalithaa's AIADMK returned to power in May 2011 and passed a fresh Assembly resolution withdrawing the request; the 2010 Act remains unnotified to this day.
- **Jammu and Kashmir (1957–2019):** Created under the 1957 Constitution of Jammu and Kashmir as a 36-member chamber, J&K's Council stood abolished upon enactment of the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** (effective 31 October 2019).

### Mains Analysis: Utility vs Redundancy of State Legislative Councils

In GS Paper II, UPSC repeatedly asks candidates to evaluate whether the State Legislative Council serves as a constructive **"revising chamber"** or has degenerated into a **"costly anachronism"** and **"dilatory ornament"**. A balanced Mains answer weighs both constitutional merits and empirical pathologies before anchoring reforms in authoritative committee reports.

#### Arguments Defending Legislative Councils (The Case for Bicameralism)

1. **Check Against Hasty and Populist Legislation:** With State Assemblies averaging barely 24 sitting days per year and clearing roughly 30% of Bills on the very day of introduction (PRS 2026), a second chamber provides structured cooling-off time for sober second thought, public feedback, and Select Committee scrutiny.
2. **Functional Representation of Non-Electoral Talent:** Direct first-past-the-post Assembly elections reward mass mobilisation, caste arithmetic, and campaign funding, often deterring scientists, jurists, educators, artists, and grassroots social workers. `Article 171(3)` reserves explicit channels for graduates, teachers, local-body representatives, and Governor-nominated domain experts from five spheres (including the cooperative movement).
3. **Institutional Voice for Local Self-Governments:** Because roughly **one-third of MLCs** are elected by municipalities, zila parishads, and district bodies, the Vidhan Parishad creates an organic institutional link between the third tier (73rd and 74th Amendments) and State-level law-making—a bridge absent in unicameral States.
4. **Reducing Workload and Enhancing Deliberative Quality:** Non-controversial or technical Bills can originate in the Legislative Council, easing legislative bottlenecks in large States like Uttar Pradesh (population >24 crore) or Maharashtra, while continuous existence preserves institutional memory across Assembly dissolutions.
5. **Accommodating Regional and Marginalised Voices:** In sprawling, regionally diverse States (such as Vidarbha/Marathwada in Maharashtra, Bundelkhand/Purvanchal in UP, or Kalyana-Karnataka), indirect Council seats allow parties and Governors to balance regional, linguistic, and minority representation without altering delimited Assembly maps.

#### Arguments Criticising Legislative Councils (The Case Against Bicameralism)

1. **Toothless Constitutional Powers ("Dilatory, Not Revising"):** Unlike the Rajya Sabha, a Legislative Council enjoys **no Joint Sitting**, **no role in Constitutional Amendments**, **no vote in Presidential elections**, and a capped **four-month maximum delay (3 + 1)** on Ordinary Bills (`Article 197`). Critics argue that a House so structurally weak cannot meaningfully check a determined Assembly majority—it merely delays inevitable passage.
2. **"Rehabilitation Home for Defeated Politicians":** In political practice, the MLA-elected one-third quota—and even Governor nominations—are routinely deployed by ruling and opposition parties alike as a **backdoor entry for candidates rejected by voters in Assembly polls**, wealthy party financiers, or factional loyalists needing a six-month-safe ministerial berth (including Chief Ministers who skip direct electoral tests).
3. **Heavy Recurring Burden on State Exchequers:** Maintaining a second Secretariat, legislative hall, presiding officers, committees, staff, salaries, pensions, and Constituency Development Funds costs cash-strapped States tens of crores annually—diverting scarce fiscal space away from education, healthcare, and capital expenditure.
4. **Partisan Football Under Article 169:** Because creation and abolition follow partisan swings (Andhra Pradesh 1985/2007/2020/2021; Tamil Nadu 1986/2010/2011; West Bengal 1969/2021), Councils suffer chronic institutional insecurity, undermining long-term legislative stature.
5. **Anachronistic Electorates (Graduates and Teachers):** Critics—including the **Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2nd ARC)**—point out that reserving separate electorates for graduates made sense in 1950 when literacy was under 20%, but has lost its *raison d'être* in a mass-higher-education democracy; similarly, singling out only one profession (teaching) while excluding doctors, engineers, farmers, and industrial workers appears constitutionally arbitrary.

> [!debate]
> **Reforming Bicameralism: Second ARC (2007) & Parliamentary Standing Committee (2013–2014)**
> Rather than treating Legislative Councils as an all-or-nothing binary, two landmark official bodies proposed structural redesign:
> - **Second Administrative Reforms Commission (6th Report, *Local Governance*, Oct 2007):** Recommended that Parliament constitute a Legislative Council in **every State**, but **refashion it as a genuine Chamber of Local Governments** composed predominantly of members elected by Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) and Urban Local Bodies (ULBs), while abolishing separate graduates' and teachers' constituencies as outdated in a modern diversified economy.
> - **Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice (63rd Report on Rajasthan, Dec 2013 & 70th Report on Assam, Feb 2014):** Deplored that bicameralism currently depends on *"the mood of the government of the day."* It urged the Union Government to evolve a **uniform National Policy on Legislative Councils** setting objective criteria (population, fiscal capacity, legislative workload), fixing a **time limit** for Parliament to decide `Article 169` resolutions, reviewing graduates'/teachers' quotas, and expanding local-body representation.

## 2. Composition, Duration and Membership (Articles 170–173, 188–193)

While `Articles 168–169` determine the outer architecture of a State Legislature, **`Articles 170–173` and `188–195`** govern its human core: how large each House may be, how voters and indirect electorates choose representatives, how reservations promote social and gender inclusion, how long each House endures, who is eligible to sit, what triggers disqualification or seat vacation, and what penalties guard legislative sanctity. UPSC Prelims targets the **micro-level statutory and constitutional contrasts** in this cluster with surgical precision.

### Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha): Composition, Exceptions and Duration (Articles 170, 172, 332–334A)

The **Legislative Assembly (*Vidhan Sabha*)** is the directly elected popular House of the State—the functional counterpart of the Lok Sabha. Every executive mandate in the State flows from its floor.

#### General Strength Formula and Constitutional Exceptions (`Article 170(1)`)

Under **`Article 170(1)`**, subject to the provisions of `Article 333` (now lapsed), the Legislative Assembly of each State shall consist of **not more than 500** and **not less than 60** members chosen by **direct election** from territorial constituencies in the State on the basis of **universal adult suffrage** (`Article 326`, voting age lowered from 21 to 18 by the **`61st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1988`**) under the **First-Past-the-Post (FPTP)** system.

Among India's 28 States, **Uttar Pradesh** possesses the largest Legislative Assembly (**403 seats**), followed by **West Bengal (294)**, **Maharashtra (288)**, **Bihar (243)**, **Tamil Nadu (234)**, **Madhya Pradesh (230)**, and **Karnataka (224)**. Because enforcing a 60-seat floor in demographically small States would produce absurdly tiny constituencies and disproportionate administrative costs, **Part XXI (`Articles 371A` and `371F–371I`)** carves out **five explicit constitutional exceptions** permitting lower minimum floors, alongside statutory strengths for the three Union Territories with Assemblies:

#### Territorial Constituencies, Population Ratio and the Delimitation Freeze (`Article 170(2)–(3)`)

To safeguard equal voting weight across citizens within a State, **`Article 170(2)`** mandates that each State be divided into territorial constituencies such that the **ratio between the population of each constituency and the number of seats allotted to it shall, so far as practicable, be the same throughout the State**.

Following each decennial Census, **`Article 170(3)`** requires readjustment of both (a) total seats in each State's Assembly and (b) division of the State into territorial constituencies by an authority prescribed by Parliament (**Delimitation Commission**—constituted in **1952, 1962, 1972, and 2002**, with its orders final and non-justiciable under `Article 329(a)`). However, to prevent States that successfully stabilised population growth (principally southern States) from losing political representation relative to faster-growing northern States, three constitutional amendments froze seat totals while refreshing internal boundaries:

- **`42nd Amendment Act, 1976`:** Froze total Assembly seat allocations across States at the **1971 Census** level until the year 2000.
- **`84th Amendment Act, 2001`:** Extended the freeze on total State Assembly seats at the **1971 Census** for another twenty-five years—**until the relevant figures for the first Census taken after the year 2026 have been published**—while initially allowing internal constituency rationalisation on the 1991 Census.
- **`87th Amendment Act, 2003`:** Upgraded internal constituency delimitation (and SC/ST seat readjustment without altering a State's total seats) to the **2001 Census** figures, which formed the basis of the **Delimitation Commission of 2002–2008** (Chaired by Justice Kuldip Singh).

#### Reservations in State Assemblies: SC/ST (`Art. 332`), End of Anglo-Indian Nomination (`Art. 333`) and Women's Reservation (`Art. 332A`)

1. **Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (`Article 332` & `Article 334`):** Seats are reserved for SCs and STs in every State Legislative Assembly in proportion to their population share in that State. Originally fixed for ten years (until 1960), this political reservation has been extended every decade—most recently by the **`104th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019`**, which extended SC/ST seat reservations in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies for another ten years until **25 January 2030** (80 years from constitutional commencement).
2. **Abolition of Anglo-Indian Nomination (`Article 333` & `104th Amendment Act, 2019`):** Originally, `Article 333` empowered the Governor to **nominate ONE member** of the Anglo-Indian community to the State Legislative Assembly if the community was not adequately represented. While extending SC/ST reservations, the **`104th Amendment Act, 2019`** deliberately **allowed the Anglo-Indian reservation under `Article 334(b)` to lapse on 25 January 2020**, ending all Governor-nominated seats in State Legislative Assemblies across India (mirroring the simultaneous abolition of the two Presidential Anglo-Indian nominees in the Lok Sabha under `Article 331`).
3. **One-Third Women's Reservation (`106th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2023` — *Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam*):** Enacted on **28 September 2023**, the `106th Amendment` inserted **`Article 332A`** (for State Legislative Assemblies, alongside `Article 330A` for Lok Sabha and `Article 239AA(2)(b)` amendment for NCT of Delhi) and **`Article 334A`** (commencement and sunset):
   - **Horizontal One-Third Quota (`Article 332A(1) & (3)`):** Reserves **as nearly as may be, one-third (33%)** of the total number of directly elected seats in the Legislative Assembly of every State for women.
   - **Sub-Quota Within SC/ST Seats (`Article 332A(2)`):** Reserves **one-third** of the seats already reserved for SCs and STs under `Article 332(3)` specifically for women belonging to SCs and STs (included within, not over and above, the overall 33% women's share). There is **no separate OBC sub-quota** within `Article 332A`.
   - **No Quota in Legislative Councils:** Crucially for Prelims, **`Article 332A` applies ONLY to directly elected State Legislative Assemblies (and Lok Sabha & Delhi Assembly)**—it does **NOT** apply to **State Legislative Councils** or the **Rajya Sabha**!
   - **2026 Commencement & Delimitation Trigger (`Article 334A`):** On **16 April 2026** (via Gazette notification `S.O. 1922(E)`), the Union Ministry of Law and Justice formally notified the commencement of the `106th Amendment Act, 2023`. However, under **`Article 334A(1)`**, actual seat reservation on the ground **comes into effect only after a delimitation exercise undertaken after the relevant figures for the first Census taken after commencement have been published**, and will operate for **15 years** (extendable by Parliament by law), with reserved constituencies rotating after each subsequent delimitation (`Article 334A(3)`). *(Note: The Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026, which sought to modify the delimitation linkage for earlier roll-out alongside expanding Lok Sabha seats, failed to clear the Lok Sabha special-majority vote on **17 April 2026**.)*

#### Duration of the Legislative Assembly (`Article 172(1)`)

Unlike the Legislative Council, the Legislative Assembly is **not a continuing chamber**. Under **`Article 172(1)`**, its normal term is **five years from the date appointed for its first meeting** (NOT from the date of declaration of election results or swearing-in of the Chief Minister!). Upon the expiration of five years, the Assembly stands **automatically dissolved**. However, two constitutional exceptions alter this normal term:
- **Earlier Dissolution:** The Governor may dissolve the Assembly **at any time before the completion of five years** (`Article 174(2)(b)`), normally on the advice of the Chief Minister enjoying a majority, or during a breakdown of constitutional machinery (`Article 356`).
- **Extension During National Emergency (`Proviso to Article 172(1)`):** While a Proclamation of **National Emergency (`Article 352`)** is in operation, **Parliament** (NOT the State Legislature and NOT the Governor!) may by law extend the normal five-year term of a State Legislative Assembly for a period **not exceeding ONE YEAR at a time** (any number of times), **and NOT extending in any case beyond a period of SIX MONTHS after the Proclamation has ceased to operate**. *(Historical note: The `42nd Amendment, 1976` briefly raised the normal term of both Lok Sabha and State Assemblies from 5 to 6 years, before the `44th Amendment, 1978` restored the original 5-year term.)*

### Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad): Strength, Five-Part Composition and Duration (Articles 171–172)

Unlike the popularly elected Vidhan Sabha, the **Legislative Council (*Vidhan Parishad*)** is a **continuing, indirectly constituted revising chamber** whose membership blends local-government delegates, legislators' nominees, educated electorates, educators, and gubernatorial nominees.

#### Maximum and Minimum Strength (`Article 171(1)`)

Under **`Article 171(1)`**, the total number of members in the Legislative Council of a State having such a Council:
- shall **NOT exceed one-third (1/3rd)** of the total number of members in the Legislative Assembly of that State; **AND**
- shall **in NO case be less than 40**!

Thus, while the Constitution fixes both an upper ceiling (proportional to Assembly size) and an absolute lower floor (**40**, where Telangana currently sits), the **actual strength** of each Council within those bounds is fixed by **Parliament by law** (Third Schedule of the RPA, 1950). *(Historical Prelims exception: Prior to 2019, Jammu and Kashmir's Council had **36 members** because it functioned under the State's own separate Constitution rather than `Article 171`.)*

#### Exact Five-Part Composition Formula (`Article 171(2)–(5)`)

A classic constitutional subtlety sits inside **`Article 171(2)`**: the Constitution explicitly stipulates that the composition set out in `Article 171(3)` is **tentative**—operative ***"until Parliament by law otherwise provides."*** In practice, Parliament has retained the five-channel structure while calibrating exact seat counts in the Third Schedule of the RPA, 1950 (for example, Uttar Pradesh has 10 nominated seats out of 100 rather than a strict 1/6th, and Maharashtra has 30 MLA-elected vs 22 local-body seats). Nevertheless, UPSC Prelims consistently tests the exact **`Article 171(3)`** constitutional fractions: **five-sixths (5/6th) are indirectly elected** and **one-sixth (1/6th) are nominated by the Governor**. All elections under the four elected channels are held in accordance with the system of **proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote (PR-STV)** (`Article 171(4)`), and territorial constituencies for local bodies, graduates, and teachers are delimited by **Parliament by law** (`Delimitation of Council Constituencies Orders` under the RPA, 1950).

Furthermore, courts cannot scrutinise the *merits* or subjective adequacy of a nominee's distinction in those five fields unless patent illegality or non-eligibility is shown (*Har Sharan Verma*, 1985).

#### Duration and Staggered Continuity (`Article 172(2)`)

Mirroring the Rajya Sabha (`Article 83(1)`), **`Article 172(2)`** declares that the **Legislative Council shall NOT be subject to dissolution**, making it a **permanent, continuing chamber**. As nearly as possible, **one-third (1/3rd) of its members retire on the expiration of every second year**, in accordance with provisions made by **Parliament by law**.

Notice a prized Prelims fact: just as the six-year term of a Rajya Sabha MP is fixed by Section 154 of the RPA, 1951 rather than the Constitution itself, the **six-year term of a Member of the Legislative Council (MLC)** is likewise fixed by **Section 156 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951**—NOT written directly into `Article 172(2)`! Vacant seats are filled by fresh elections and gubernatorial nominations at the start of every third year; retiring MLCs remain eligible for re-election and re-nomination any number of times; and a member elected/nominated to a **casual vacancy** serves only for the **unexpired remainder** of the predecessor's term.

### Qualifications for Membership: The Crucial State-Domicile Distinction (Article 173 & RPA 1951)

To become a member of a State Legislature, a candidate must satisfy **both constitutional qualifications (`Article 173`)** and **statutory qualifications enacted by Parliament (`Sections 5 and 6` of the Representation of the People Act, 1951)**.

#### Constitutional Qualifications (`Article 173`)

1. **Citizenship (`Article 173(a)`):** Must be a **citizen of India** (by birth or naturalisation).
2. **Pre-Election Oath or Affirmation (`Article 173(a)` & Third Schedule):** Must make and subscribe before a person authorised by the **Election Commission of India** (normally the Returning Officer) an oath or affirmation according to the form set out in the **Third Schedule**—swearing to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution and to **uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India** *(these closing words were inserted by the **`16th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963`** on the recommendation of the National Integration Committee)*.
3. **Minimum Age (`Article 173(b)`):**
   - **Not less than 25 years of age** for a seat in the **Legislative Assembly**;
   - **Not less than 30 years of age** for a seat in the **Legislative Council**.
4. **Parliamentary Qualifications (`Article 173(c)`):** Must possess such other qualifications as may be prescribed in that behalf by or under any law made by **Parliament** (NOT the State Legislature!).

#### Statutory Qualifications (`Sections 5 & 6`, RPA 1951) — The Huge Domicile Contrast With Rajya Sabha!

Exercising its exclusive authority under `Article 173(c)`, Parliament laid down three decisive statutory conditions in the **Representation of the People Act, 1951**:

1. **Mandatory State Elector Rule for Legislative Assembly (`Section 5(c)`):** A person shall not be qualified to be chosen to fill a seat in the **Legislative Assembly of a State** unless they are an **elector for ANY Assembly constituency IN THAT STATE**! *(Contrast sharply with the **Lok Sabha** under `Section 4(d)`, where a candidate may be an elector in any parliamentary constituency anywhere in India!)*
2. **Mandatory State Elector / Residence Rule for Legislative Council (`Section 6`):**
   - For **elected MLC seats** (`Section 6(1)`), the candidate **MUST be an elector for an Assembly constituency IN THAT VERY STATE**!
   - For **Governor-nominated MLC seats** (`Section 6(2)`), the nominee **MUST be ordinarily resident IN THAT STATE**!
   - **Classic UPSC Prelims Contrast:** In **2003**, Parliament amended `Section 3` of the RPA, 1951 to **ABOLISH the State-domicile requirement for Rajya Sabha candidates** (upheld by a 5-judge Constitution Bench in ***Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India*, 2006**), allowing any Indian voter registered anywhere in India to contest a Rajya Sabha seat from any State. **Parliament made NO such relaxation for State Legislatures!** Thus, to contest a Vidhan Sabha or Vidhan Parishad seat in Karnataka or Uttar Pradesh, the candidate **must still be a registered elector in that specific State**!
3. **Reserved Seat Eligibility (`Section 5(a)–(b)`):** A candidate contesting a seat reserved for **Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes** in a State Assembly must belong to an SC or ST of **that State** (and be an elector in any Assembly constituency of that State). However, an SC or ST candidate is completely free to contest any **unreserved (general)** seat in that State.

### Disqualifications and the Bifurcated Deciding Authority (Articles 191–192 & Tenth Schedule)

Mirrored closely on `Articles 102–103` for Parliament, disqualifications for State legislators operate along **three independent legal tracks**: constitutional grounds (`Article 191(1)`), statutory grounds enacted by Parliament (`RPA, 1951`), and anti-defection grounds (`Article 191(2)` read with the Tenth Schedule).

#### 1. Constitutional Disqualifications (`Article 191(1)`)

A person is disqualified for **being chosen as, and for being**, a member of the Legislative Assembly or Legislative Council of a State if they:
1. **Hold an Office of Profit (`Article 191(1)(a)`):** Hold any office of profit under the **Government of India OR the Government of any State**, other than an office declared by the **Legislature of the State by law** not to disqualify its holder.
   - **Constitutional Carve-out (`Article 191(2)` Explanation / `Article 191(1)` Explanation):** A person shall **NOT** be deemed to hold an office of profit merely because they are a **Minister** either for the Union or for any State.
   - **Judicial Test (*Jaya Bachchan v. Union of India*, 2006):** What matters is whether the office is **capable of yielding pecuniary gain or profit** (honorarium, perks, emoluments), irrespective of whether the holder actually draws the money!
   - **Parliamentary Secretaries Bar:** Several States attempted to bypass the **`91st Amendment's` 15% ministerial cap (`Article 164(1A)`)** by appointing ruling-party MLAs as "Parliamentary Secretaries" with ministerial rank and exempting them via State Prevention of Disqualification laws. In ***Bimolangshu Roy v. State of Assam* (2017)**, the Supreme Court struck down Assam's Parliamentary Secretaries Act as unconstitutional—holding that States lack legislative competence to create quasi-ministerial offices undermining `Article 164(1A)`—and in 2018 the ECI recommended disqualifying 20 Delhi AAP MLAs (subsequently remanded on procedural hearing grounds).
2. **Unsound Mind (`Article 191(1)(b)`):** Are of unsound mind and stand so declared by a **competent court**.
3. **Undischarged Insolvent (`Article 191(1)(c)`):** Are an undischarged insolvent.
4. **Non-Citizenship or Foreign Allegiance (`Article 191(1)(d)`):** Are not a citizen of India, or have voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign State, or are under any acknowledgement of allegiance or adherence to a foreign State.
5. **Disqualification Under Parliamentary Law (`Article 191(1)(e)`):** Are so disqualified by or under any law made by **Parliament** *(again, NOT the State Legislature—uniformity of electoral disqualifications across India is a Union preserve!)*.

#### 2. Statutory Disqualifications Under the Representation of the People Act, 1951

Under **`Sections 8 to 11A` of the RPA, 1951**, Parliament prescribes six major statutory disqualifications applying identically to MPs, MLAs, and MLCs:
1. **Electoral Offences or Corrupt Practices (`Section 8A`):** Found guilty of specified election offences or corrupt practices (disqualification up to **6 years** fixed by the President after consulting ECI).
2. **Criminal Convictions (`Section 8`):**
   - `Section 8(1)`: Conviction for specified grave offences (enmity between groups, bribery, rape, untouchability, UAPA, Prevention of Corruption Act, NDPS, etc.) triggers disqualification for **6 years** from fine-only conviction or **sentence + 6 years** upon imprisonment.
   - `Section 8(2)`: Conviction with at least **6 months' imprisonment** for hoarding, profiteering, food/drug adulteration, or dowry offences triggers **sentence + 6 years** disqualification.
   - `Section 8(3)`: Conviction for **any other offence** carrying imprisonment of **2 years or more** triggers **immediate disqualification** from the date of conviction plus **6 years after release**. Following ***Lily Thomas v. Union of India* (2013)** (striking down `Section 8(4)'s` three-month grace period for sitting legislators) and ***Lok Prahari v. ECI* (2018)**, only an appellate court's explicit **stay on the conviction itself**—not mere bail or stay of sentence—suspends disqualification! *(Classic Prelims Trap: **Preventive detention** under NSA or similar laws is **NOT** a disqualification under `Section 8`!)*
   - **Chief Minister / Non-Legislator Impact (*B.R. Kapur v. State of Tamil Nadu*, 2001 — 5-Judge Bench):** A person disqualified under `Section 8(3)` of the RPA, 1951 **cannot even be sworn in as Chief Minister under the 6-month non-legislator clause (`Article 164(4)`)**!
3. **Failure to Lodge Election Expense Accounts (`Section 10A`):** Disqualified by the ECI for **3 years** for failing to submit campaign expenditure accounts without justification.
4. **Subsisting Government Contracts (`Section 9A`):** Holding a personal/business interest in a subsisting contract with the appropriate government for supply of goods or execution of works.
5. **Office in Government Corporations (`Section 10`):** Serving as managing agent, manager, or secretary of a corporation (excluding cooperative societies) where the government holds **at least 25% share**.
6. **Dismissal for Corruption or Disloyalty (`Section 9`):** Dismissed from government service for corruption or disloyalty to the State—disqualified for **5 years** from dismissal.

#### 3. Disqualification on Ground of Defection (`Article 191(2)` & Tenth Schedule)

Inserted by the **`52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985`** (and tightened by the **`91st Amendment Act, 2003`**, which deleted the 1/3rd split loophole while preserving the ≥2/3rd merger clause and adding **`Article 164(1B)`** barring defectors from ministerial office), **`Article 191(2)`** disqualifies an MLA or MLC on four Tenth Schedule grounds: (i) voluntarily giving up membership of the political party (*Ravi S. Naik*, 1994—inferred even from outside-House conduct); (ii) voting or abstaining contrary to a party whip without prior permission and uncondoned within 15 days; (iii) an **independent** legislator joining any political party after election; or (iv) a **nominated** legislator (MLC) joining any political party **after six months** from taking their seat. Crucially, under **Paragraph 5**, a Speaker, Deputy Speaker, Chairman, or Deputy Chairman who voluntarily resigns party membership upon election to preserve neutrality is exempt.

#### The Critical Institutional Split in Deciding Authorities (`Article 192` vs Tenth Schedule)

- **Post-Election `Article 191(1)` & RPA Disqualifications (`Article 192`):** Decided by the **Governor**, whose decision is final (subject to judicial review). Under **`Article 192(2)`**, before taking any decision, **the Governor SHALL obtain the opinion of the Election Commission of India (ECI) and SHALL act according to such opinion!** Notice two lethal Prelims traps here: (a) the Governor acts on the binding opinion of the **ECI**—**NOT the State Election Commission (SEC)** (which handles only Panchayats and Municipalities under `Arts. 243K/243ZA`) and **NOT the State Council of Ministers**; and (b) `Article 192` applies **strictly to disqualifications arising *after* election** (*Election Commission v. Saka Venkata Rao*, 1953)!
- **Pre-Election Disqualifications (`Article 329(b)` & RPA 1951):** If a candidate was already disqualified on nomination/election day, only the concerned **High Court** can adjudicate it via an **election petition** under the RPA, 1951—not the Governor!
- **Tenth Schedule Defection (`Paragraph 6`):** Decided exclusively by the **Speaker** (for MLAs) or the **Chairman** (for MLCs)—**NOT** the Governor and **NOT** the ECI—acting as a judicial Tribunal reviewable by High Courts and the Supreme Court (*Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992; *Keisham Meghachandra*, 2020; *Padi Kaushik Reddy*, 2025).

> [!prelims]
> **Four High-Yield UPSC Prelims Traps on Articles 170–193**
> 1. **ECI, NEVER State Election Commission (`Art. 192`):** Under `Article 192(2)`, the Governor seeks the binding opinion of the **Election Commission of India (ECI)**—NOT the State Election Commission! The SEC has **zero jurisdiction** over State Assembly or Council elections or disqualifications.
> 2. **State Legislature vs Parliament on Qualifications & Disqualifications:** **Parliament** prescribes statutory qualifications (`Art. 173(c)`) and disqualifications (`Art. 191(1)(e)`) for State legislators—**EXCEPT** that the **State Legislature** declares which offices are exempt from **"office of profit"** under `Article 191(1)(a)`!
> 3. **Domicile Contrast (`RPA 1951`):** Rajya Sabha candidates need **NO State domicile** (since 2003); State **MLA and MLC candidates MUST be registered electors in THAT State** (or ordinarily resident for Governor's MLC nominees)!
> 4. **Women's Quota Exclusion (`106th Amendment`, notified 16 Apr 2026):** `Article 332A` grants 33% women's reservation in **State Legislative Assemblies** (including 1/3rd within SC/ST quotas), **NOT** in **State Legislative Councils**!

### Double Membership, Vacation of Seats, Oath, Penalty and Salaries (Articles 188–190, 193, 195)

To prevent dual representation, prolonged absenteeism, or unsworn participation in law-making, **`Articles 188, 190, 193, and 195`** lay down strict rules governing legislative seats.

#### Vacation of Seats (`Article 190`)

A member of a State Legislature vacates their seat in **five distinct circumstances**:

1. **Double Membership (`Article 190(1)–(2)` & RPA 1951 / Prohibition of Simultaneous Membership Rules, 1950):**
   - **Parliament + State Legislature (`Article 101(2)` & `Rule 2`, 1950 Rules):** If a person is elected to **both a House of Parliament and a House of a State Legislature**, they must resign their seat in the State Legislature within **14 days**; if they fail to do so, **their seat in Parliament becomes vacant**! *(Mark that reversal carefully: under `Article 101(2)` and the Prohibition of Simultaneous Membership Rules, 1950, failure to resign within 14 days vacates the **PARLIAMENTARY** seat, NOT the State seat!)*
   - **Both Houses of the Same State Legislature (`Article 190(1)`):** No person shall be a member of both the Legislative Assembly and the Legislative Council of a State; the **State Legislature by law** provides for vacation of one seat (normally, failure to intimate choice within **10 days** vacates the **Legislative Council** seat—mirroring `Section 68` of RPA 1951; and if a sitting member of one House is elected to the other, the seat in the **first House** becomes vacant automatically).
   - **Legislatures of Two or More States (`Article 190(2)` & `Rule 3`, 1950 Rules):** If elected to the Legislatures of two or more States simultaneously, unless the member resigns all but one within the prescribed period (**10 days** under the 1950 Rules), **seats in ALL those State Legislatures become vacant**!
   - **Two Seats in the Same House (`Section 70`, RPA 1951):** If elected from two constituencies in the same Assembly, the member must resign one seat within **14 days**; otherwise **BOTH seats become vacant**!
2. **Disqualification (`Article 190(3)(a)`):** If a sitting member incurs any constitutional, statutory, or Tenth Schedule disqualification under `Article 191`, their seat immediately becomes vacant.
3. **Voluntary Resignation (`Article 190(3)(b)` — `33rd Amendment Act, 1974`):** A member may resign by writing under their own hand addressed to the **Speaker** (in the Assembly) or the **Chairman** (in the Council). Crucially, the **`33rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1974`** inserted a proviso empowering the Speaker or Chairman **NOT to accept a resignation** if, from information received and after making such inquiry as deemed fit, they are satisfied that **the resignation is NOT voluntary or genuine** (preventing coerced resignations during political defection crises).
4. **Continuous Absence Without Permission (`Article 190(4)`):** A House **may declare the seat of a member vacant** if, for a period of **60 days**, the member is absent from all meetings of the House without its permission. In computing this 60-day period, **no account is taken of any period during which the House is prorogued or is adjourned for more than four consecutive days**. *(Notice two critical points: first, vacation is **discretionary**—the House "may" declare it vacant, it is not automatic; second, as the Supreme Court stressed in ***Ashish Shelar* (2022)**, suspending an MLA beyond 60 sitting days across multiple sessions effectively triggers constitutional seat vacation and disenfranchises an entire constituency!)*
5. **Other Constitutional/Judicial Vacations:** A seat also falls vacant if the High Court/Supreme Court declares the election **void**, if the House validly **expels** the member (subject to proportionality review under *Dr. Sunil Kumar Singh*, 2025), or if the member is elected **President or Vice-President** or appointed **Governor** (`Article 158(1)`).

#### Oath or Affirmation (`Article 188`), Daily Penalty (`Article 193`) and Salaries (`Article 195`)

- **Oath Before Taking Seat (`Article 188`):** Every MLA and MLC must, before taking their seat, make and subscribe an oath or affirmation (in the Third Schedule form) before the **Governor**, or some person appointed in that behalf by the Governor (conventionally the **Speaker *pro tem*** for newly elected MLAs). Until taking the oath, an elected legislator **cannot vote or participate in House proceedings**, does not become eligible for legislative privileges under `Article 194`, and cannot draw legislative allowances. *(Note the contrast: `Article 173(a)` is the pre-nomination **candidate oath** before the ECI-authorised Returning Officer; `Article 188` is the post-election **member oath** before the Governor or designate!)*
- **Penalty for Unqualified Sitting or Voting (`Article 193`):** If any person sits or votes as a member of a State Assembly or Council (a) before taking the `Article 188` oath, (b) knowing they are not qualified or are disqualified, or (c) knowing they are prohibited from doing so by any parliamentary or State law, they become liable to a penalty of **₹500 for every day** on which they so sit or vote, recoverable as a debt due to the State (mirroring `Article 104` for Parliament).
- **Salaries and Allowances (`Article 195`):** Members of the Legislative Assembly and Legislative Council receive such salaries and allowances as may from time to time be determined by the **Legislature of the State by law** (NOT by Parliament and NOT fixed in the Constitution!). Like Parliament (`Article 106`), the Constitution makes **no provision for legislative pensions**—State Legislatures confer MLA/MLC pensions purely through ordinary State statutes, resulting in wide inter-State disparities across India.

> [!example]
> **How Double-Membership Rules Play Out in Real Politics**
> During the **2023–2024 election cycle**, several sitting Lok Sabha MPs—including Narendra Singh Tomar, Prahlad Singh Patel, and Diya Kumari—contested and won State Assembly elections in Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan. Under **`Article 101(2)`** and the **Prohibition of Simultaneous Membership Rules, 1950**, had they not resigned their Lok Sabha seats within **14 days** of the Assembly results notification, their **Parliamentary seats** (NOT their newly won Assembly seats!) would have automatically fallen vacant—the exact opposite of the trap many UPSC aspirants guess!

## 3. Presiding Officers, Sessions and Legislative Procedure (Articles 174–212)

Once a State Legislature is constituted, its constitutional vitality hinges on neutral **Presiding Officers** (`Articles 178–187`), regular **sessions and quorum safeguards** (`Articles 174–177, 189`), disciplined **law-making pathways** (`Articles 196–201`), and rigorous **financial oversight** (`Articles 202–207`). It is precisely inside this procedural machinery that State Legislatures diverge most sharply from Parliament—especially regarding the **Chairman's membership and voting status**, the **dual-floor quorum threshold**, the **absence of a Joint Sitting**, the **three-plus-one-month cap** on Legislative Council review, and **gubernatorial reservation of State Bills for the President**.

### Presiding Officers of the State Legislature (Articles 178–187)

Every House of a State Legislature has its own pair of elected presiding officers: a **Speaker** and **Deputy Speaker** in the Legislative Assembly (`Article 178`), and a **Chairman** and **Deputy Chairman** in the Legislative Council (`Article 182`). Unlike the Lok Sabha–Rajya Sabha asymmetry at the Centre, **all four presiding officers in a State Legislature are elected members of their respective Houses**—a constitutional fact carrying direct Prelims consequences.

#### Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Legislative Assembly (Articles 178–181)

Under **`Article 178`**, every Legislative Assembly shall, ***"as soon as may be,"*** choose two of its members to be respectively **Speaker** and **Deputy Speaker**. Conventionally, the **Governor** fixes the date for the election of the Speaker, whereas the **Speaker** fixes the date for the election of the Deputy Speaker.

- **Tenure Beyond Dissolution (`Article 179`, Second Proviso):** Ordinarily, the Speaker remains in office throughout the life of the Assembly. Crucially, whenever the Legislative Assembly is **dissolved**, **the Speaker does NOT vacate office**—the Speaker continues until **immediately before the first meeting of the newly elected Assembly** (ensuring institutional continuity of the Assembly Secretariat and presiding duties).
- **Three Modes of Vacation (`Article 179`):** The Speaker (or Deputy Speaker) vacates office earlier only if they: (a) cease to be a member of the Assembly; (b) **resign in writing**—with the **Speaker resigning to the Deputy Speaker** and the **Deputy Speaker resigning to the Speaker** *(never to the Governor!)*; or (c) are **removed by a resolution** of the Assembly passed by a **majority of all the then members of the Assembly** (**Effective Majority** = >50% of total strength minus actual vacancies), **only after giving at least 14 days' advance notice** of the intention to move the resolution.
- **Right to Participate and Vote During Own Removal (`Article 181`):** While a resolution for the removal of the Speaker (or Deputy Speaker) is under consideration, the officer **cannot preside** over the sitting, even though present. However, the Speaker **has the right to speak, take part in proceedings, and vote in the FIRST INSTANCE** on that resolution or any other matter—**NOT a casting vote** in case of an equality of votes!
- **Core Powers and Duties:** The Speaker maintains order and decorum inside the Assembly; acts as the final interpreter of the Constitution, Assembly Rules, and legislative precedents inside the Chamber; **adjourns or suspends the sitting in the absence of a quorum (`Article 189(4)`)**; exercises a **casting vote** solely in the event of a tie (`Article 189(1)`); allows a **secret sitting** upon request of the Leader of the House; **certifies Money Bills with final, non-justiciable authority (`Article 199(3)`)**; adjudicates **Tenth Schedule** anti-defection petitions (`Paragraph 6`, subject to judicial review under *Kihoto Hollohan*); and appoints chairpersons of all Assembly committees while personally chairing the **Business Advisory Committee**, **Rules Committee**, and **General Purposes Committee**.
- **Deputy Speaker, Speaker Pro Tem and Panel of Chairpersons (`Article 180`):** The **Deputy Speaker** is **NOT subordinate** to the Speaker and is directly responsible to the Assembly; whenever appointed to a legislative committee, the Deputy Speaker automatically becomes its chairperson. Under **`Article 180(1)`**, if the offices of **both** Speaker and Deputy Speaker are **vacant**, the **Governor appoints any member of the Assembly** to perform the Speaker's duties (which is also how the **Speaker *pro tem***—conventionally the senior-most MLA—is appointed after general elections to administer `Article 188` oaths and preside over the Speaker's election). By contrast, members of the **Panel of Chairpersons** (nominated by the Speaker under Assembly Rules) preside **ONLY during temporary *absence*** of the Speaker and Deputy Speaker—**NEVER during a *vacancy*!**
- **Chronic Constitutional Violation of Deputy Speaker Vacancies:** Despite `Article 178`'s explicit mandate to elect a Deputy Speaker *"as soon as may be,"* partisan reluctance has left the office vacant across several States. According to **PRS Legislative Research's *Annual Review of State Laws 2025* (May 2026)**, **eight State and UT Assemblies** lacked a Deputy Speaker as of May 2026, with the **Jharkhand Legislative Assembly functioning without a Deputy Speaker for over 21 years**!

#### Chairman and Deputy Chairman of the Legislative Council (Articles 182–187)

Under **`Article 182`**, every Legislative Council chooses two of its **own members** to be **Chairman** and **Deputy Chairman**. This creates the single most frequently tested Prelims contrast between the Rajya Sabha and the Vidhan Parishad:

- **Member-Elected Chair vs Ex-Officio Chair:** Whereas the **Vice-President of India** is the *ex-officio* Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (`Article 64/89`) and is **NOT a member** of that House, the **Chairman of a State Legislative Council IS an elected/nominated member of the Council**, chosen directly by fellow MLCs!
- **Mutual Resignation and Effective-Majority Removal (`Article 183`):** Like the Assembly pair, the **Chairman resigns to the Deputy Chairman** and the **Deputy Chairman resigns to the Chairman**; either may be removed by a Council resolution passed by an **Effective Majority** (majority of all the then members) after **14 days' advance notice** (whereas removing the Vice-President requires Rajya Sabha effective majority *plus* Lok Sabha simple majority under `Article 67(b)`!).
- **First-Instance Voting Trap (`Article 185` vs `Article 92`):** When a resolution for the removal of the **Chairman of a Legislative Council** is under consideration, **`Article 185(2)`** entitles the Chairman to speak, participate, and **VOTE IN THE FIRST INSTANCE** (though not in case of a tie), precisely because the Chairman is a member of the Council! Contrast this with **`Article 92(2)`**, under which the **Vice-President (Chairman of the Rajya Sabha) CANNOT vote at all** during his own removal proceedings because he is not a member of the Rajya Sabha!
- **Powers, Salaries and Separate Secretariats (`Articles 184, 186–187`):** The Chairman enjoys almost identical house-management powers to the Speaker, **save one decisive exclusion**: only the **Speaker** certifies Money Bills (`Article 199(3)`). During simultaneous vacancy of Chairman and Deputy Chairman, the **Governor** appoints an MLC to perform the duties (`Article 184(1)`), whereas the Panel of Vice-Chairpersons acts only during temporary *absence*. Under **`Article 186`** and **`Article 202(3)(b)`**, the salaries and allowances of all four presiding officers are fixed by the **State Legislature by law** and **charged upon the Consolidated Fund of the State** (non-votable). Under **`Article 187`**, each House maintains its own **separate secretarial staff**, preserving institutional independence from the State Executive.

### Sessions, Quorum, Language, Special Address and Participation Rights (Articles 174–177, 189, 208–211)

The operational rhythm of a State Legislature is governed by a tightly linked set of constitutional safeguards designed to guarantee regular ministerial accountability and prevent unilateral executive bypass.

#### Summoning, Prorogation, Dissolution and Adjournment (`Article 174`)

Under **`Article 174(1)`**, the Governor summons each House from time to time, subject to the mandatory constitutional rule that **six months shall not intervene between its last sitting in one session and the date appointed for its first sitting in the next session**—requiring at least **two sessions every year** (in practice, most States hold Budget, Monsoon, and Winter sessions, though tiny Assemblies often compress them to just two brief sittings). Under **`Article 174(2)`**, the Governor may prorogue either House or dissolve the Legislative Assembly.

Crucially, the Supreme Court's five-judge Constitution Bench in ***Nabam Rebia and Bamang Felix v. Deputy Speaker, Arunachal Pradesh Legislative Assembly* (2016)** settled that **`Article 174` is NOT a discretionary power of the Governor**: so long as the Chief Minister and Council of Ministers enjoy Assembly confidence, the Governor is **constitutionally bound by Cabinet aid and advice** in summoning, advancing, or proroguing sessions (retaining discretion only when the ministry demonstrably appears to have lost its majority and refuses to face a floor test). Reinforcing legislative autonomy, a three-judge Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud in ***State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor* (10 November 2023)** upheld the **Speaker's authority to reconvene a House that had been adjourned *sine die*** (without prorogation by the Governor), validating Bills passed during such reconvened sittings.

Differentiating the four procedural closures is essential for Prelims:
1. **Adjournment:** Terminates a *sitting* (not a session) for a specified time (hours, days, or weeks); exercised by the **Presiding Officer**; does **NOT** affect pending Bills or notices.
2. **Adjournment *Sine Die*:** Terminates a sitting for an **indefinite period**; exercised by the **Presiding Officer**, who may recall the House before prorogation (*State of Punjab*, 2023).
3. **Prorogation:** Terminates a **session** formally; exercised by the **Governor** (`Article 174(2)(a)`); **lapses all pending notices** (except notices to introduce Bills), **but does NOT lapse any pending Bill (`Article 196(3)`)**!
4. **Dissolution:** Ends the very **life of the Legislative Assembly** (never applicable to the permanent Legislative Council); exercised by the **Governor** (`Article 174(2)(b)`) or automatically at five years; lapses all pending motions, resolutions, notices, and specified categories of Bills (`Article 196(4)–(5)`).

#### Quorum: The High-Yield Dual-Floor Contrast (`Article 189(3)`)

Under **`Article 189(3)`**, until the State Legislature by law otherwise provides, the **quorum** to constitute a meeting of a House of a State Legislature shall be:
- **TEN (10) members**, **OR**
- **ONE-TENTH (1/10th) of the total number of members of the House**,
**WHICHEVER IS GREATER** (including the Presiding Officer)!

This dual-floor formula creates one of UPSC's favourite Prelims traps comparing Parliament and State Legislatures:
- In **Parliament (`Article 100(3)`)**, quorum is a flat **1/10th** of total membership (55 in Lok Sabha, 25 in Rajya Sabha), with **no fixed minimum of 10** because both parliamentary Houses are large.
- In **State Legislatures (`Article 189(3)`)**, the **"10 members OR 1/10th, whichever is greater"** floor protects tiny Houses from passing laws with three or four legislators! Thus, in **Sikkim (32 seats)**, **Goa (40)**, **Mizoram (40)**, **Arunachal Pradesh (60)**, **Nagaland (60)**, and **Telangana's Council (40)**, one-tenth falls below 10, so the constitutional quorum locks at **10 members**; whereas in **Uttar Pradesh's Assembly (403 seats)**, one-tenth (41) exceeds 10, so the quorum is **41 members**. Under **`Article 189(4)`**, if at any time during a meeting there is no quorum, it is the **mandatory duty of the Speaker or Chairman** either to adjourn the House or to suspend the meeting until a quorum is present.

#### Voting, Language, Governor's Special Address and Participation Rights (`Articles 175–177, 189, 208–211`)

- **Voting and Validity Despite Vacancies (`Article 189(1)–(2)`):** All questions at any sitting are decided by a **majority of votes of members present and voting**, excluding the Presiding Officer, who does not vote in the first instance but **must exercise a casting vote** in case of an equality of votes (`Article 189(1)`). Under **`Article 189(2)`**, a House retains full power to act notwithstanding any vacancy in its membership, and proceedings remain valid even if it is later discovered that an unqualified person sat or voted.
- **Language in the State Legislature (`Article 210`):** Business in a State Legislature is transacted in the **official language or languages of the State**, or in **Hindi**, or in **English**. However, the Speaker or Chairman may permit any member who cannot adequately express themselves in any of those languages to address the House in their **mother tongue**. Under `Article 210(2)`, English was originally scheduled to cease fifteen years after constitutional commencement (1965) unless the State Legislature by law continued it—an option almost every State exercised; Parliament also extended that initial cutoff to **25 years** for Himachal Pradesh, Manipur, Meghalaya, and Tripura, and to **40 years** for Arunachal Pradesh, Goa, and Mizoram.
- **Governor's Address, Messages and Special Address (`Articles 175–176`):** Under `Article 175`, the Governor may address either House (or both Houses assembled together) and send messages regarding any pending Bill. Under **`Article 176`**, the Governor **must deliver a Special Address** to the Assembly (or both Houses assembled together in bicameral States) at the commencement of (a) the **first session after each general election** to the Assembly, and (b) the **first session of each year**, informing the Legislature of the causes of its summons. Because this address outlines the policies and legislative programme of the elected State Government—drafted by the **State Council of Ministers**—it is debated through a **Motion of Thanks**, whose defeat in the Assembly signifies loss of confidence! *(Recent Federal Flashpoint, 2023–2026: Disputes erupted in **Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and West Bengal** when Governors omitted paragraphs from Cabinet-drafted `Article 176` addresses or walked out after reading only opening/closing lines, prompting constitutional scholarship to reaffirm that `Article 176` is a formal ministerial-advice duty mirroring the British Monarch's Speech from the Throne, not a platform for personal gubernatorial dissent.)*
- **Rights of Ministers and the Advocate General (`Article 177`):** Mirroring `Article 88` at the Centre, **`Article 177`** grants every **State Minister** and the **Advocate General for the State (`Article 165`)** the right to **speak in, and otherwise take part in the proceedings of**, the Legislative Assembly—and, in a bicameral State, **both Houses**—as well as any legislative committee of which they may be named a member. Crucially, this article confers **NO right to vote**: a Minister votes **only in the House of which they are a member** (and a non-legislator Minister under `Article 164(4)` votes in neither House), while the **Advocate General can NEVER vote** in either House!
- **Rules of Procedure (`Article 208`) and Bar on Discussing Judicial Conduct (`Article 211`):** Each House frames its own rules of procedure subject to the Constitution (`Article 208`). Under **`Article 211`**, **no discussion shall take place in a State Legislature with respect to the conduct of any Judge of the Supreme Court or of a High Court in the discharge of their duties**. Notice a sharp Prelims contrast with Parliament (`Article 121`): Parliament *may* discuss a judge's conduct strictly upon a motion for presenting an address to the President for removal, whereas **State Legislatures can NEVER discuss the conduct of Supreme Court or High Court judges under ANY circumstance**, because State Legislatures have zero role in judicial removal!

> [!prelims]
> **Five High-Yield Prelims Contrasts in Articles 174–211**
> 1. **Quorum (`Art. 189(3)` vs `Art. 100(3)`):** State Legislatures require **10 members OR 1/10th, whichever is GREATER**; Parliament requires a flat **1/10th** (no 10-member floor).
> 2. **Council Chairman's Removal Vote (`Art. 185(2)` vs `Art. 92(2)`):** A **Legislative Council Chairman CAN vote in the first instance** during own removal (being a House member); the **Rajya Sabha Chairman (VP) CANNOT vote at all**!
> 3. **Judicial Conduct Bar (`Art. 211` vs `Art. 121`):** **Absolute prohibition** in State Legislatures (zero impeachment exception!), unlike Parliament's narrow removal-motion exception.
> 4. **Panel of Chairpersons vs Governor Appointment (`Art. 180(1)`):** Panel of Chairpersons presides **ONLY during absence**; during simultaneous **vacancy** of Speaker & Deputy Speaker, the **Governor** appoints an acting MLA.
> 5. **Advocate General (`Art. 177`):** May speak in **both Houses** and committees of which named a member, enjoys `Art. 194` privileges, yet **NEVER votes**.

### Legislative Procedure for Ordinary Bills (Articles 196–197)

An **Ordinary Bill** (relating to any subject in the State List or Concurrent List, other than a Money Bill) may be introduced by either a **Minister** (Government Bill) or a **Private Member** in **either House** of a bicameral State Legislature (`Article 196(1)`). Every Ordinary Bill passes through **five classical stages** before becoming law:
1. **First Reading:** Leave to introduce, reading of the title and objectives, and Gazette publication (no general debate).
2. **Second Reading:** The most substantive phase, spanning three sub-stages—**General Discussion** on underlying principles; **Committee Stage** (referral to a Select Committee of the House or Joint Committee of both Houses, or circulation for public opinion); and **Consideration Stage** (clause-by-clause debate, amendments, and voting).
3. **Third Reading:** Confined strictly to acceptance or rejection of the Bill as a whole (only formal/verbal amendments permitted).
4. **Passage in the Second House** (in the 6 bicameral States).
5. **Assent of the Governor**—or, upon reservation, the **President** (`Articles 200–201`).

#### No Joint Sitting! The Three-Plus-One-Month Override Formula (`Article 197`)

Here lies the **single starkest structural divergence** between the Union Parliament and bicameral State Legislatures. In Parliament, when the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha deadlock over an Ordinary Bill, **`Article 108`** provides for a **Joint Sitting** of both Houses where both chambers deliberate together as co-equals. **The Constitution makes NO PROVISION WHATSOEVER for a Joint Sitting in State Legislatures!** Instead, **`Article 197`** establishes unequivocal **Legislative Assembly primacy** by splitting Ordinary Bills into two completely asymmetric tracks:

1. **Track A — Ordinary Bill Originating in the Legislative Assembly (`Article 197(1)–(2)`):**
   When an Ordinary Bill passed by the Legislative Assembly is transmitted to the Legislative Council, the Council has **four options**: (i) pass the Bill without amendments; (ii) pass the Bill with amendments and return it to the Assembly; (iii) reject the Bill altogether; or (iv) take no action and keep the Bill pending.
   - If the Council **rejects** the Bill, **passes it with amendments unacceptable to the Assembly**, or allows **more than THREE (3) MONTHS** to elapse from the date the Bill is laid before the Council without passing it, the **Legislative Assembly may pass the Bill a SECOND TIME** (with or without accepting any Council amendments) and transmit it again to the Council.
   - Upon this second transmission, if the Council again **rejects** the Bill, **passes unacceptable amendments**, or allows **more than ONE (1) MONTH** to elapse without passing it, the Bill is **constitutionally DEEMED TO HAVE BEEN PASSED BY BOTH HOUSES** in the form in which it was passed by the Legislative Assembly for the second time!
   - **Maximum Delaying Power of the Legislative Council:** Strictly **FOUR MONTHS in aggregate** (**3 months** in the first instance **+ 1 month** in the second instance)!
2. **Track B — Ordinary Bill Originating in the Legislative Council (`Article 197` Silence / `Article 196(2)`):**
   Notice that **`Article 197`'s overriding mechanism applies ONLY to Bills passed by the Assembly and sent to the Council—NEVER in reverse!** Consequently, if an Ordinary Bill originates in and is passed by the **Legislative Council** and sent to the **Legislative Assembly**, and the Assembly **rejects** it, **the Bill DIES IMMEDIATELY!** There is no second passage, no four-month clock, and no Joint Sitting to rescue a Council-originated Bill!

```flow
# Ordinary Bill Passage in a Bicameral State Legislature (Art. 197 — NO Joint Sitting!)
Ordinary Bill Passed by Legislative Assembly (1st Time) | Transmitted to Legislative Council (Note: A Council-originated Bill rejected by Assembly DIES IMMEDIATELY!)
Council Passes As-Is | Sent Directly to Governor (Art. 200) || Council Rejects / Unacceptable Amendments / Delays > 3 MONTHS | First-Instance Deadlock Triggered (Art. 197(1))
Legislative Assembly Passes Bill a SECOND Time | Assembly Free to Accept or Reject Council Amendments → Re-transmitted to Council
Council Passes Within 1 Month | Sent to Governor (Art. 200) || Council Rejects / Unacceptable Amendments / Delays > 1 MONTH | Bill DEEMED PASSED by Both Houses in Assembly's 2nd Form (Art. 197(2))!
Maximum Council Delay = 4 Months (3 + 1) | Sent to Governor (Art. 200) — Zero Joint Sitting Mechanism Anywhere in Part VI
```

#### Exact Rules of Lapsing of Bills Upon Dissolution of the Legislative Assembly (`Article 196(3)–(5)`)

Just as `Article 107(5)` is a perennial UPSC Prelims minefield for Parliament, **`Article 196(3)–(5)`** lays down the **exact six scenarios** determining whether a Bill survives or lapses when a State Legislative Assembly is **dissolved** (remember: **prorogation NEVER lapses any Bill** under `Article 196(3)`!):

| # | Procedural Status of the Bill at the Moment of Assembly Dissolution | Does It Lapse? | Constitutional Basis & Core Logic |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Pending in the Legislative Assembly** (whether originating in the Assembly OR passed by the Council and transmitted to the Assembly) | **LAPSES** | `Art. 196(5)` — Any Bill sitting on the Assembly's floor dies with the dissolved Assembly |
| **2** | **Passed by the Legislative Assembly, but pending in the Legislative Council** | **LAPSES** | `Art. 196(5)` — Although sitting in the permanent Council, it depends on the dissolved Assembly's legislative mandate |
| **3** | **Pending in the Legislative Council, and NOT YET passed by the Legislative Assembly** | **DOES NOT LAPSE** | `Art. 196(4)` — The Council is a continuing House, and the dissolved Assembly never touched the Bill |
| **4** | **Passed by the Assembly (unicameral) or by BOTH Houses (bicameral), awaiting Governor's assent** | **DOES NOT LAPSE** | Legislative process is already complete; Governor may still assent (`Art. 200`) |
| **5** | **Reserved by the Governor for the President's consideration (`Art. 201`)** | **DOES NOT LAPSE** | Pending before President after full legislative passage |
| **6** | **Returned by the Governor or the President for reconsideration** | **DOES NOT LAPSE** | Reconsidered by the newly constituted Assembly (and Council, if bicameral) |

> [!remember]
> **One-Sentence Master Shortcut for Bill Lapsing (`Art. 196` & `Art. 107`)**
> Whenever a Bill is **unfinished** AND has **touched the Legislative Assembly** (either sitting inside the Assembly OR passed by the Assembly and pending in the Council), it **LAPSES** upon dissolution! If it is **still purely inside the Council** (never passed by the Assembly) OR has **already cleared both Houses** (awaiting assent, reserved for President, or returned for reconsideration), it **SURVIVES**!

### Money Bills and Financial Bills in State Legislatures (Articles 198–199, 207)

Mirroring `Articles 109–110` and `117` of Parliament, the Constitution gives the directly elected **Legislative Assembly** absolute financial sovereignty over taxation and withdrawals from the Consolidated Fund of the State, restricting the indirectly elected Legislative Council to an advisory fourteen-day role on Money Bills.

#### Exhaustive Definition and Certification of a State Money Bill (`Article 199`)

Under **`Article 199(1)`**, a Bill is deemed to be a **Money Bill** if it contains **"only"** provisions dealing with all or any of **six exhaustive financial matters** (plus incidental matters):
1. **(a)** The imposition, abolition, remission, alteration, or regulation of any **tax**;
2. **(b)** The regulation of the **borrowing of money** or the giving of any **guarantee** by the State, or the amendment of the law concerning any financial obligations undertaken by the State;
3. **(c)** The custody of the **Consolidated Fund** or the **Contingency Fund of the State**, or the payment of moneys into or withdrawal of moneys from any such Fund;
4. **(d)** The **appropriation of moneys** out of the Consolidated Fund of the State;
5. **(e)** The declaring of any expenditure to be **expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State**, or increasing the amount of any such expenditure;
6. **(f)** The receipt of money on account of the **Consolidated Fund** or the **Public Account of the State**, or the custody or issue of such money; or
7. **(g)** Any matter **incidental** to any of the matters specified in `(a)` to `(f)`.

Equally crucial for Prelims are the **three express exclusions (`Article 199(2)`)**: a Bill is **NOT** a Money Bill merely because it provides for (i) the imposition of **fines or other pecuniary penalties**; (ii) the demand or payment of **fees for licences or fees for services rendered**; or (iii) the imposition, abolition, remission, alteration, or regulation of **any tax by any local authority or body for local purposes** (such as municipal property taxes or panchayat cesses!).

If any question arises whether a Bill is a Money Bill or not, **`Article 199(3)`** declares that the **decision of the Speaker of the Legislative Assembly thereon shall be FINAL**—cannot be questioned in any court, in either House, or even by the Governor *(subject only to narrow constitutional review for patent *mala fide* / fraud on the word "only", paralleling the pending seven-judge reference in *Rojer Mathew*, 2019)*. Under **`Article 199(4)`**, the Speaker must endorse a signed certificate on every Money Bill when transmitting it to the Legislative Council and when presenting it to the Governor for assent.

#### Special Procedure for Money Bills (`Article 198` & `Article 207(1)`)

- **Assembly-Only Introduction & Governor's Prior Recommendation:** A Money Bill **cannot be introduced in the Legislative Council** (`Article 198(1)`). It can be introduced **only in the Legislative Assembly**, **only by a Minister** (Government Bill), and **only on the prior recommendation of the Governor (`Article 207(1)`)**.
- **Strict 14-Day Cap on Legislative Council (`Article 198(2)–(5)`):** Once passed by the Assembly, the Money Bill is transmitted to the Council for its **recommendations**. The Council **CANNOT reject or amend** a Money Bill; it must return the Bill to the Assembly **within 14 days** with or without recommendations. The Assembly may **accept or reject all or any** of those recommendations. Whether the Assembly accepts them, rejects them, or the Council fails to return the Bill within 14 days, the Money Bill is **deemed passed by both Houses** in the form determined by the Assembly!
- **No Return by Governor (`Article 200`, First Proviso):** When presented to the Governor, the Governor may assent, withhold assent, or reserve the Money Bill for the President—**but CANNOT return a Money Bill to the State Legislature for reconsideration!** (Likewise, under `Article 201`, the President may assent to or withhold assent from a reserved Money Bill, **but CANNOT return it for reconsideration!**)

#### Financial Bills Category I (`Article 207(1)`) and Category II (`Article 207(3)`)

Distinguishing Money Bills from the two classes of **Financial Bills** mirrors `Article 117` at the Centre:
- **Financial Bill Category I (`Article 207(1)`):** Contains one or more `Article 199(1)(a)–(f)` matters *along with* general legislative provisions. It shares **two traits with Money Bills**—introduced **only in the Assembly** and **only with Governor's prior recommendation**—but **in all other respects follows Ordinary Bill procedure** (meaning the Legislative Council may amend, reject, or delay it up to **3 + 1 = 4 months**, and the Governor may return it for reconsideration!).
- **Financial Bill Category II (`Article 207(3)`):** Contains provisions involving **expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of the State**, without any `Article 199(1)(a)–(f)` matter. It is treated as a pure **Ordinary Bill**—may be introduced in **either House** *without* prior recommendation, **except** that neither House can take it up for **consideration** unless the Governor has recommended its consideration!

```compare
# Ordinary Bill vs Money Bill in a State Legislature (Articles 196–201, 207)
Ordinary Bill (Articles 196–197, 200) | Money Bill (Articles 198–199, 200, 207)
Introduction House :: Either **Legislative Assembly OR Legislative Council** | **Legislative Assembly ONLY** (`Art. 198(1)`)
Sponsor :: Minister **OR** Private Member | **Minister ONLY** (always a Government Bill)
Governor's Prior Recommendation :: **Not required** (except special Arts. like `Art. 304(b)` President sanction) | **Mandatory** prior recommendation of Governor (`Art. 207(1)`)
Speaker's Certification :: Not required | **Mandatory & Final** (`Art. 199(3)–(4)`)
Legislative Council's Powers :: Can amend, reject, or delay up to **4 months (3 + 1)** for Assembly Bills; **Council Bill dies** if Assembly rejects | **CANNOT amend or reject**; can only recommend changes within **14 days**; Assembly free to ignore
Deadlock Mechanism :: **NO Joint Sitting!** Assembly overrides on 2nd passage (`Art. 197`) | **NO deadlock possible**; Assembly's will prevails automatically after 14 days
Governor's / President's Return :: Governor (`Art. 200`) & President (`Art. 201`) **CAN return** once for reconsideration | **CANNOT be returned** for reconsideration by either Governor or President!
```

### Assent and Reservation of Bills for the President (Articles 200–201)

A major federal check distinguishing State law-making from parliamentary law-making is the **Governor's constitutional power to reserve a State Bill for the consideration of the President (`Articles 200 and 201`)** *(examined exhaustively from the executive angle in Chapter 14, and summarised here in its legislative workflow)*.

#### Governor's Options (`Article 200`) and Reservation Triggers

When a Bill has been passed by the State Legislature and presented to the Governor under **`Article 200`**, the Governor may: (1) **give assent** (the Bill becomes a State Act); (2) **withhold assent and return the Bill** (if it is **not a Money Bill**) *"as soon as possible"* with a message requesting reconsideration—and if the Legislature passes the Bill again, with or without amendments, the Governor **shall not withhold assent therefrom**; or (3) **reserve the Bill for the consideration of the President**.

Reservation is **constitutionally MANDATORY** under the **second proviso to `Article 200`** in one explicit contingency: whenever, in the opinion of the Governor, a Bill would, if it became law, **so derogate from the powers of the High Court as to endanger the position which that Court is by the Constitution designed to fill**. In addition, Governors may reserve Bills in constitutional discretion or on specific federal grounds—such as saving a **Concurrent List State law** repugnant to a parliamentary law (`Article 254(2)`), compulsory acquisition of estates (`Article 31A`), taxation of inter-State water/electricity (`Article 288(2)`), restrictions on inter-State trade (`Article 304(b)`), grave conflict with DPSPs or national interest, or patent *ultra vires* unconstitutionality.

#### Presidential Action (`Article 201`) — No Obligation to Assent Even on Re-Passage!

Once a State Bill is reserved, the **Governor plays NO further role** in its enactment! Under **`Article 201`**, the **President** (acting on the aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers) may: (a) **assent** to the Bill; (b) **withhold assent** (exercising an absolute veto); or (c) if it is **not a Money Bill**, direct the Governor to **return the Bill** to the State Legislature with a message for reconsideration. When so returned, the State Legislature **must reconsider the Bill within SIX MONTHS**; however—in a **huge Prelims contrast with `Article 111` and `Article 200`**—even if the State Legislature passes the Bill again and submits it to the President, **the President is NOT constitutionally bound to give assent!**

> "The Governor, as an unelected Head of the State, is entrusted with certain constitutional powers. However, this power cannot be used to thwart the normal course of lawmaking by the State Legislatures." — Supreme Court of India, *State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor of Punjab*, 10 November 2023

> [!news]
> **The 2023–2025 Supreme Court Settlement on `Articles 200–201`: *Special Reference No. 1 of 2025***
> Prolonged gubernatorial stalling on State Assembly Bills in Punjab, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and Telangana produced a historic three-step judicial settlement:
> - ***State of Punjab* (10 Nov 2023, 3-Judge Bench):** Ruled that *"as soon as possible"* bars any gubernatorial pocket veto; withholding assent to a non-Money Bill requires returning it to the Assembly.
> - ***State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu* (8 Apr 2025, 2-Judge Bench, `2025 INSC 481`):** Imposed strict **1-month / 3-month timelines** under `Arts. 200–201`, barred second-stage reservation of re-passed Bills, and invoked `Article 142` to declare 10 re-passed Tamil Nadu Bills **"deemed assented."**
> - ***In Re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills* (`Special Reference No. 1 of 2025`, 20 Nov 2025, 5-Judge Constitution Bench, `2025 INSC 1333`):** Answering 14 Presidential `Article 143` questions unanimously, the Constitution Bench recalibrated the April 2025 ruling (without disturbing Tamil Nadu's final relief): (i) under `Art. 200`, the Governor enjoys **independent constitutional discretion** across three options (assent, reserve, or withhold-and-return non-Money Bills) and may even reserve a re-passed Bill where warranted; (ii) courts **cannot impose rigid numerical timelines** or grant **`Art. 142` "deemed assent"** without violating separation of powers; **YET** (iii) **no absolute pocket veto exists**, and in glaring cases of **prolonged, unexplained, indefinite inaction**, constitutional courts may issue a **limited writ of mandamus** directing a decision within a reasonable period, unimpeded by `Article 361` personal immunity!

### State Budget, Financial Procedure and State Funds (Articles 202–207, 266–267)

No tax can be levied or collected by a State Government except by authority of law (`Article 265`), and no rupee can be withdrawn from the Consolidated Fund of the State without an Appropriation Act enacted by the State Legislature (`Article 266(3)` & `Article 204`). **`Articles 202 to 207`** establish a financial architecture mirroring Parliament (`Articles 112–117`), with two vital State-level Prelims traps.

#### Annual Financial Statement (`Article 202`) and Charged vs Voted Expenditure

The word **"Budget"** appears nowhere in the Constitution; **`Article 202(1)`** calls it the **Annual Financial Statement (AFS)**—a statement of estimated receipts and expenditure of the State for every financial year (1 April to 31 March), caused by the Governor to be laid before the House(s) of the State Legislature and distinguishing **expenditure on revenue account** from other (capital) expenditure (`Article 202(2)`).

Like the Union Budget, State expenditure is split into two distinct buckets:
1. **Expenditure Charged Upon the Consolidated Fund of the State (`Article 202(3)`):** **Non-votable** by the Assembly—it **may be discussed** in either House (`Article 203(1)`), **but CANNOT be submitted to the vote** of the Legislative Assembly! It comprises **six items**:
   - (a) Emoluments and allowances of the **Governor** and other expenditure relating to the Governor's office;
   - (b) Salaries and allowances of the **Speaker and Deputy Speaker** of the Assembly, and (in bicameral States) the **Chairman and Deputy Chairman** of the Council;
   - (c) **Debt charges** for which the State is liable (interest, sinking fund, redemption charges);
   - (d) Expenditure in respect of the **salaries and allowances of Judges of any High Court**;
   - (e) Any sums required to satisfy any **judgment, decree, or award** of any court or arbitral tribunal; and
   - (f) Any other expenditure declared by the Constitution (e.g., expenses of the **State Public Service Commission** under `Article 322` and administrative expenses of the **High Court** under `Article 229(3)`) or by the State Legislature by law to be so charged.
2. **Voted Expenditure (`Article 203(2)`):** All other expenditure is submitted in the form of **Demands for Grants** **EXCLUSIVELY to the Legislative Assembly** (never to the Legislative Council!). The Assembly may assent to, refuse, or reduce any Demand—**never increase it**—and **no Demand for a Grant can be made except on the recommendation of the Governor (`Article 203(3)`)**. MLAs scrutinise and censure specific departmental Demands through three **Cut Motions**: **Policy Cut** (reduce demand to ₹1, signalling disapproval of underlying policy), **Economy Cut** (reduce by a specified amount to enforce thrift), and **Token Cut** (reduce by ₹100 to ventilate a specific grievance)—passing any Cut Motion in the Assembly amounts to a defeat of the government!

> [!prelims]
> **The Classic High Court Judges Salary vs Pension Charging Trap!**
> Mark this distinction with extreme care—UPSC has tested it repeatedly:
> - **Salaries and allowances of High Court Judges** (plus High Court administrative expenses under `Art. 229(3)`) are charged upon the **Consolidated Fund of the STATE (`Article 202(3)(d)`)**!
> - **PENSIONS of High Court Judges**, however, are charged upon the **Consolidated Fund of INDIA (`Article 112(3)(d)(iii)`)**—NOT the State—because High Court Judges are frequently transferred across States during their careers!

#### Appropriation Bill (`Article 204`), Special Grants (`Articles 205–206`) and Three State Funds (`Articles 266–267`)

- **Appropriation Bill (`Article 204`):** Once Demands for Grants are voted by the Assembly, an **Appropriation Bill** is introduced to provide for legal withdrawal out of the Consolidated Fund of the State of both (a) Assembly-voted grants and (b) charged expenditure. **No amendment can be proposed** to an Appropriation Bill that alters the amount or destination of any grant or charged expenditure (`Article 204(2)`), and until it receives assent, no money may be withdrawn from the CFS (`Article 204(3)`).
- **Special and Exceptional Grants (`Articles 205–206`):**
  - **Supplementary Grant (`Art. 205(1)(a)`):** When the authorised grant for a particular service during the current financial year proves insufficient.
  - **Additional Grant (`Art. 205(1)(a)`):** When a need arises during the current year for supplementary/additional expenditure upon some **new service** not contemplated in the Annual Financial Statement.
  - **Excess Grant (`Art. 205(1)(b)`):** When money has already been spent on a service in excess of the budgeted grant—voted retrospectively by the Assembly *after* the close of the financial year (and after scrutiny by the State **Public Accounts Committee** based on the **CAG's State Audit Report** under `Article 151(2)`!).
  - **Vote on Account (`Article 206(1)(a)`):** Advance grant made by the Legislative Assembly to cover a part of the estimated expenditure for a portion of the financial year (ordinarily two months, or longer during election years) pending completion of full Budget scrutiny and passage of the Appropriation Bill.
  - **Vote of Credit (`Article 206(1)(b)`):** "Blank cheque" grant for meeting an unexpected demand on State resources when the magnitude or indefinite character of the service prevents detailed budget specification.
  - **Exceptional Grant (`Article 206(1)(c)`):** Granted for a special purpose forming no part of the current service of any financial year.
  - **Token Grant:** A nominal ₹1 grant sought when funds for a new service can be met via reappropriation from existing savings.
- **Three Public Funds of Every State (`Articles 266–267`):**
  1. **Consolidated Fund of the State (`Article 266(1)`):** Holds all State tax/non-tax revenues, loans raised, and loan repayments received; **no withdrawal without State Legislature's Appropriation Act**.
  2. **Public Account of the State (`Article 266(2)`):** Holds all other public moneys received by or on behalf of the State Government (provident funds, judicial deposits, small savings/trust funds); operated by **executive action** without requiring legislative appropriation (as the State holds them as banker/trustee).
  3. **Contingency Fund of the State (`Article 267(2)`):** Established by the **Legislature of the State by law** (NOT by Parliament!), consisting of sums paid into it periodically as an imprest, and **placed at the disposal of the Governor** (operated on the Governor's behalf by the State Finance Secretary) to make advances for meeting unforeseen urgent expenditure pending subsequent authorisation by the State Legislature under `Article 205` or `Article 206`.

## 4. Privileges, Comparative Position and Institutional Reforms

A State Legislature can check a dominant State Executive only if its legislators deliberate without fear of retaliatory lawsuits, remain insulated from both corruption and partisan suspensions, operate within a clearly understood institutional balance between Houses, and sit long enough to scrutinise Bills and Budgets meaningfully. This final section integrates **`Articles 194 and 212`** legislative privileges, landmark **2022–2025 Supreme Court guardrails**, exhaustive **institutional comparisons**, empirical **PRS 2024–2026 findings**, and a structured **Mains Reform Blueprint**.

### Privileges of State Legislatures and Judicial Guardrails (Articles 194 and 212)

**`Article 194`** confers special constitutional rights, immunities, and exemptions upon State Legislatures—mirroring `Article 105` for Parliament verbatim. Under **`Article 194(4)`**, these privileges extend to **each House collectively, its committees, all MLAs and MLCs, State Ministers, and the Advocate General (`Article 177`)**—every person constitutionally entitled to speak and participate in legislative proceedings. **Crucial Prelims Trap:** Although the **Governor** is an integral part of the State Legislature under `Article 168`, **the Governor does NOT enjoy legislative privileges under `Article 194`**; gubernatorial personal immunity flows separately from **`Article 361`**.

#### Collective and Individual Privileges

1. **Collective Privileges (Belonging to Each House as a Whole):**
   - **Right to Publish and Prohibit Publication:** Each House controls the publication of its debates, reports, and proceedings and may expunge unparliamentary remarks or prohibit outsiders from publishing expunged or secret material. *(Note: The **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`** inserted **`Article 361A`**, granting constitutional immunity to substantially true newspaper and broadcast reports of open State Legislature proceedings, **excluding secret sittings**!)*
   - **Right to Exclude Strangers and Hold Secret Sittings:** Each House may clear galleries and deliberate behind closed doors on sensitive public matters.
   - **Autonomy Over Internal Rules (`Article 208`):** Each House frames its own procedural rules and regulates its internal business.
   - **Power to Punish Contempt and Breach of Privilege:** Each House may punish both members and outsiders for contempt or breach of privilege by reprimand, admonition, imprisonment, suspension, or expulsion (subject to constitutional limits and judicial proportionality review).
   - **Intimation of Arrest and Precinct Immunity:** Authorities must immediately inform the Speaker or Chairman upon the arrest, detention, conviction, imprisonment, or release of a member; no member or outsider may be arrested or served legal process within the precincts of the House without the Presiding Officer's permission.
   - **Bar on Courts Inquiring Into Procedural Irregularity (`Article 212`):** Under **`Article 212(1)`**, the validity of any proceedings in a State Legislature shall **NOT** be called in question on the ground of any ***"alleged irregularity of procedure."*** Under **`Article 212(2)`**, no officer or member vested with powers to regulate procedure or maintain order is subject to court jurisdiction in respect of exercising those powers. *(Crucially, as the Supreme Court established in *Raja Ram Pal* [2007], *Ashish Shelar* [2022], and *Dr. Sunil Kumar Singh* [2025], `Article 212(1)` shields **procedural irregularities ONLY**—NEVER **substantive illegality**, **constitutional violations**, or **grossly disproportionate punishments**!)*

2. **Individual Privileges (Belonging to Members Personally):**
   - **Freedom of Speech Inside the Legislature (`Article 194(1)–(2)`):** Subject to the Constitution and Rules (such as `Article 211`'s ban on discussing judicial conduct), no legislator is liable to any court proceedings for anything said or any vote given in the Legislature or any committee thereof.
   - **Freedom from Arrest in Civil Cases Only:** Legislators cannot be arrested in **civil cases** during a session and for **40 days before and 40 days after** a session. This privilege **does NOT extend to criminal offences or preventive detention**!
   - **Exemption from Jury Service and Court Appearance:** Members may decline to give evidence or appear as witnesses in court while the House is in session without the Presiding Officer's permission.

#### Sources, Non-Codification and Judicial Boundaries (1959–2025)

Under **`Article 194(3)`**, where privileges are not codified by State law, they remain those possessed by the House and its members immediately prior to the **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`**—which deleted the explicit colonial-era reference to the **"British House of Commons"** from `Article 194(3)` while preserving its substantive pre-1978 scope. Because Indian State Legislatures have never enacted comprehensive privilege statutes (fearing codification would convert privileges into ordinary "law" fully subject to `Article 13` Fundamental Rights scrutiny), the Supreme Court has drawn firm constitutional boundaries across five landmark judgments:

- ***M.S.M. Sharma v. Sri Krishna Sinha (Searchlight Case)* (1959, 5-Judge Bench) & *In Re: Keshav Singh (UP Assembly Reference)* (1964, 7-Judge Bench):** While *Searchlight* harmonised `Article 194(3)` with press freedom (`Article 19(1)(a)`), the seven-judge Bench in *Keshav Singh* decisively checked legislative absolutism after the Uttar Pradesh Assembly ordered the arrest of two Allahabad High Court judges who had granted bail to a citizen jailed by the House for contempt. The Court held that **legislative privileges are subject to `Article 21` (life and personal liberty) and `Article 226` judicial review**, and High Court judges cannot be hauled up for contempt by an Assembly for performing judicial duties!
- ***Ashish Shelar v. Maharashtra Legislative Assembly* (28 January 2022, 3-Judge Bench, `2022 INSC 116`):** Struck down the one-year suspension of twelve Opposition MLAs as substantively illegal and unconstitutional beyond the ongoing session (detailed in the case box below).
- ***Sita Soren v. Union of India* (4 March 2024, 7-Judge Unanimous Constitution Bench):** Arising directly out of a **Jharkhand MLA** (Sita Soren) accused of accepting a bribe to cast a vote in the **2012 Rajya Sabha election**, a unanimous seven-judge Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud **overruled *P.V. Narasimha Rao* (1998)**. It ruled that **`Articles 194(2)` and `105(2)` confer ZERO immunity against criminal prosecution for bribery** tied to a legislative vote or speech: bribery is neither *"necessary"* nor *"essential"* to deliberative democracy, and the criminal offence is complete the instant a bribe is offered or accepted, regardless of whether or how the legislator eventually votes!
- ***Dr. Sunil Kumar Singh v. Bihar Legislative Council* (25 February 2025, `2025 INSC 264`, Justices Surya Kant & N. Kotiswar Singh):** After the Bihar Legislative Council expelled RJD MLC Dr. Sunil Kumar Singh in July 2024 on an Ethics Committee report for hurling derogatory slogans mocking Chief Minister Nitish Kumar inside the House, the Supreme Court **quashed the expulsion as grossly disproportionate** and modified it to the seven-month suspension already undergone. Laying down a multi-factor **proportionality framework** (degree of obstruction, prior conduct, remorse, impact on House dignity), the Court affirmed that **`Article 212` does NOT bar constitutional courts from reviewing disproportionate expulsions** because removing a representative punishes the electorate as well!
- **Tenth Schedule Adjudication in State Assemblies (*Kihoto Hollohan* 1992, *Subhash Desai* 2023 & *Padi Kaushik Reddy* 2025):** Alongside privileges, State Speakers face strict constitutional oversight under the Tenth Schedule. Following *Kihoto Hollohan* (1992, Speaker acts as reviewable Tribunal) and *Keisham Meghachandra* (2020, three-month normal deadline), the five-judge Bench in ***Subhash Desai v. Governor of Maharashtra* (11 May 2023)** held that the **organisational political party—not a rebel MLA faction—appoints the binding whip** (while referring *Nabam Rebia* [2016] on a removal-facing Speaker's defection powers to a seven-judge Bench). Most recently, in ***Padi Kaushik Reddy v. State of Telangana* (31 July 2025, `2025 INSC 912`)**, CJI B.R. Gavai's Bench ordered the Telangana Speaker to conclude year-long pending defection petitions against ten BRS MLAs within **three months**, empowering adverse inferences against stalling MLAs (closing contempt proceedings on **12 March 2026** once orders were passed).

> "Parliamentary privilege, codified in Articles 105 and 194 of the Constitution, is integral to deliberative democracy in facilitating the functioning of a parliamentary form of governance." — Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud, *Sita Soren v. Union of India*, 4 March 2024

> "There is no gainsaid that imposing a disproportionate punishment not only undermines democratic values by depriving the member from participating in the proceedings of the House but also affects the electorates of the constituency who remain unrepresented." — Justice Surya Kant, *Dr. Sunil Kumar Singh v. Bihar Legislative Council*, 25 February 2025

> [!case]
> ***Ashish Shelar & Ors. v. Maharashtra Legislative Assembly* (28 January 2022, `2022 INSC 116`) — 3-Judge Bench**
> - **Facts:** During the Monsoon Session (July 2021), the Maharashtra Legislative Assembly passed a resolution suspending **12 Opposition (BJP) MLAs for one full year** for allegedly disorderly conduct and contempt in the presiding officer's chamber, bypassing the Committee of Privileges.
> - **Bench & Holding (Justices A.M. Khanwilkar, Dinesh Maheshwari, C.T. Ravikumar):** Unanimously **quashed the one-year suspension beyond the remainder of the ongoing Monsoon Session** as **"non est in law, unconstitutional, substantively illegal, and grossly irrational."**
> - **Four Pillars of Ratio:** (1) Under Rule 53, suspension is a **graded, self-protective measure** strictly capped at the remainder of the **ongoing session**, not a punitive weapon; (2) Suspension beyond **60 sitting days** effectively mimics **`Article 190(4)`** seat vacancy and violates **`Section 151A` of RPA, 1951** (which prohibits leaving a constituency unrepresented beyond six months), thereby punishing innocent voters and artificially altering Assembly strength; (3) Bypassing the Privileges Committee denied natural justice; and (4) **`Article 212(1)` protects only procedural irregularities**, never substantive unconstitutionality!

> "Inflicting suspension for a period 'beyond the period necessary' than to ensure smooth working/functioning of the House during the Session 'by itself' ... suffer from the vice of being grossly irrational measure adopted against the erring member and also substantively illegal and unconstitutional." — Justice A.M. Khanwilkar, *Ashish Shelar v. Maharashtra Legislative Assembly*, 28 January 2022

### Exhaustive Institutional Comparisons

For both UPSC Prelims elimination and GS-2 Mains analysis, mastery of State Legislatures requires crystal-clear command over **three overlapping constitutional comparisons**: (1) **Legislative Assembly vs Legislative Council**, (2) **Rajya Sabha vs Legislative Council**, and (3) **Parliament vs State Legislature**.

#### Legislative Assembly vs Legislative Council: Equal vs Unequal Spheres

Within a bicameral State, the **Legislative Council stands equal to the Legislative Assembly in eight limited spheres**: (i) introduction and passage of Ordinary Bills (subject to Assembly's override); (ii) approval of Governor's Ordinances (`Article 213`); (iii) selection of Ministers including the Chief Minister (who may belong to either House); (iv) consideration of reports of constitutional bodies (State Finance Commission, State Public Service Commission, CAG's State Audit Report); (v) enlargement of SPSC jurisdiction (`Article 321`); (vi) debating the Governor's Special Address (`Article 176`); (vii) general discussion of the State Budget; and (viii) identical privileges (`Article 194`).

In **eleven decisive domains**, however, the **Legislative Assembly enjoys sweeping constitutional supremacy**: Money Bills (intro, certification, 14-day cap), Financial Bills Category I introduction, Ordinary Bill final override (3+1 month cap; Council-originated Bill dies on Assembly rejection), exclusive voting on Demands for Grants and Cut Motions, exclusive **collective responsibility of the Chief Minister and Council of Ministers (`Article 164(2)`)** and No-Confidence Motions, exclusive State-legislator voting in **Presidential elections (`Article 54`)**, exclusive voting in **Rajya Sabha elections (`Article 80(4)`)**, exclusive authority to **ratify `Article 368(2)` federal Constitutional Amendments**, exclusive initiating power to **abolish the Legislative Council itself (`Article 169`)**, direct popular democratic legitimacy, and dominance in joint committees due to its 3:1 numerical edge.

#### Rajya Sabha vs State Legislative Council: Why the Vidhan Parishad Is Structurally Weaker

Although both are permanent upper chambers with staggered six-year terms, the **Rajya Sabha** is a vital federal pillar exercising near-equal legislative authority with the Lok Sabha, whereas the **Legislative Council** is constitutionally a **weak, subordinate, optional chamber**. The root cause is federal architecture: the Rajya Sabha represents constituent States in a federal Union, whereas a State is internally unitary—giving the Vidhan Parishad no federal constituency to defend against the directly elected Assembly!

### Empirical Health of State Legislatures: PRS Data (2024–2026)

Beyond constitutional design, the sharpest Mains critique in GS Paper II concerns the **steep institutional decline of State Legislatures**. Empirical audits published by **PRS Legislative Research—*Annual Review of State Laws 2024* (May 2025) and *Annual Review of State Laws 2025* (May 2026)**, analysing **27 States and 3 Union Territories** (Manipur did not convene in 2025 while under President's Rule from 13 February 2025 to 4 February 2026)—document five alarming governance deficits:

1. **Chronically Shrinking Sitting Days:** Across India, State Legislative Assemblies sat for an average of just **24 days (133 hours) in 2025**—marginally above **21 days in 2024** (reported as 20 days / 100 hours across 31 legislatures in ARSL 2024) and **23 days in 2023**, yet a catastrophic collapse compared to the 45–60 annual sitting days common in the 1950s and 1960s! In 2025, sitting days ranged from a high of **43 days in Odisha** down to merely **7 days in Nagaland**. Over the five-year **2021–2025** period, **Kerala (41 days/year)**, **Odisha (39)**, and **Karnataka (37)** led national averages, whereas **Nagaland (8)** and **Tripura (9)** sat least. Despite several States prescribing statutory or rule-based minimum sitting targets (e.g., 60–90 days), **every single State except Himachal Pradesh failed to meet its own target** in 2025!
2. **Rushed, Unscrutinised Law-Making:** State Legislatures collectively passed **over 600 Bills in 2025** (up from **~500 Bills in 2024**)—led by **Karnataka (84 Bills in 34 days)** and **Assam (60 Bills in 21 days)**. Strikingly, roughly **30 per cent of all Bills were passed on the very day of introduction**, leaving MLAs virtually zero time to read complex clauses or table amendments; seven legislatures (**Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Gujarat, Jharkhand, Mizoram, Puducherry, and Punjab**) passed **100 per cent of their Bills within a single day** of introduction!
3. **Collapse of Committee and Budgetary Scrutiny:** Unlike Parliament's 24 permanent DRSCs, most State Legislatures lack permanent department-wise Standing Committees. Across States with published legislative records in 2025, **fewer than 5 per cent (under one in twenty) of Bills were referred to committees**—with **Kerala** (operating permanent **Subject Committees**) standing out as the lone institutional exemplar. Similarly, State Budgets collectively authorising over **₹58 lakh crore** in public spending are routinely guillotined after just a handful of days of debate.
4. **Rising "Ordinance Raj":** Bypassing brief Assembly sessions altogether, State Governors (on Cabinet advice) promulgated **127 ordinances in 2025**—a **27 per cent surge** over **100 ordinances in 2024**—concentrated heavily in **local governance (31%)** and **education (13%)**. Repeated ordinance promulgation and repromulgation directly subverts the Constitution Bench warnings in ***D.C. Wadhwa* (1987)** and ***Krishna Kumar Singh* (2017)** that `Article 213` is an emergency power, not a parallel executive law-making factory.
5. **Constitutional Omissions and Mass Suspensions:** As of **May 2026**, **eight State and UT Assemblies functioned without a Deputy Speaker** in breach of `Article 178`'s *"as soon as may be"* mandate—with **Jharkhand going over 21 years** without one! Compounding weak debate, abrupt session scheduling and sweeping Opposition suspensions frequently empty Chambers during contentious votes.

### Mains Reform Blueprint: Revitalising State Legislatures

Rebuilding State Legislatures into genuine pillars of India's cooperative federalism demands five structural reforms anchored in official constitutional commissions:

1. **Mandatory Annual Sitting Calendar:** Amend `Article 174` (or enact uniform State statutes) mandating a fixed minimum annual sitting floor—implementing the **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC / Venkatachaliah Commission, 2002)** recommendation of **at least 50 sitting days for smaller States and 90 sitting days for larger States**, echoed by the **All-India Presiding Officers' Conferences (60–90 days/year)**—and empower a specified share of MLAs (e.g., 1/3rd) to requisition a special session.
2. **Permanent Departmentally Related Subject Committees (Kerala Model):** Institutionalise permanent **Subject Committees** across all 28 States and 3 UTs (replicating **Kerala's Subject Committees** and Parliament's 24 DRSCs), with **mandatory committee referral** of all non-emergency Bills and Departmental Demands for Grants, coupled with mandatory **pre-legislative public consultation** (minimum 30 days) for major State Bills.
3. **Protecting Opposition Space and Impartial Chairmanship:** Adopt UK-style **"Opposition Days"** (allotting fixed days where the Opposition controls the agenda); enforce `Article 178` through a fixed statutory deadline (e.g., first session) to elect the **Deputy Speaker**—conventionally from the Opposition; enforce ***Ashish Shelar* (2022)** and ***Dr. Sunil Kumar Singh* (2025)** strictly against punitive suspensions/expulsions; and transfer **Tenth Schedule** defection adjudication to an **independent permanent tribunal** (*Keisham Meghachandra*, 2020; Law Commission 255th Report) to restore the Speaker's neutrality.
4. **Uniform National Policy on Legislative Councils:** Enact the **Parliamentary Standing Committee (63rd Report, 2013 & 70th Report, 2014)** framework setting transparent national criteria and a parliamentary decision timeline for `Article 169` proposals—paired with the **Second ARC (6th Report, 2007)** proposal to transform Vidhan Parishads into genuine **Chambers of Local Self-Governments** representing PRIs and ULBs rather than patronage refuges for defeated politicians.
5. **Research Capacity, Ordinance Discipline and Open Archives:** Expand **NeVA** to all 37 Legislative Houses with mandatory **live-streaming**, machine-readable Bill/vote archives, and State-level **legislative research services** (modelled on Parliament's LARRDIS and PRS fellowships) so MLAs can scrutinise technical Bills and fiscal budgets independently, while enforcing ***Krishna Kumar Singh* (2017)** strictly against `Article 213` ordinance abuse.

## 1. Organisation and Bicameralism in States (Articles 168–169)

- **Constitutional Seat (`Part VI, Arts. 168–212`):** Every State Legislature consists of the **Governor** (`Art. 168`, integral organ, NOT a member) + **1 House** (unicameral: Legislative Assembly / *Vidhan Sabha*) OR **2 Houses** (bicameral: Assembly + Legislative Council / *Vidhan Parishad*). **CM & State CoM** are collectively responsible **ONLY to the Assembly (`Art. 164(2)`)**.
- **22 Unicameral vs 6 Bicameral States (Nov 2026):** Only **6 States** have active Legislative Councils (**KUMB-AT** — **426 MLCs total**: 367 elected + 59 nominated under Third Schedule, RPA 1950): **Uttar Pradesh (403 MLA / 100 MLC)**, **Maharashtra (288 / 78)**, **Bihar (243 / 75)**, **Karnataka (224 / 75)**, **Andhra Pradesh (175 / 58)**, and **Telangana (119 / 40)**. **No UT has a Council** (Delhi 70, Puducherry 30+3, J&K 90 elected; J&K's 36-member Council abolished in **2019**). **Madhya Pradesh** (inserted in `Art. 168` by `7th Amend 1956`) & **Tamil Nadu** (`2010 Act`) were **never notified** → both remain **unicameral**!
- **Creation & Abolition (`Article 169`):** **Two steps:** (1) **State Legislative Assembly** (Council has **NO say**) passes resolution by **Special Majority** (>50% of **total membership** AND ≥2/3rd **present & voting**); (2) **Parliament** passes an Act by **Simple Majority** (updates `Art. 168` & RPA 1950). **`Art. 169(3)`:** Expressly **NOT an `Art. 368` Constitutional Amendment**! Parliament is **NOT bound** to accept an Assembly resolution (`"may"`).
- **Historical Swings & Pending Proposals (2026):** Abolished in **West Bengal (1 Aug 1969)**, **Punjab (7 Jan 1970)**, **AP (1 Jun 1985 → revived 2007 under 2005 Act → 27 Jan 2020 abolition resolution → withdrawn 23 Nov 2021!)**, **Tamil Nadu (1 Nov 1986 → 2010 Act unnotified → withdrawn 2011)**, **J&K (2019)**. **4 Pending Proposals (2026):** **Assam** (2005/2010; 2013 Bill), **Rajasthan** (2012; 2013 Bill), **Odisha** (Sep 2018 — 49 seats), **West Bengal** (6 Jul 2021 revival).
- **Mains Reform Debate:** Revising chamber + local-body/talent voice vs expensive *"dilatory chamber"* + *"rehabilitation home for defeated politicians"*. **2nd ARC (6th Report, 2007):** Make Councils universal **Local-Body Chambers** (drop graduates'/teachers' quotas). **Parliamentary Standing Committee (63rd Report 2013 & 70th Report 2014):** Enact a **Uniform National Policy on Legislative Councils** + Parliament decision timeline.

## 2. Composition, Duration and Membership (Articles 170–173, 188–193)

- **Legislative Assembly (`Art. 170`):** Direct **FPTP** (`Art. 326`, voting age 18 via `61st Amend 1988`); strength **60 to 500** (UP largest: 403). **5 Part XXI Exceptions:** **Sikkim** (`Art. 371F`, min 30 / actual **32**), **Goa** (`Art. 371I`, min 30 / actual **40**), **Mizoram** (`Art. 371G`, min 40 / actual **40**), **Arunachal Pradesh** (`Art. 371H`, min 30 / actual **60**), **Nagaland** (`Art. 371A`, min 46 / actual **60**). **UTs:** Puducherry 30 (+ up to 3 Centre nominees), Delhi 70, J&K 90 (+ 5 LG nominees).
- **Delimitation, Reservations & Duration:** State totals frozen at **1971 Census** until first Census post-**2026** (`84th Amend 2001`); internal boundaries on **2001 Census** (`87th Amend 2003`). **SC/ST reservation (`Art. 332`)** extended to **25 Jan 2030** (`104th Amend 2019`), which **abolished Governor's 1 Anglo-Indian nominee (`Art. 333`)**. **`106th Amendment Act, 2023` (*Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam*, `Art. 332A` & `Art. 334A`):** **33% women's quota** in State Assemblies (incl. 1/3rd within SC/ST; **NOT in Legislative Councils!**); formally **notified into force on 16 April 2026** (`S.O. 1922(E)`), operational post-census & delimitation for 15 yrs (`131st Bill` defeated 17 Apr 2026). **Term (`Art. 172(1)`):** **5 years** from first sitting; during **`Art. 352` National Emergency**, **Parliament** may extend **1 yr at a time**, max **6 months** post-Emergency.
- **Legislative Council (`Art. 171–172`):** Max **1/3rd of Assembly**, Min **40** (Telangana = 40); **Permanent House**, 1/3rd retire every 2 yrs; **6-year term** fixed by **`Sec. 156 RPA 1951`** (NOT Constitution!). **5-Part Composition (`Art. 171(3)`, default formula until Parliament alters via Third Schedule RPA 1950; PR-STV secret ballot):** **1/3rd Local Bodies**, **1/12th Graduates (3 yrs standing)**, **1/12th Secondary-or-higher Teachers (3 yrs standing — primary teachers excluded!)**, **1/3rd elected by MLAs from non-MLAs**, **1/6th Nominated by Governor (`Art. 171(5)`)** from **5 fields (`CLASS`): Cooperative movement, Literature, Art, Science, Social service** *(RS `Art. 80(3)` has only 4 fields — NO Cooperative Movement!)*. Governor bound by CoM advice on nominations.
- **Qualifications (`Art. 173` & `RPA 1951`):** Indian citizen; 3rd Schedule candidate oath (`16th Amend 1963`); min age **25 (MLA) / 30 (MLC)**; **HUGE CONTRAST WITH RS:** MLA & elected MLC candidates **MUST be registered electors in THAT State** (`Secs. 5–6 RPA 1951`; Governor nominees must be ordinarily resident in that State) — unlike Rajya Sabha (no domicile since 2003, *Kuldip Nayar* 2006)!
- **Disqualifications (`Arts. 191–192`, `RPA 1951` & Tenth Schedule):** `Art. 191(1)`: Office of profit (**State Legislature** exempts posts; Ministers exempt; Parliamentary Secretaries struck down in *Bimolangshu Roy* 2017), unsound mind, insolvent, non-citizen, Parliamentary law (`RPA Sec. 8(3)` 2-yr conviction = immediate disqualification, *Lily Thomas* 2013 & *Lok Prahari* 2018; preventive detention NOT a ground; *B.R. Kapur* 2001 bars disqualified non-MLA from CM post). **Deciding Authority Split:** Post-election `Art. 191(1)`/RPA → **Governor bound by ECI opinion (`Art. 192` — NEVER State Election Commission!)**; Pre-election → **High Court election petition** (*Saka Venkata Rao* 1953); Tenth Schedule (`Art. 191(2)`) → **Speaker / Chairman** (reviewable Tribunal).
- **Vacation, Oath & Penalty (`Arts. 188–190, 193, 195`):** **Double membership:** Parliament + State Legislature → must resign State seat in **14 days**, else **PARLIAMENTARY seat vacates (`Art. 101(2)`)**; two States → **10 days**, else ALL vacate; two seats same House → **14 days**, else BOTH vacate. **Resignation (`33rd Amend 1974`):** Speaker/Chairman may reject non-voluntary/non-genuine resignation. **60-day unpermitted absence (`Art. 190(4)`):** House *may* declare seat vacant (excludes prorogation/adjournment >4 consecutive days). **Member oath (`Art. 188`)** before Governor/designate (Speaker *pro tem*); **₹500/day penalty (`Art. 193`)**; salaries fixed by **State Legislature (`Art. 195`)**.

## 3. Presiding Officers, Sessions and Legislative Procedure (Articles 174–212)

- **Presiding Officers (`Arts. 178–187`):** **All 4 are members of their own Houses!** Speaker & Dy. Speaker (`Art. 178`); Chairman & Dy. Chairman (`Art. 182`). **Mutual resignation** (Speaker↔Dy. Speaker; Chairman↔Dy. Chairman). **Removal:** **Effective Majority** (>50% of all then members) + **14 days' advance notice** (`Arts. 179, 183`). **Speaker stays beyond Assembly dissolution** until just before new Assembly's first sitting. **First-Instance Vote Trap:** During own removal, Speaker (`Art. 181`) **AND Council Chairman (`Art. 185`)** **CAN vote in the first instance** (NO casting vote) — **UNLIKE RS Chairman (VP, `Art. 92`), who CANNOT vote at all!** Panel of Chairpersons presides **ONLY during absence**; during simultaneous **vacancy**, **Governor** appoints acting member (`Arts. 180(1), 184(1)`). Separate Secretariats (`Art. 187`); salaries charged on CFS (`Art. 202(3)(b)`).
- **Sessions, Quorum, Language & `Arts. 175–177, 211`:** Max **6-month gap (`Art. 174`)**; Governor bound by CoM advice (*Nabam Rebia* 2016); Speaker may reconvene Assembly adjourned *sine die* (*State of Punjab* Nov 2023). **Quorum (`Art. 189(3)`):** **10 members OR 1/10th of total membership, WHICHEVER IS GREATER** (incl. Chair) — contrasts with Parliament's flat 1/10th! **Language (`Art. 210`):** Official State language(s), Hindi, or English (+ mother tongue by Chair). **Special Address (`Art. 176`):** Post-election & first session each year (Cabinet-prepared; Motion of Thanks). **`Art. 177`:** Ministers & **Advocate General** speak in **either House**, without non-member vote (AG never votes). **`Art. 211`:** **Absolute bar** on discussing SC/HC judges' conduct (zero impeachment exception, unlike Parliament's `Art. 121`!).
- **Ordinary Bills (`Arts. 196–197`):** **NO Joint Sitting in State Legislatures!** Assembly Bill → Council delays max **3 months** (1st time) → Assembly passes 2nd time → Council delays max **1 month** (2nd time) → **DEEMED PASSED** → **Max Council delay = 4 months (3 + 1)**! **Council-originated Bill rejected by Assembly DIES IMMEDIATELY!** **Lapsing on Dissolution (`Art. 196(3)–(5)`):** Prorogation **NEVER** lapses Bills; on dissolution, any unfinished Bill that **touched the Assembly** (pending in Assembly OR passed by Assembly & pending in Council) **LAPSES**; Bill purely inside Council (not passed by Assembly), awaiting assent, reserved for President, or returned for reconsideration **SURVIVES**.
- **Money Bills & Financial Bills (`Arts. 198–199, 207`):** Money Bill (`Art. 199` 6 criteria; excludes fines, licence/service fees, local body taxes): **Assembly ONLY**, Minister ONLY, **Governor's prior recommendation (`Art. 207(1)`)**, **Speaker's final certificate (`Art. 199(3)`)**, **14-day Council recommendatory cap (`Art. 198`)**, **NO return** by Governor/President. Financial Bill Cat. I (`Art. 207(1)`): Assembly intro + Governor recommendation, then Ordinary Bill (3+1 cap). Cat. II (`Art. 207(3)`): Either House intro, Governor recommendation for **consideration** only.
- **Reservation for President (`Arts. 200–201`) & 2025 Settlement:** Mandatory reservation if Bill derogates from **High Court powers** (`Art. 200` 2nd proviso). Under `Art. 201`, President **NOT bound** to assent even after State Legislature re-passes within 6 months! ***Special Reference No. 1 of 2025* (20 Nov 2025, 5-Judge, `2025 INSC 1333`):** Recalibrated *State of Tamil Nadu* (8 Apr 2025); restored **independent gubernatorial discretion**, rejected rigid timelines & `Art. 142` *"deemed assent"*, **YET** confirmed **no absolute pocket veto** and allowed **limited mandamus** for prolonged unexplained inaction despite `Art. 361`.
- **State Budget & Funds (`Arts. 202–207, 266–267`):** AFS (`Art. 202`, word "Budget" absent). **Charged on CFS (`Art. 202(3)` — discussed, NOT voted):** Governor, 4 Presiding Officers, State debt, **HC Judges' salaries & allowances** *(**TRAP:** HC Judges' **pensions** charged on **Consolidated Fund of INDIA**, `Art. 112(3)(d)(iii)`!)*, court decrees, SPSC (`Art. 322`), HC admin (`Art. 229(3)`). Demands for Grants (`Art. 203`): **Assembly ONLY** + Governor recommendation; Cut Motions (Policy ₹1 / Economy / Token ₹100). Appropriation Bill (`Art. 204`); Supplementary/Additional/Excess Grants (`Art. 205`); **Vote on Account (`Art. 206`)**; **Contingency Fund of State (`Art. 267(2)`)** established by **State Legislature law** at Governor's disposal.

## 4. Privileges, Comparative Position and Institutional Reforms

- **Privileges (`Arts. 194, 212, 361A`):** Cover Houses, committees, MLAs, MLCs, Ministers & Advocate General (**NOT Governor**, who has `Art. 361`). `44th Amend 1978` dropped House of Commons reference (`Art. 194(3)`) & added `Art. 361A` press immunity. Individual: speech/vote immunity + **40-day civil-only arrest freedom** (NOT criminal/preventive detention). **`Art. 212(1)`** shields only **procedural irregularities**, NEVER substantive illegality!
- **Landmark Judicial Guardrails (1964–2025):**
  - *In Re: Keshav Singh* (1964, 7-Judge): Contempt warrants reviewable under `Arts. 21 & 226`.
  - ***Ashish Shelar v. Maharashtra Assembly* (28 Jan 2022, 3-Judge, `2022 INSC 116`):** Struck down **1-year suspension of 12 MLAs** beyond ongoing session as unconstitutional, irrational, violating `Art. 190(4)` & `Sec. 151A RPA 1951`; NOT shielded by `Art. 212(1)`.
  - ***Subhash Desai* (11 May 2023, 5-Judge):** Organisational party (not MLA faction) appoints whip; *Nabam Rebia* sent to 7-judge Bench.
  - ***Sita Soren v. Union of India* (4 Mar 2024, 7-Judge unanimous):** Arose from Jharkhand MLA 2012 RS poll bribe; overruled *Narasimha Rao* (1998) → **ZERO bribery immunity under `Arts. 194(2)` & `105(2)`**.
  - ***Dr. Sunil Kumar Singh v. Bihar Legislative Council* (25 Feb 2025, `2025 INSC 264`):** Quashed disproportionate **expulsion of MLC**; affirmed `Art. 212` does NOT bar proportionality review protecting voters.
  - ***Padi Kaushik Reddy v. State of Telangana* (31 Jul 2025, `2025 INSC 912`):** 3-month deadline for Telangana Speaker on 10 BRS MLAs with adverse inference; contempt closed 12 Mar 2026.
- **Comparative Position:** **Assembly vs Council:** Council equal in 8 limited spheres, subordinate in 11 (NO Money Bill/Budget vote, NO No-Confidence, NO Presidential/RS poll, NO `Art. 368(2)` ratification, NO veto on its own `Art. 169` abolition, max 3+1 Ordinary Bill delay). **RS vs Council:** RS mandatory, co-equal (Joint Sitting, `Art. 368` veto, 4 exclusive powers), VP ex-officio Chair, 4 nomination fields, no domicile, open ballot; Council optional, subordinate, member-elected Chair, 5 fields (+ Cooperative Movement), State domicile mandatory, secret ballot.
- **PRS Empirical Findings (*ARSL 2025*, May 2026) & Reforms:** Avg **24 sitting days / 133 hrs** in 2025 (vs 21 in 2024; Odisha 43, Nagaland 7; Kerala 41 5-yr high; only HP met own target); **>600 Bills** passed, **~30% same-day** (7 legislatures 100% within 1 day), **<5% sent to committees**; **127 ordinances** (+27% YoY; 31% local governance); **8 Assemblies lack Deputy Speaker** (Jharkhand 21+ yrs). **NeVA (PIB, 27 Jul 2026):** **33 of 37** State/UT Houses signed MoUs; **21 fully live digital Houses** (NIC MeghRaj 2.0 + BHASHINI). **Way Forward:** **NCRWC** 50/90 sitting-day floor; **Kerala-style Subject Committees**; Opposition Days & independent Tenth Schedule tribunal; **National Policy on Legislative Councils** (Standing Committee + 2nd ARC); 100% NeVA & MLA research support.
