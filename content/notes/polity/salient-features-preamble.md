## 1. What makes the Constitution distinctive

Every constitution sets up institutions and limits power, but the Indian Constitution carries the marks of the country that made it. It had to hold together a subcontinent of many languages, religions and regions just after partition. It had to carry the promises of the freedom struggle. And it had to build on the administrative machinery the British had left behind. Its salient features, the ones that set it apart, follow from those three tasks. Examiners ask about them in two ways: Prelims tests the facts (which feature came from where, and what the Preamble says), and Mains asks what the features tell us about the kind of state India chose to be.

This note covers the Constitution's length and detail, its sources, the blend of rigidity and flexibility, secularism, universal adult franchise and single citizenship, and the Preamble. Other features have notes of their own and get only a line here: the federal system with a unitary bias, the parliamentary form of government, the balance between parliamentary sovereignty and judicial supremacy, and the Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties.

## 2. The lengthiest written constitution in the world

Constitutions can be written, like the American, or unwritten, like the British. The Indian Constitution is the lengthiest of all the written constitutions in the world. As adopted in 1949 it had a Preamble, 395 Articles in 22 Parts, and 8 Schedules. Amendments have since expanded it to about 470 Articles in 25 Parts, and 12 Schedules. New Articles are slotted in with letter suffixes, such as Article 21A or 243ZH, and new Parts likewise (IVA, IXA, IXB and XIVA), so the original numbering still ends at Article 395. More than a hundred amendments have been made; the 106th (2023), on reserving one-third of seats in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies for women, is among the most recent.

Four factors explain the length:

1. **Geography and diversity.** A vast country with many communities needed detailed safeguards: special provisions for particular states (Articles 371 to 371J), for Scheduled Areas and tribes, and for minorities.
2. **The legacy of the Government of India Act, 1935.** The framers took over much of its bulky administrative detail.
3. **A single constitution for the Union and the states.** The states have no constitutions of their own. Jammu and Kashmir was the exception until 2019.
4. **The dominance of lawyers in the Constituent Assembly**, who preferred to put things in writing rather than leave them to convention.

The Constitution also contains matters that elsewhere would be left to ordinary law: the organisation of the public service commissions, the Election Commission and the Comptroller and Auditor General, for instance. That is part of the explanation for its length, and also of why it has been amended so often.

## 3. Drawn from many sources

The framers did not hide that they borrowed. They studied about sixty constitutions and adapted what suited Indian conditions. The structural part of the Constitution comes largely from the Government of India Act, 1935; its philosophical part, the Fundamental Rights and the Directive Principles, comes mainly from the American and Irish constitutions respectively; and its political part, cabinet government and the relationship between the executive and the legislature, comes largely from the British Constitution.

| Source | Features borrowed |
|---|---|
| Government of India Act, 1935 | Federal scheme, office of Governor, judiciary, public service commissions, emergency provisions, administrative details |
| British Constitution | Parliamentary government, rule of law, legislative procedure, single citizenship, cabinet system, prerogative writs, parliamentary privileges, bicameralism |
| US Constitution | Fundamental Rights, independence of the judiciary, judicial review, impeachment of the President, removal of Supreme Court and High Court judges, post of Vice-President |
| Irish Constitution | Directive Principles of State Policy, nomination of members to the Rajya Sabha, method of electing the President |
| Canadian Constitution | Federation with a strong Centre, residuary powers with the Centre, appointment of state Governors by the Centre, advisory jurisdiction of the Supreme Court |
| Australian Constitution | Concurrent List, freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse, joint sitting of the two Houses of Parliament |
| Weimar Constitution of Germany | Suspension of Fundamental Rights during an emergency |
| Soviet Constitution (USSR) | Fundamental Duties; the ideal of justice (social, economic and political) in the Preamble |
| French Constitution | Republic; the ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity in the Preamble |
| South African Constitution | Procedure for amending the Constitution; election of members of the Rajya Sabha |
| Japanese Constitution | "Procedure established by law" (Article 21) |

Critics called the result a "borrowed constitution" and a "patchwork". Ambedkar's answer, in substance, was that there was nothing to be ashamed of in borrowing: the fundamental ideas of a constitution had been settled by then, and the framers' work lay in adapting them to India and avoiding the faults that experience had exposed elsewhere.

> **Prelims pointer:** Look-alikes that examiners pair wrongly. Directive Principles came from Ireland, Fundamental Duties from the USSR. The Concurrent List came from Australia, residuary powers with the Centre from Canada. The amendment procedure came from South Africa, and "procedure established by law" from Japan. Emergency suspension of rights came from Weimar Germany.

## 4. Rigid, flexible, or both

Constitutional theorists classify constitutions as **rigid**, needing a special procedure to amend (like the American), or **flexible**, amendable like an ordinary law (like the British). The Indian Constitution is neither; it blends the two. Article 368 provides two kinds of amendment, and certain provisions can also be changed outside Article 368 altogether.

| Method | What it needs | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Simple majority of Parliament (outside Article 368) | An ordinary law | Forming new states and altering boundaries (Articles 2–4), creating or abolishing legislative councils (Article 169), citizenship, the quorum of Parliament |
| Special majority of Parliament | A majority of the total membership of each House, and two-thirds of those present and voting | Most provisions, including Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles |
| Special majority plus ratification by half the states | The special majority, and ratification by the legislatures of half the states by simple majority | Federal provisions: the election of the President, the distribution of legislative powers, representation of states in Parliament, the Supreme Court and High Courts, Article 368 itself |

The design reflects a judgment the framers made deliberately: the basic arrangements of the federation should be hard to change, while the rest should be able to adapt. Nehru put it in the Assembly in the image of growth: a constitution, he argued, should be solid and permanent, yet flexible enough to keep a nation's life from being stunted. The Supreme Court later added a limit the text does not state: no amendment may destroy the basic structure (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).

## 5. Secularism

The Constitution establishes a **secular state**: it does not uphold any religion as the official religion of India. The word "secular" was added to the Preamble only by the 42nd Amendment (1976), but the idea was present from the start, in the Fundamental Rights. The Supreme Court held in the S.R. Bommai case (1994) that secularism is a basic feature of the Constitution.

Indian secularism differs from the Western model. The Western idea is a wall of separation, with the state keeping out of religion entirely. The Indian idea is positive: the state gives equal respect to all religions, and protects them equally, but it can also intervene in religion for social reform, as when it threw Hindu temples open to all castes. Scholars such as Rajeev Bhargava call this "principled distance".

The Constitution's secular character shows in many provisions:

- Article 14: equality before the law and equal protection of the laws.
- Article 15: no discrimination on grounds of religion, among others.
- Article 16: equality of opportunity in public employment.
- Articles 25 to 28: freedom of conscience and religion.
- Articles 29 and 30: cultural and educational rights of minorities.
- Article 44: the directive on a Uniform Civil Code.

## 6. Universal adult franchise

The Constitution adopted **universal adult franchise** as the basis of elections to the Lok Sabha and the state legislative assemblies (Article 326). Every citizen aged 18 or more has the right to vote, without discrimination on grounds of caste, race, religion, sex, literacy or wealth. The voting age was lowered from 21 to 18 by the **61st Amendment Act, 1988**, which came into force in 1989.

It was a bold choice. In 1950 most Indians could not read, and many Western democracies had extended the vote to all adults only gradually, over a century or more. The framers gave it to every adult at a stroke. Their reasons were that it would make democracy broad-based, enhance the self-respect of ordinary people, uphold equality and let minorities protect their own interests.

## 7. Single citizenship

The American federation has dual citizenship: a person is a citizen of the United States and of a state. India, though federal, has **single citizenship**, Indian citizenship only, with no separate state citizenship. Every citizen, whichever state they were born in or live in, has the same rights of citizenship across the country. The framers chose this to strengthen national unity. (There are exceptions in practice, such as residence requirements for some state jobs and the protection of tribal areas; the citizenship note covers them.)

## 8. The other salient features

These features have their own notes and are listed here for completeness:

- **A federal system with a unitary bias:** two levels of government, but a strong Centre, a single constitution, single citizenship, an integrated judiciary, all-India services and emergency provisions.
- **The parliamentary form of government**, modelled on Britain's: the executive answers to the legislature.
- **A synthesis of parliamentary sovereignty and judicial supremacy:** Parliament can amend most of the Constitution, while the courts can strike down laws, and even amendments, that break the basic structure.
- **An integrated and independent judiciary**, with the Supreme Court at the top.
- **Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties**, the last added in 1976 on the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendation.
- **Emergency provisions**, which allow the federal system to turn unitary in a crisis.
- **Independent bodies**, such as the Election Commission, the CAG and the public service commissions.
- **Three tiers of government**, after the 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) gave panchayats and municipalities constitutional status. The 97th Amendment (2011) added co-operative societies.

## 9. The Preamble

The Preamble is the introduction to the Constitution, a summary of its philosophy and purpose. It was based on the Objectives Resolution moved by Nehru, and it was amended once, by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976, which added the words **Socialist, Secular** and **Integrity**.

> "WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens: JUSTICE, social, economic and political; LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; EQUALITY of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation; IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION." — The Preamble to the Constitution of India

### What the Preamble tells us

The Preamble reveals four things:

```text
        The Preamble tells us...
   ┌──────────┬──────────┬──────────┐
 Source      Nature     Objectives   Date
 "We, the    sovereign, justice,     26 Nov
  people"    socialist, liberty,     1949
             secular,   equality,
             democratic fraternity
             republic
```

1. **The source of the Constitution's authority:** the people of India.
2. **The nature of the Indian state:** sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic and republican.
3. **Its objectives:** justice, liberty, equality and fraternity.
4. **The date of its adoption:** 26 November 1949.

### The key words

- **Sovereign.** India is neither a dependency nor a dominion of any other nation, but an independent state, free to conduct its own affairs, internal and external. Membership of the Commonwealth, and of the United Nations, does not limit this.
- **Socialist.** Indian socialism is "democratic socialism", not communist "state socialism". It aims at a mixed economy, in which public and private sectors coexist, and at ending poverty, ignorance, disease and inequality of opportunity. The economic reforms since 1991 have diluted its economic content but not its commitment to social justice.
- **Secular.** All religions in India get equal respect, protection and support from the state.
- **Democratic.** India is a representative, parliamentary democracy, based on popular sovereignty. The Preamble has in mind political democracy, and also social and economic democracy, as Ambedkar stressed.
- **Republic.** The head of state, the President, is elected indirectly for a fixed term, rather than inheriting the office as in a monarchy. Political sovereignty lies with the people, and every public office is open to every citizen without discrimination.

The objectives draw on several revolutions. **Justice** (social, economic and political) is taken from the Russian Revolution of 1917. **Liberty, equality and fraternity** come from the French Revolution. **Fraternity**, a sense of common brotherhood, is meant to assure two things: the dignity of the individual, and the unity and integrity of the nation. The Fundamental Duty in Article 51A(e) echoes it, by asking citizens to promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood.

### Is the Preamble part of the Constitution?

The question reached the Supreme Court twice.

| Case | Held |
|---|---|
| *Berubari Union* (1960) | The Preamble shows the general purposes behind the Constitution's provisions and is a key to the framers' minds, but it is **not a part of the Constitution** |
| *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) | The earlier view was wrong: the Preamble **is a part of the Constitution**, and the Constitution must be read in its light |
| *LIC of India* (1995) | Reaffirmed that the Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution |

Two further points follow. The Preamble is **not a source of power** for the legislature, nor a limit on its powers, and it is **not enforceable in a court of law** (it is non-justiciable). It is used to interpret the Constitution where the text is ambiguous. And since it is part of the Constitution, it **can be amended** under Article 368, provided the amendment does not touch its basic features. The 42nd Amendment of 1976 is the only amendment made so far.

> **Prelims pointer:** The Preamble is part of the Constitution (Kesavananda, 1973), but it is neither a source of power nor justiciable. It has been amended once (1976), adding "socialist", "secular" and "integrity". "Justice" in the Preamble comes from the Russian Revolution; "liberty, equality, fraternity" from the French.

> **In the news:** On 25 November 2024, in *Dr Balram Singh v Union of India*, a bench of the Supreme Court headed by Chief Justice Sanjiv Khanna dismissed petitions challenging the insertion of "socialist" and "secular" into the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment. The Court held that Parliament's amending power extends to the Preamble, and that the date of adoption written into it does not restrict that power. It also noted the long delay, since the petitions had been filed in 2020, decades after the words became part of the Preamble.

### What it is worth

The framers and later commentators valued the Preamble highly. K.M. Munshi called it the "horoscope" of the sovereign democratic republic, and N.A. Palkhivala called it the "identity card" of the Constitution. Thakur Das Bhargava described it as the most precious part, the soul and key of the Constitution.

> **Mains angle:** A question on the Preamble usually wants three things. First, what it contains: the source of authority, the nature of the state, the objectives and the date. Second, its legal status: Berubari, then Kesavananda and LIC; non-justiciable but interpretive; amendable within the basic structure. Third, its continuing relevance. Here use current debates: secularism and the Uniform Civil Code, socialism in a liberalised economy, and fraternity in a diverse society. Conclude that the Preamble is the yardstick against which laws and policies are measured.
