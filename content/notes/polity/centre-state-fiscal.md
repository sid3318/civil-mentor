## 1. Legislative Relations (Part XI, Articles 245–255)

A federal constitution stands or falls on how cleanly it distributes sovereign power between the national government and its constituent regional units. Because India is a "holding-together" federation forged out of British provinces and 552 princely states amid the trauma of Partition, the framers designed an extraordinarily detailed, three-dimensional distribution of powers between the Union and the States: **legislative relations** (`Part XI`, `Articles 245–255`), **administrative relations** (`Part XI`, `Articles 256–263`), and **financial relations** (`Part XII`, `Articles 268–293`). Notably, unlike the United States—where federal courts and state courts run as parallel, watertight hierarchies—the Indian Constitution makes **no division of judicial power**. India operates a single, integrated judicial hierarchy headed by the Supreme Court and the High Courts, enforcing Union and State statutes alike (the philosophical foundations of Indian federalism and inter-state institutional forums are examined in **Chapter 9**, while full emergency mechanics are detailed in **Chapter 11**).

> "The Draft Constitution has sought to forge means and methods whereby India will have Federation and at the same time will have uniformity in all the basic matters which are essential to maintain the unity of the country. The means adopted by the Draft Constitution are three: (1) a single judiciary, (2) uniformity in fundamental laws, civil and criminal, and (3) a common All-India Civil Service to man important posts." — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, 4 November 1948

Structurally modelled on the **Government of India Act, 1935** and the **Canadian Constitution**, `Parts XI` and `XII` deliberately build a strong centripetal bias to preserve territorial integrity, macroeconomic unity, and a single national market while leaving substantial legislative, administrative, and developmental autonomy to States in normal times. Understanding legislative relations requires mastering four distinct dimensions: the **territorial extent** of Central and State laws, the **subject-matter distribution** under the Seventh Schedule, the **five extraordinary circumstances** permitting Parliament to legislate in the State field, and the **Centre's constitutional control** over State legislation—together with the judicial doctrines that referee federal boundary disputes.

```keyfacts
# Centre–State Constitutional Architecture at a Glance
Part XI (Arts. 245–255) | Legislative Relations | Territorial nexus, Seventh Schedule 3 Lists, 5 extraordinary Parliamentary routes & Art. 254 repugnancy
Part XI (Arts. 256–263) | Administrative Relations | Co-extensive executive power, Arts. 256–257 directions, Arts. 258/258A delegation & Art. 312 All-India Services
Part XII (Arts. 268–293) | Financial Relations | Strict tax separation (except GST Art. 246A), Divisible Pool (Art. 270), Grants (Arts. 275/282) & Borrowing (Arts. 292–293)
Arts. 279A & 280 | Twin Fiscal Institutions | Finance Commission (balancing wheel of transfers, 16th FC 2026–31) & GST Council (pooled indirect-tax sovereignty)
```

### Territorial Extent of Central and State Legislation (Article 245)

`Article 245` defines the geographical boundaries within which Parliament and State Legislatures may enact laws, distinguishing sharply between territorial and extra-territorial competence.

#### General Territorial Jurisdiction & Exclusive Extra-Territorial Power

Under `Article 245(1)`, **Parliament** may make laws for the **whole or any part of the territory of India**—encompassing all States, Union Territories, and any other territory currently included in the territory of India. Conversely, a **State Legislature** may make laws only for the **whole or any part of that State**, and its enactments ordinarily lack legal force outside its borders.

Under `Article 245(2)`, **"no law made by Parliament shall be deemed to be invalid on the ground that it would have extra-territorial operation."** Consequently, **Parliament alone** possesses the constitutional power to enact **extra-territorial legislation**—statutes binding Indian citizens and their property located anywhere in the world (for instance, `Section 4` of the erstwhile IPC / `Section 1(5)` of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, or international taxation statutes). In ***GVK Industries Ltd. v. Income Tax Officer* (2011, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court clarified a vital constitutional nuance: while Indian courts cannot strike down a Parliamentary Act merely because it operates abroad, Parliament may legislate on extra-territorial aspects only when those aspects bear a real nexus with India (or impact India's interests); Parliament cannot enact laws purely governing foreign events with zero connection to India.

#### The Doctrine of Territorial Nexus for State Laws

Although State Legislatures cannot pass extra-territorial statutes per se, a State law is **not** automatically invalid merely because it applies to persons, objects, or transactions located outside the State, provided a **sufficient territorial nexus** connects the State with the subject matter of the legislation. Courts test State legislation on two strict conditions established in ***Wallace Brothers & Co. Ltd. v. Commissioner of Income Tax* (1948)** and crystallized by the Supreme Court in ***State of Bombay v. R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala* (1957)** and ***Tata Iron & Steel Co. Ltd. v. State of Bihar* (1958)**:

1. **Real, Not Illusory Connection:** The nexus between the legislating State and the external object or person must be genuine, substantial, and direct—not remote, fanciful, or illusory.
2. **Pertinence of Liability:** The tax or legal liability imposed must be directly pertinent and proportionate to that territorial connection.

In *Chamarbaugwala* (1957), the promoter of a prize competition printed and published a weekly newspaper from Bangalore (Mysore State), but solicited entries, collected fees through local depots, and enjoyed wide circulation in Bombay State. Upholding Bombay's tax on the promoter's Bombay collections, the Supreme Court ruled that the standing invitation and active business inside Bombay supplied an unmistakable territorial nexus. Similarly, in *State of Bihar v. Charusila Dasi* (1959), Bihar's Hindu Religious Trusts Act validly reached trust properties situated outside Bihar because the trust itself was located and managed inside Bihar.

#### Four Territorial Exceptions to Parliament's Plenary Reach

Parliament's power to legislate for the entire territory of India is not absolute; the Constitution carves out four distinct regional exceptions designed to protect vulnerable Union Territories, tribal autonomy, and asymmetric federal compacts:

- **President's Regulations for Specified Union Territories (`Article 240`):** The President is empowered to make regulations for the peace, progress, and good government of **Andaman and Nicobar Islands**, **Lakshadweep**, **Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu**, and **Ladakh**. Such a Presidential regulation carries the **same force and effect as an Act of Parliament** and may even **repeal or amend any Act of Parliament** or other existing law in its application to that Union Territory. For **Puducherry**, however, a crucial caveat applies: the President may make regulations **only when its Legislative Assembly is suspended or dissolved** (never while a functioning Assembly sits).
- **Fifth Schedule — Scheduled Areas:** The **Governor** of a State containing Scheduled Areas (currently 10 States) is empowered to direct by public notification that any particular Act of Parliament (or of the State Legislature) shall **not apply** to a Scheduled Area or any part thereof, or shall apply subject to specified **modifications and exceptions**.
- **Sixth Schedule — Tribal Areas of the Northeast:** Here the Constitution creates a famous asymmetry between **Assam** and the other three Sixth Schedule States (**Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram**). In **Assam**, the **Governor** may direct that an Act of Parliament does not apply to a tribal area (autonomous district) or applies with specified modifications. In **Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram**, however, the power to bar or modify an **Act of Parliament** vests solely in the **President** (while the respective **Governor** exercises that power only with respect to *State Legislature* Acts!).
- **Special Asymmetric Protections (`Articles 371A` & `371G`):** Under `Article 371A` (**Nagaland**) and `Article 371G` (**Mizoram**), no Act of Parliament relating to religious or social practices of the Nagas/Mizos, customary law and procedure, administration of civil and criminal justice involving customary law, or ownership and transfer of land and its resources applies to those States **unless the respective State Legislative Assembly so decides by a resolution**.

> [!prelims]
> **High-Yield Territorial Exceptions Traps (`Art. 245`):**
> 1. **Sixth Schedule Split:** For **Assam**, the **Governor** modifies or excludes Acts of Parliament; for **Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram**, the **President**—*not* the Governor—exercises that authority over Parliamentary Acts!
> 2. **Puducherry under `Art. 240`:** The President can issue `Art. 240` regulations for Puducherry **only** when its Assembly is dissolved or suspended—whereas for A&N Islands, Lakshadweep, DNH&DD, and Ladakh, the power is continuous.
> 3. **Extra-Territorial Legislation:** Only Parliament can enact extra-territorial laws (`Art. 245(2)`); States can never claim extra-territorial competence, though valid State laws may affect external actors through the **Doctrine of Territorial Nexus**.

### Distribution of Legislative Subjects: Seventh Schedule, Article 246A & Residuary Powers

While `Article 245` draws the geographical map of legislative power, `Article 246` divides the substantive subject-matter of governance across three exhaustive lists housed in the **Seventh Schedule**. The three-list blueprint was adapted directly from the **Government of India Act, 1935** (which featured Federal, Provincial, and Concurrent Lists), while the specific design of the **Concurrent List** drew inspiration from the **Australian Constitution**.

#### The Three Lists: Exact Counts, Amendments & Key UPSC Entries

Over seven decades of constitutional amendments—most notably the `7th` (1956), `42nd` (1976), `88th` (2003), and `101st` (2016) Amendments—entries have been inserted using lettered suffixes (such as `2A`, `92A`, `92B`) and omitted without renumbering the final serial entry. Aspirants must know **both** the traditional pre-GST counts commonly cited in older textbooks and the exact active counts after the **`101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016` (GST)**, which omitted **List I Entries `92` and `92C`** and **List II Entries `52` and `55`** (while substituting List I Entry `84` and List II Entries `54` and `62`):

| Seventh Schedule List | Original (1950) | Pre-GST Count | Active Count (Post-101st Amdt) | Exclusive / Shared Authority | High-Yield UPSC Subject Entries |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **List I — Union List** | 97 *(last entry 97)* | 100 | **98** | **Parliament** exclusively | Defence, armed forces, atomic energy, foreign affairs, war & peace, citizenship, railways, highways, shipping, airways, RBI & currency, foreign loans, banking, insurance, stock exchanges, **Census**, interstate trade & commerce, **Entry 2A** (CAPFs in States), **Entry 52** (declared industries), **Entry 54** (mines/minerals regulation), **Entry 97** (residuary) |
| **List II — State List** | 66 *(last entry 66)* | 61 | **59** | **State Legislatures** (in normal times) | **Public order, police**, public health & sanitation, **intoxicating liquors (`Entry 8`)**, local government, **agriculture**, land & land revenue, irrigation, fisheries, **markets & fairs**, betting & gambling, prisons, **taxes on lands & buildings (`Entry 49`)**, **taxes on mineral rights (`Entry 50`)**, capitation taxes, State excise on liquor |

Three structural details of `Article 246` carry decisive Prelims significance:

- **Parliament's Power Over Union Territories (`Article 246(4)`):** Parliament has plenary power to make laws with respect to **any matter**—including subjects enumerated in the **State List**—for any part of the territory of India not included in a State (i.e., Union Territories). Even for UTs with legislatures (Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu & Kashmir), Parliament retains overriding concurrent competence over State List subjects (with Delhi's Assembly further excluded from List II Entries `1` [Public Order], `2` [Police], and `18` [Land] under `Article 239AA`).
- **Strict Federal Precedence Hierarchy:** `Article 246` establishes an explicit priority order through its opening *non-obstante* ("notwithstanding") and "subject to" clauses: **`Union List > Concurrent List > State List`**. If an irreconcilable overlap arises between the Union List and the State List, the Union List prevails; between Union and Concurrent, the Union List prevails; between Concurrent and State, the Concurrent List prevails.
- **The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 (Five Shifted Subjects):** The Emergency-era `42nd Amendment` significantly tilted the Seventh Schedule toward the Centre by transferring **five crucial subjects** from the **State List (List II)** to the **Concurrent List (List III)**:
  1. **Education** (including technical education, medical education, and universities — moved from Entry 11, List II to Entry 25, List III).
  2. **Forests** (moved from Entry 19, List II to Entry 17A, List III).
  3. **Weights and Measures** (except establishment of standards, which was already in List I — moved from Entry 29, List II to Entry 33A, List III).
  4. **Protection of Wild Animals and Birds** (moved from Entry 20, List II to Entry 17B, List III).
  5. **Administration of Justice; constitution and organisation of all courts, except the Supreme Court and the High Courts** (moved from Entry 3, List II to Entry 11A, List III).
  In addition, the `42nd Amendment` inserted **Entry `2A` in List I** (deployment of Union armed forces in any State in aid of civil power) and **Entry `20A` in List III** (**Population control and family planning**).

> **Mnemonic for the 5 Subjects Shifted by the 42nd Amendment (1976) — `E-F-W-W-A`:**
> **E**ducation · **F**orests · **W**eights & Measures · **W**ild Animals & Birds · **A**dministration of Justice (subordinate courts).
>
> **Classic UPSC Prelims Pair Traps:**
> - **Census** = `Union List (Entry 69)` vs **Population Control & Family Planning** = `Concurrent List (Entry 20A)`.
> - **Agriculture** & **Public Health** = `State List` vs **Forests** & **Education** = `Concurrent List`.
> - **Preventive Detention** for defence/foreign affairs/security of India = `Union List (Entry 9)` vs Preventive Detention for security of a State, public order, or essential supplies = `Concurrent List (Entry 3)`.
> - **Inter-State Trade & Commerce** = `Union List (Entry 42)` vs **Intra-State Trade & Commerce** = `State List (Entry 26)` vs **Trade/Commerce in essential foodstuffs/cattle fodder/raw cotton** = `Concurrent List (Entry 33)`.

#### Special Provision for Goods and Services Tax (`Article 246A`)

Inserted by the **`101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016`**, **`Article 246A`** broke the classic watertight mold of `Article 246` by conferring a **simultaneous, non-obstante taxing power** on both tiers of government:

- **`Article 246A(1)`:** Notwithstanding anything contained in `Articles 246` and `254`, **Parliament** and the **Legislature of every State** possess simultaneous power to make laws with respect to Goods and Services Tax (GST) imposed by the Union or by such State.
- **`Article 246A(2)`:** **Parliament** enjoys **exclusive power** to make laws with respect to GST where the supply of goods, or of services, or both takes place in the course of **inter-State trade or commerce** (which under the Explanation to `Article 269A(1)` also encompasses **imports** into the territory of India).

As the Supreme Court later underlined in ***Union of India v. Mohit Minerals* (2022)**, `Article 246A` is uniquely egalitarian in federal terms: unlike List III subjects governed by `Article 254`, `Article 246A` contains **no repugnancy clause** subordinating State GST laws to Parliamentary GST laws. Both legislatures operate as equal, simultaneous sovereigns coordinated politically through the GST Council.

#### Residuary Powers of Legislation (`Article 248` & Entry `97`, List I)

Any subject not enumerated in the Union, State, or Concurrent Lists falls under **residuary powers**. Under **`Article 248(1)`** read with **`Entry 97` of List I**, **Parliament has exclusive power** to legislate on all residuary matters—and **`Article 248(2)`** explicitly confirms that this includes the **exclusive power to impose any tax** not mentioned in the State or Concurrent Lists (e.g., Gift Tax, Expenditure Tax, Wealth Tax, and Service Tax prior to 2003).

Comparatively, India's allocation of residuary powers tracks the **Canadian model** (vesting them in the strong federal Centre) and departs deliberately from three other constitutional precedents:
- **United States & Australia:** Only federal powers are enumerated; all unenumerated residuary powers remain with the **States**.
- **Government of India Act, 1935:** Residuary powers were assigned to neither the Federal Legislature nor the Provincial Legislatures, but left to the personal discretion of the **Governor-General (Viceroy)**.

In ***Union of India v. H.S. Dhillon* (1971, 7-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court held that when examining Parliament's competence under `Entry 97, List I`, the decisive test is negative: *Does the subject fall in List II (State List)?* If the answer is no, Parliament is automatically competent—whether under a specific Union entry, a Concurrent entry, or `Entry 97` residuary power.

### Parliamentary Legislation in the State Field: Five Extraordinary Circumstances

The Seventh Schedule's distribution governs **normal peacetime**. To prevent federal rigidity from paralysing national security, international obligations, or urgent inter-state coordination, the Constitution empowers **Parliament to legislate directly on State List (List II) subjects in five extraordinary circumstances**—four housed in Part XI (`Articles 249, 250, 252, 253`) and one in Part XVIII (`Article 356`). Every UPSC aspirant must master the exact trigger, voting threshold, expiry rule, and impact on State legislative power across all five routes.

```flow
# Five Extraordinary Routes for Parliament to Legislate on the State List (List II)
Normal Rule (Art. 246(3)) | State Legislatures hold exclusive competence over all 59 active entries in List II
Art. 249 (National Interest) | RS resolution by 2/3rd present & voting; valid 1 yr (renewable); expires 6 mo after || Art. 250 (National Emergency) | Active during Art. 352; expires 6 mo after Emergency ends; State retains concurrent power || Art. 252 (Consent of 2+ States) | Requesting States surrender power; ONLY Parliament can amend or repeal
Art. 253 (International Treaties) | No State consent needed; permanent override to implement treaties/conventions || Art. 356 (President's Rule) | Parliament/President legislates for State; law SURVIVES after rule ends until State alters it
Constitutional Safeguard | Outside Art. 252 & Art. 356 suspension, State Legislatures stay intact—Central law prevails only to the extent of repugnancy (Art. 251)
```

#### 1. When Rajya Sabha Passes a Resolution in National Interest (`Article 249`)

If the **Rajya Sabha** (Council of States) declares by a resolution supported by **not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting** that it is necessary or expedient in the **national interest** that Parliament should make laws with respect to GST (`Article 246A`, inserted by `101st Amendment`) or any matter enumerated in the **State List**, Parliament becomes competent to legislate on that matter for the whole or any part of India.

- **Critical Majority Nuance:** Unlike an `Article 368` constitutional amendment, `Article 249` requires **ONLY two-thirds of members present and voting**—there is **NO requirement of a majority of the total membership** of the Rajya Sabha! Why is the Rajya Sabha exclusively entrusted with this gatekeeping role? Because as the federal second chamber representing the States, its supermajority signals collective State acquiescence to temporary centralisation.
- **Duration & Expiry:** The resolution remains in force for a period not exceeding **one year**; it may be renewed any number of times, but not exceeding **one year at a time**. Any Parliamentary law enacted under `Article 249` ceases to have effect on the expiration of **six months** after the resolution ceases to be in force (except as respects things done or omitted before expiry).
- **State's Residual Power (`Article 251`):** `Article 249` does **not** strip State Legislatures of their power to legislate on the same subject; however, in case of repugnancy between the State law and the Parliamentary law, the **Parliamentary law prevails** so long as it remains operative. (Historically invoked in 1950–51 for trade/commerce and evacuated property, and in 1986 for creating a security belt along international borders.)

#### 2. During a National Emergency (`Article 250`)

While a **Proclamation of National Emergency (`Article 352`)** is in operation, Parliament automatically acquires power to make laws for the whole or any part of India with respect to **any matter in the State List** (and GST under `Art. 246A`).

- **Expiry Rule:** Like `Article 249`, laws enacted by Parliament under `Article 250` cease to have effect upon the expiry of **six months** after the National Emergency ceases to operate (saving past actions).
- **State's Residual Power (`Article 251`):** State Legislatures are **neither suspended nor dissolved** during a National Emergency; they retain concurrent power to legislate on State List subjects, subject to Parliamentary supremacy in case of conflict (`Art. 251`).

#### 3. When Two or More States Make a Request (`Article 252`)

If the Legislatures of **two or more States** pass resolutions declaring it desirable that any State List matter should be regulated in those States by Parliament by law, Parliament may enact a law for regulating that matter.

- **Territorial Application & Adoption:** Such an Act applies **only to the requesting States** and to any other State whose Legislature subsequently **adopts** it by passing a resolution to that effect.
- **Permanent Surrender of State Competence (Perennial Prelims Trap!):** Unlike `Articles 249` and `250`, passing a resolution under `Article 252` amounts to a **complete abdication** of legislative jurisdiction over that statute by the requesting and adopting States. Once Parliament passes the Act, **ONLY Parliament can amend or repeal it**—a participating State Legislature **cannot** unilaterally amend, modify, or repeal the Act even within its own territory!
- **Landmark Statutes Enacted Under `Article 252`:** The **Prize Competitions Act, 1955**; the **Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972** (prior to wildlife moving to List III in 1976); the **Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974**; the **Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976**; and the **Transplantation of Human Organs Act, 1994**.

#### 4. To Implement International Agreements, Treaties and Conventions (`Article 253`)

Notwithstanding anything in the federal distribution of powers, **`Article 253`** empowers Parliament to make any law for the whole or any part of the territory of India for implementing **any treaty, agreement, or convention** with any other country or countries, or **any decision made at any international conference, association, or other body**—even when the subject lies squarely inside the **State List**!

- **No State Consent Required:** Parliament does **not** require State resolutions or Rajya Sabha supermajorities to invoke `Article 253`. The framers recognised that India must speak with one voice in international law and honour its multilateral commitments without being vetoed by provincial legislatures (contrast this with Canada, where judicial rulings like the *Labour Conventions Case*, 1937, restricted federal treaty implementation over provincial fields).
- **Landmark Statutes Enacted Under `Article 253`:** The **United Nations (Privileges and Immunities) Act, 1947**; the **Geneva Conventions Act, 1960**; the **Anti-Hijacking Act, 1982**; the **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986** (implementing the 1972 Stockholm Conference); the **Biological Diversity Act, 2002**; and intellectual-property amendments complying with WTO/TRIPS (such as the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act, 2001). Note, however, that treaty implementation cannot override Fundamental Rights or cede Indian territory without an `Article 368` constitutional amendment (***Berubari Union***, 1960).

#### 5. During President's Rule in a State (`Article 356` & `Article 357`)

When **President's Rule** is imposed in a State under **`Article 356`**, the President may declare that the powers of the State Legislature shall be exercisable by or under the authority of **Parliament**. Under `Article 357`, Parliament may in turn delegate this law-making authority to the **President** (who enacts "President's Acts" in consultation with a parliamentary committee) or authorise the President to sub-delegate it to another authority.

- **The Crucial Post-Revocation Survival Rule:** Unlike laws under `Articles 249` and `250` (which die six months after the resolution or emergency ends), a law made by Parliament or the President under `Article 356` is **NOT coterminous with President's Rule**. Under **`Article 357(2)`**, such a law **continues to remain in full force even after President's Rule is revoked**, until it is expressly **repealed, altered, or re-enacted** by the restored State Legislature!

| Route | Constitutional Trigger & Majority | State Consent Needed? | Duration & Expiry of Parliamentary Law | State Legislature's Competence | Key Examples / Traps |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **`Art. 249`** | Rajya Sabha resolution in **"national interest"** (**2/3rd present & voting**) | Indirect (via RS) | Resolution lasts **1 yr** (renewable); law expires **6 months** after resolution ends | Retains concurrent power; Parliamentary law prevails (`Art. 251`) | No >50% total strength rule; covers State List & `Art. 246A` |
| **`Art. 250`** | **National Emergency** (`Art. 352`) in operation | No | Law expires **6 months** after Emergency ceases to operate | Retains concurrent power; Parliamentary law prevails (`Art. 251`) | State Assembly is NOT suspended during `Art. 352` |
| **`Art. 252`** | Resolutions passed by Legislatures of **2 or more States** | **Yes** (requesting/adopting States only) | **Permanent** until amended or repealed by Parliament | **Surrendered:** ONLY Parliament can amend or repeal the Act | Wildlife Act 1972, Water Act 1974, Human Organs Act 1994 |
| **`Art. 253`** | Implementing **international treaties, conventions, or conference decisions** | **No** | **Permanent** until Parliament alters it | Overridden on the treaty field | EPA 1986, Geneva Conventions Act 1960, TRIPS laws |
| **`Art. 356` / `357`** | Proclamation of **President's Rule** in a State | No | **Survives** indefinitely after President's Rule ends | Suspended during rule; restored Assembly may later alter/repeal (`Art. 357(2)`) | Does NOT auto-expire after 6 months! |

### Judicial Doctrines Interpreting Legislative Competence & Repugnancy

Because legislative entries in the Seventh Schedule are broad thematic heads rather than surgical definitions, overlaps between Union and State statutes are inevitable. Over seventy-five years, the Federal Court, Privy Council, and Supreme Court have refined **five classic interpretive doctrines**—frequently tested in both UPSC Prelims conceptual MCQs and Mains GS-2 federalism answers.

#### 1. Doctrine of Pith and Substance

When a statute enacted by one legislature incidentally touches upon a subject allotted to another legislature, courts do **not** strike it down mechanically. Instead, they examine the enactment as a whole to ascertain its **"pith and substance"**—its true nature, core character, and dominant objective. If the legislation in substance falls within the enacting legislature's allotted List, it remains **constitutionally valid** even if it causes an incidental encroachment into another List.

- **Foundational Precedents:** In ***Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee v. Bank of Commerce, Khulna* (1947, Privy Council)**, the Bengal Money-Lenders Act (Provincial List) incidentally affected promissory notes (Federal List); it was upheld because its pith and substance was moneylending relief. In ***State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara* (1951)**, the Bombay Prohibition Act validly prohibited possession and sale of foreign liquor even though "import and export across customs frontiers" was in the Union List, because its pith and substance was intoxicating liquor (`Entry 8, List II`). Similarly, in ***State of Rajasthan v. G. Chawla* (1959)**, a State law restricting loudspeaker amplifiers was upheld under "public health and sanitation" (`Entry 6, List II`), despite amplifiers touching Union communication devices (`Entry 31, List I`).

#### 2. Doctrine of Colourable Legislation

Rooted in the Latin maxim *quando aliquid prohibetur ex directo, prohibetur et per obliquum* (**"what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly"**), the Doctrine of Colourable Legislation polices **disguised transgressions of legislative competence**. If a legislature lacks authority over a subject, it cannot dress up an ultra vires enactment in a constitutional disguise to achieve an unauthorised result indirectly.

- **Crucial Prelims Trap — Competence, NOT Motive:** In ***K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa* (1953)**, the Supreme Court emphasised that colourable legislation has **nothing to do with legislative motive, malice, or *bona fides***. If a legislature *possesses* constitutional competence, its statute cannot be struck down as "colourable" even if its political motive is questionable; conversely, if it *lacks* competence, the purest public motive cannot save it. In ***State of Bihar v. Kameshwar Singh* (1952)**—the rare case where the doctrine actually invalidated a statute—provisions of the Bihar Land Reforms Act pretending to lay down "principles of compensation" while actually paying zero compensation were struck down as colourable legislation.

#### 3. Doctrine of Incidental and Ancillary Powers

An express grant of legislative power over a Seventh Schedule entry carries with it all **subsidiary, incidental, and ancillary powers** reasonably necessary to make the main legislative power effective and meaningful. As the Federal Court held in ***United Provinces v. Atiqa Begum* (1940)**, legislative entries must receive the widest possible liberal construction. Thus, the power to levy a sales or goods tax necessarily includes ancillary power to mandate invoicing, conduct searches/seizures, impose penalties for evasion, and provide refunds (***R.S. Joshi v. Ajit Mills Ltd.***, 1977)—though in ***R. Abdul Quader & Co. v. Sales Tax Officer* (1964)**, the Court cautioned that ancillary power cannot be stretched to confiscate amounts collected on transactions the State had no competence to tax in the first place.

#### 4. Doctrine of Harmonious Construction

Where two entries in different Lists—or within the same List—appear to conflict or overlap on the surface, courts must attempt to **read them harmoniously**, giving full effect to both so that neither entry is rendered dead, redundant, or stripped of its core content. Only when every reasonable effort at reconciliation fails does the Constitution's `Article 246` supremacy rule (**`Union > Concurrent > State`**) step in to resolve the clash. In ***Calcutta Gas Co. (Proprietary) Ltd. v. State of West Bengal* (1962)**, the Court harmonised List II `Entry 25` ("gas and gas-works") with List I `Entry 52` ("declared industries") so that State authority over gas-works was preserved without emptying the Union's industrial-control power.

#### 5. Doctrine of Repugnancy (`Article 254`)

`Article 254` resolves conflicts strictly within the **Concurrent List (List III)** (never direct List I vs List II clashes, which are governed by `Article 246` and *Pith and Substance*—see ***Hoechst Pharmaceuticals Ltd. v. State of Bihar***, 1983). In ***M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India* (1979, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court distilled the **three tests of repugnancy**: (i) a **direct, irreconcilable conflict** where obeying one statute forces disobedience of the other; (ii) Parliament evinces a clear intent to enact an **exhaustive code covering the entire occupied field** (even absent direct textual collision); or (iii) both laws legislate on the **identical subject-matter** creating overlapping rules/penalties.

Once repugnancy on a Concurrent List subject is established, **`Article 254`** operates through a strict **three-step rule-exception-proviso sequence**:

1. **General Rule (`Article 254(1)`):** If any provision of a State law is repugnant to any provision of a Parliamentary law (whether enacted **before or after** the State law) on a Concurrent List matter, the **Parliamentary law shall prevail**, and the State law shall, **to the extent of the repugnancy**, be **void**.
2. **The Presidential Assent Exception (`Article 254(2)`):** If a State law on a Concurrent List matter contains provisions repugnant to an *earlier* Parliamentary law, and the State Bill has been **reserved for the consideration of the President and has received Presidential assent**, then that **State law shall prevail in that State**! (This mechanism lets States tailor central Concurrent frameworks—such as labour codes, land acquisition amendments, or procedural rules—to regional realities with Union oversight.)
3. **The Parliamentary Override Proviso (`Proviso to Article 254(2)`):** Even after the President grants assent to a State law under `Article 254(2)`, **nothing prevents Parliament from subsequently enacting another law adding to, amending, varying, or REPEALING that very State law!** Notably, this proviso empowers Parliament not merely to pass a new superseding law, but to **directly repeal a State statute**—a remarkable assertion of Parliamentary primacy on Concurrent subjects (see ***Zaverbhai Amaidas v. State of Bombay***, 1954).

### Centre's Control Over State Legislation

Beyond direct Parliamentary law-making on the State List, the Constitution arms the Union executive with **four potent checks over bills passed by State Legislatures**—creating what critics describe as a central legislative veto over provincial democracy:

1. **Reservation of State Bills by the Governor (`Articles 200` & `201`):** Under `Article 200`, when a Bill passed by a State Legislature is presented to the Governor, the Governor may **reserve the Bill for the consideration of the President**. Reservation is **mandatory** whenever a Bill, in the Governor's opinion, would **derogate from the powers of the High Court** so as to endanger its constitutional position; it is also constitutionally required to attract specific protections under `Articles 31A`, `31C`, `254(2)`, or `288(2)`. Once reserved, **`Article 201`** vests the President with an effective **absolute veto**: the President may assent, withhold assent, or direct the Governor to return a non-Money Bill for reconsideration within six months—yet **even if the State Legislature re-passes the Bill and sends it back, the President is NOT bound to give assent** (unlike `Article 111`, where the President *must* assent to a re-passed Parliamentary Bill!).
2. **Prior Presidential Sanction for Trade-Restricting Bills (`Article 304(b)`):** No Bill imposing reasonable restrictions on the freedom of trade, commerce, or intercourse within or with a State in the public interest may be **introduced or moved** in a State Legislature without the **previous sanction of the President**. Crucially, under **`Article 255`**, failure to obtain prior Presidential sanction is treated merely as a **procedural irregularity**—if the Bill subsequently receives **Presidential assent**, the law is completely cured and valid!
3. **Reservation of State Money Bills During Financial Emergency (`Article 360(4)(a)(ii)`):** While a Financial Emergency is in operation, the President may direct any State to reserve **all Money Bills and other Financial Bills** for Presidential consideration after they are passed by the State Legislature.
4. **Presidential Assent for Inter-State River Authority Taxation (`Article 288(2)`):** Any State legislation imposing taxes on water or electricity stored, generated, consumed, or sold by an authority established by Parliament for regulating an inter-state river or river valley has **no effect** unless reserved for and assented to by the President.

> - **Five-Judge Constitution Bench Advisory Opinion (`20 November 2025`, `2025 INSC 1333`):** Answering a 14-question **`Article 143(1)` Presidential Reference** (referred on 13 May 2025), a unanimous five-judge Bench led by **CJI B.R. Crucially, however, the Constitution Bench affirmed that Governors **cannot sit indefinitely** on Bills without constitutional justification—prolonged, unexplained inaction remains open to **limited judicial review** directing timely constitutional action.

## 2. Administrative Relations (Part XI, Articles 256–263)

Division of legislative competence would remain an empty paper exercise unless matched by smooth administrative coordination across the Union and the 28 States. Unlike classic dual federations such as the United States—where federal departments maintain a massive parallel field bureaucracy across every state to execute federal statutes—India's Constitution entrusts **State administrative machinery** with the day-to-day execution of not only State laws and Concurrent laws, but also a vast range of Union laws and central schemes. To ensure that this shared administrative apparatus does not fracture under political divergence between New Delhi and State capitals, `Part XI, Chapter II` (`Articles 256–263`), reinforced by `Articles 312`, `315–317`, `355`, and `365`, constructs an intricate framework of co-extensive executive authority, binding Union directions, mutual delegation, and integrated all-India institutions.

### Distribution of Executive Power (Articles 73 & 162)

The foundational rule governing Centre–State administrative relations is that **executive power is co-extensive with legislative power**—the tier that makes the law ordinarily administers it—subject to one extraordinarily important federal rule on the Concurrent List:

- **Union Executive Power (`Article 73`):** Extends to the whole of India with respect to (i) all matters on which **Parliament has exclusive power to make laws** (`List I — Union List`), and (ii) the exercise of rights, authority, and jurisdiction exercisable by the Government of India by virtue of any **treaty or agreement**.
- **State Executive Power (`Article 162`):** Extends to the territory of the State with respect to all matters on which the **State Legislature has exclusive power to make laws** (`List II — State List`).
- **The Concurrent List Rule (`Proviso to Article 73(1)` & `Proviso to Article 162`):** Because *both* Parliament and States can legislate on **Concurrent List (List III)** subjects, who executes a Parliamentary law passed on a Concurrent subject? The Constitution answers unambiguously: **executive power on Concurrent List matters ordinarily vests in the STATES**—*except* where the **Constitution itself** or a **Parliamentary statute expressly confers executive power on the Centre** or its authorities! Thus, when Parliament enacts the Essential Commodities Act, Criminal Procedure laws (BNSS), or the Right to Education Act under the Concurrent List, routine enforcement and administration rest with State Governments unless Parliament explicitly reserves specific executive functions to Union agencies.

> [!prelims]
> **Classic Concurrent List Executive Trap (`Arts. 73 & 162`):**
> A frequent UPSC Prelims statement claims: *"Executive power in respect of subjects in the Concurrent List vests exclusively in the Union."* **FALSE!** Under the proviso to `Article 73(1)`, executive power over Concurrent List matters **ordinarily rests with the States**, unless a specific constitutional provision or Parliamentary Act explicitly vests it in the Centre.

### Obligation of States and the Centre's Directions (Articles 256, 257 & 365)

Because the Union relies heavily on State governments to implement Union laws and protect national infrastructure inside State boundaries, the Constitution places **two positive obligations** on State executive power (`Articles 256` and `257`) and backs them with one of the sharpest coercive sanctions in any federal constitution (`Article 365`).

#### General Obligation & Compliance Directions (`Article 256`)

`Article 256` lays down two interlocking mandates:
1. The executive power of every State shall be so exercised as to **ensure compliance with the laws made by Parliament** and any existing laws which apply in that State.
2. The executive power of the Union shall extend to the **giving of such directions to a State** as may appear to the Government of India to be necessary for that purpose.

#### Non-Impediment & Infrastructure Protection Directions (`Article 257`)

`Article 257` moves beyond mere compliance with Parliamentary statutes to protect the active functioning of Union administration and strategic national assets:
- **General Non-Impediment Rule (`Article 257(1)`):** The executive power of every State must be exercised so as **not to impede or prejudice** the exercise of the executive power of the Union—and the Union may issue whatever directions appear necessary to prevent such obstruction.
- **Means of Communication (`Article 257(2)`):** The Union may direct a State regarding the **construction and maintenance of means of communication** declared in the direction to be of **national or military importance** (without impairing Parliament's separate power over national highways/waterways or the Union's own construction of defence communications).
- **Protection of Railways (`Article 257(3)`):** The Union may give directions to a State as to the measures to be taken for the **protection of the railways** within the State (vital because policing and public order lie in the State List, while railways belong to the Union List!).
- **Mandatory Financial Reimbursement (`Article 257(4)`):** Whenever complying with Union directions under `Article 257(2)` (communications) or `Article 257(3)` (railways) forces a State to incur **extra costs** beyond normal State duties, the Government of India **must reimburse** the agreed sum to the State. In default of agreement, the amount is determined by an **arbitrator appointed by the Chief Justice of India**!

#### Additional Union Direction Powers Outside Part XI

Two further peacetime provisions empower the Union to issue binding administrative directions to States:
- **Welfare of Scheduled Tribes (`Article 339(2)`):** The executive power of the Union extends to directing any State as to the **drawing up and execution of schemes** specified in the direction to be essential for the welfare of the **Scheduled Tribes** in the State.
- **Primary Education in Mother-Tongue (`Article 350A`, inserted by `7th Amendment, 1956`):** The President may issue directions to any State as deemed necessary or proper for securing adequate facilities for **instruction in the mother-tongue at the primary stage of education** to children belonging to linguistic minority groups.

#### The Coercive Sanction — Article 365 (Part XVIII)

What happens if a State defies a valid constitutional direction issued by the Centre under `Articles 256, 257, 339(2), 350A, 353`, or `360`? The answer sits quietly in Part XVIII under **`Article 365`**: where any State has failed to comply with, or to give effect to, **any directions given in the exercise of the executive power of the Union under any of the provisions of the Constitution**, it shall be lawful for the President to hold that a situation has arisen in which the **government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution**.

In plain terms, `Article 365` acts as a direct **constitutional trigger for President's Rule under `Article 356`!** Both the **Sarkaria Commission (1988)** and the **Punchhi Commission (2010)** warned that `Article 365` is a drastic nuclear weapon of **last resort**: the Centre must first explore diplomatic dialogue, Inter-State Council consultations, and clear written warnings before invoking non-compliance to dismiss an elected State government.

### Mutual Delegation of Functions (Articles 258 & 258A)

Strict administrative compartmentalisation can cripple field governance whenever one tier possesses legal competence over a project while the other possesses local manpower or technical expertise. To inject administrative flexibility, the Constitution provides for **mutual delegation of executive functions**—yet with a telling **federal asymmetry** favouring the Centre.

- **Union → State via Executive Agreement (`Article 258(1)`):** The **President** may, **with the consent of the Government of a State**, entrust either conditionally or unconditionally to that Government or its officers functions in relation to any matter to which the executive power of the Union extends.
- **Union → State via Parliamentary Statute (`Article 258(2)`):** A law made by **Parliament** applying in any State may confer powers and impose duties—or authorise such conferment—upon the State or its officers and authorities, **even WITHOUT the consent of the State Government**! (For instance, Parliament routinely obliges State police and district magistrates to enforce central statutes like the Passport Act, Citizenship Act, or Foreigners Act.) Under **`Article 258(3)`**, the Union must pay the State any extra administrative costs incurred under `258(1)` or `258(2)`—again settled, if disputed, by an **arbitrator appointed by the Chief Justice of India**.
- **State → Union via Executive Agreement (`Article 258A`, inserted by `7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956`):** The original 1950 Constitution had a glaring one-way omission: while the President could entrust Union functions to a State, a State had no constitutional mechanism to entrust its own functions to the Union (a hurdle encountered when States wanted the Centre to execute massive river-valley projects like the Hirakud Dam). The `7th Amendment (1956)` plugged this gap by inserting `Article 258A`, enabling the **Governor** of a State, **with the consent of the Government of India**, to entrust conditionally or unconditionally any State executive function to the Union or its officers. Crucially, however, **no State Legislature can unilaterally impose duties on Union officers without Union consent**—preserving an unmistakable asymmetry in Parliament's favour.

### Cooperation Between Centre and States (Articles 261, 262 & 263)

To prevent administrative isolation among 28 States and 8 Union Territories, Part XI embeds three vital cooperative instruments (with inter-state river tribunals and the Inter-State Council analysed exhaustively in **Chapter 9**):

- **Full Faith and Credit (`Article 261`):** Mandates that **"full faith and credit shall be given throughout the territory of India to public acts, records and judicial proceedings of the Union and of every State."** Parliament determines by law the manner and conditions of proving such acts and records (`Art. 261(2)`). Under **`Article 261(3)`**, final judgments or orders delivered by **civil courts** in any part of India are capable of execution anywhere within India. **Crucial Prelims Distinction:** `Article 261(3)` applies strictly to **civil judgments**—it does *not* compel one State's courts to enforce the **criminal/penal laws** of another State!
- **Adjudication of Inter-State River Water Disputes (`Article 262`):** Empowers Parliament by law to provide for the adjudication of any dispute or complaint regarding the use, distribution, or control of waters of any inter-state river or river valley, and uniquely authorises Parliament to **bar the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and all other courts** over such disputes (implemented via the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956, though the Supreme Court clarified in *State of Karnataka v. State of Tamil Nadu*, 2018, that `Article 136` Special Leave appeals against Tribunal awards remain maintainable).
- **Inter-State Council (`Article 263`):** Authorises the President to establish an Inter-State Council whenever public interest appears served, tasked with inquiring into and advising upon inter-state disputes, investigating subjects of common interest, and recommending better coordination of policy and action (constituted permanently in **1990** on the Sarkaria Commission's recommendation; chaired by the Prime Minister).
- **Trade & Commerce Authority (`Article 307`):** Empowers Parliament to appoint an appropriate authority to carry out the purposes of constitutional freedom of inter-state trade, commerce, and intercourse (`Articles 301–304`)—though Parliament has never constituted such a body to date.

### All-India Services (Article 312): The 'Steel Frame' and Federal Tensions

No feature of India's administrative federalism is more unique—or more vigorously debated—than the **All-India Services (AIS)** under **`Article 312`**. Whereas classical federations like the United States maintain two completely separate bureaucracies (Federal Civil Services for Washington and State Civil Services for individual states), India overlays a **third, shared tier**: elite officers recruited and trained centrally by the Union who serve across both State administrations and Union ministries throughout their careers.

#### Constitutional Origin, Current Services & Creation Rule

At Independence, two colonial "Imperial Services" were transformed into constitutional All-India Services deemed created by Parliament under `Article 312(2)`: the **Indian Administrative Service (IAS)** (succeeding the ICS) and the **Indian Police Service (IPS)** (succeeding the IP). Parliament enacted the **All-India Services Act, 1951** to regulate their recruitment and conditions of service. In **1966**, following a 1963 amendment to the 1951 Act, a third All-India Service was constituted: the **Indian Forest Service (IFoS)**.

Creating any *new* All-India Service—including an **All-India Judicial Service (AIJS)**, explicitly added to `Article 312(1)` and `(3)–(4)` by the **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`**—follows a distinctive federal gatekeeping procedure:

1. The **Rajya Sabha** must first pass a resolution supported by **not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting** declaring that it is necessary or expedient in the **national interest** to create one or more All-India Services common to the Union and the States.
2. Only thereafter may **Parliament by law** create the service and regulate recruitment and conditions of service.
3. Crucially, under **`Article 312(4)`**, such a law (including for AIJS, covering posts **not inferior to that of a District Judge**) is **NOT deemed a constitutional amendment under `Article 368`** even if it amends other provisions of the Constitution! (Despite repeated Law Commission and parliamentary committee recommendations, the AIJS remains unconstituted due to resistance from several High Courts and State Governments over local language proficiency and judicial independence.)

#### The Architecture of Dual Control

All-India Services embody a calibrated **"dual control"** design:
- **Recruitment, Training & Cadre Allocation:** Conducted centrally by the **Union Public Service Commission (UPSC)** and controlled by Union cadre-controlling authorities—**DoPT** (Ministry of Personnel) for IAS, **Ministry of Home Affairs** for IPS, and **Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change** for IFoS. Officers are allotted to specific State cadres (or Joint Cadres like AGMUT) and move periodically to the Union on **Central Deputation**.
- **Immediate Control vs Ultimate Control:** While serving in a State, **immediate operational control**—posting, transfer, leave, annual performance appraisal, and temporary suspension—lies with the **State Government**. However, **ultimate disciplinary control**—dismissal, removal from service, or compulsory retirement (protected under `Article 311`)—vests exclusively in the **Central Government (President)**!

#### Founding Vision vs Federal Critique & the 2022–2025 IAS Cadre Rules Row

In the Constituent Assembly, both **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** and **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** ("Patron Saint of All-India Services") defended this deliberate departure from pure dual federalism as existential to India's survival:

> "There is no alternative to this administrative system... The Union will go, you will not have a united India if you do not have good All-India Service which has the independence to speak out its mind, which has sense of security that you will stand by your work... Remove them and I see nothing but a picture of chaos all over the country." — Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, Constituent Assembly Debates, 10 October 1949

Ambedkar similarly argued on 4 November 1948 that without depriving States of their own State Civil Services, every country possesses **"strategic posts"** (District Collectors, Superintendents of Police, Chief Secretaries, Principal Secretaries) that must be manned by a uniform, merit-recruited national cadre to maintain administrative quality across the Union. Supporters highlight four systemic gains: (i) uniformly high administrative standards nationwide; (ii) **national integration** through the insider–outsider cadre allocation formula; (iii) **cross-fertilisation** of field district realities into Union policy-making at Delhi; and (iv) **constitutional insulation** enabling officers to give fearless advice to State ministers without fear of arbitrary dismissal.

Yet States and constitutional committees have persistently questioned the federal legitimacy of AIS. The **Rajamannar Committee (1969, Tamil Nadu)** bluntly recommended **abolishing the IAS and IPS** and repealing `Article 312`, arguing that AIS violate State administrative autonomy, divide bureaucratic loyalty, and force States to foot the salary bill for officers they cannot ultimately dismiss. While both the **Sarkaria Commission (1988)** and **Punchhi Commission (2010)** firmly **rejected abolition**—reaffirming AIS as vital pillars of unity—they urged greater transparency and consultation in cadre management.

This latent tension flared sharply during **2022–2025** over **Rule `6(1)` of the IAS (Cadre) Rules, 1954**. Citing a steep decline in States sponsoring mid-level IAS officers for Union ministries (officers on central deputation fell from ~14% of total IAS strength in 2014 to ~6.6% in 2021, and mid-career officers from 19% to 10%), the DoPT circulated draft amendments in **January 2022** proposing to empower the Central Government to unilaterally determine the exact number of officers a State must release against its Central Deputation Reserve (CDR, up to 40%), mandate compliance **"within a specified time"** in case of disagreement, and invoke **"public interest"** to requisition specific officers directly without State concurrence. Over a dozen States—spanning both Opposition-ruled States (West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Rajasthan, Chhattisgarh) and several NDA allies—strongly opposed the draft as undermining cooperative federalism and crippling State-level administration, stalling formal notification and highlighting the urgent need for consensus-driven cadre reform (including lateral entry and expanded UPSC recruitment).

> [!debate]
> **All-India Services: Steel Frame of Unity vs Instrument of Administrative Centralisation**
> - **Unity & Governance Case (Patel, Ambedkar, Sarkaria, Punchhi):** Guarantees meritocratic standards in strategic district/secretariat posts; shields civil servants from local political vendetta via `Art. 311` + Union ultimate control; links grassroots implementation to central policymaking; fosters pan-Indian outlook.
> - **Federal Autonomy Case (Rajamannar Committee, State CMs):** Breaches classical federal dual administration; creates dual loyalty where State governments lack ultimate disciplinary authority; central deputation coercion (`Rule 6(1)` row) threatens State administrative capacity; generalist dominance sidelines domain specialists.
> - **Balanced Mains Synthesis:** Retain AIS as an indispensable federal cement, but operationalise **cooperative cadre governance**—fixed minimum tenures (per *T.S.R. Subramanian*, 2013), transparent consultation via the Inter-State Council on central deputation norms, and domain specialisation under **Mission Karmayogi**.

### Public Service Commissions, Integrated Judiciary, Security Agencies & Extra-Constitutional Devices

Five further institutional channels weave Union and State administration into a cohesive—though sometimes contested—fabric:

#### 1. Public Service Commissions (`Articles 315–317`)

- **Removal Safeguard (`Article 317`):** Although the **Chairman and members of a State Public Service Commission (SPSC)** are appointed by the **Governor**, they **can be removed ONLY by the President** (and on grounds of misbehaviour, only after an inquiry and binding report by the Supreme Court!).
- **Joint State Public Service Commission (`Article 315(2)`):** Upon request resolutions passed by the Legislatures of two or more States, **Parliament** may by law create a statutory **Joint State Public Service Commission (JSPSC)**—whose Chairman and members are **both appointed and removed by the President**.
- **UPSC Assistance (`Article 315(4)` & `Article 321`):** The **UPSC** can serve the recruitment needs of a State upon the request of the State Governor and with the **approval of the President**; further, UPSC assists two or more States in framing joint recruitment schemes for specialised services (`Art. 320(2)`).

#### 2. Integrated Judicial, Election & Audit Machinery

- **Single Integrated Judiciary:** Judges of every State **High Court** are appointed by the **President** (in consultation with the CJI and State Governor, via the Collegium system) and may be **transferred** to another High Court by the President or **removed** only by Parliamentary impeachment. Under `Article 231`, Parliament can establish a **Common High Court** for two or more States (e.g., Bombay High Court for Maharashtra and Goa; Punjab & Haryana High Court; Gauhati High Court for Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram, and Arunachal Pradesh).
- **Election Commission of India (`Article 324`):** A single independent central constitutional body supervises elections to **both** Parliament and every State Legislature (whereas State Election Commissions under `Arts. 243K/243ZA` handle only Panchayats and Municipalities).
- **Comptroller and Auditor General (`Articles 148–151`):** A single CAG—appointed and removable like a Supreme Court judge without State participation—audits the accounts of **both the Union and all States**, submitting State audit reports to Governors for laying before State Legislatures (`Art. 151(2)`).

#### 3. Central Security Forces (CAPFs) & Investigative Agencies (CBI)

Security administration sits on one of the sharpest fault lines of Indian federalism. Under the Seventh Schedule, **Public Order (`Entry 1, List II`)** and **Police (`Entry 2, List II`)** belong exclusively to States. Yet **`Article 355`** imposes a constitutional duty on the Union to protect every State against **external aggression and internal disturbance** and to ensure governance runs in accordance with the Constitution. Reinforcing this, the **`42nd Amendment Act, 1976`** inserted **`Entry 2A` in List I**, empowering the Union to deploy **Central Armed Police Forces (CAPFs)**—such as CRPF, BSF, CISF, ITBP, and SSB—in any State in aid of civil power (and even unilaterally when necessary to protect central installations or quell grave disorder, though the Sarkaria Commission advised prior consultation with the State whenever feasible).

Two high-profile administrative controversies dominate contemporary Mains debates:
- **BSF Territorial Jurisdiction Extension (October 2021):** The Union Home Ministry extended the Border Security Force's operational search, seizure, and arrest belt from **15 km to 50 km** inside international borders in **Punjab, West Bengal, and Assam** (bringing it in line with Gujarat's reduction from 80 km to 50 km and Rajasthan's 50 km). Punjab and West Bengal challenged the notification via Assembly resolutions and an `Article 131` suit as encroaching on State police competence.
- **Withdrawal of "General Consent" to the CBI (`Section 6, DSPE Act, 1946`):** Because the Central Bureau of Investigation derives jurisdiction from the **Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946**, it requires the **consent of the State Government (`Section 6`)** to investigate crimes inside any State (unless specifically ordered by the Supreme Court or a High Court under writ jurisdiction). Between 2018 and 2026, nearly a dozen States (including West Bengal, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Punjab, Telangana, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Karnataka, and Jharkhand—with periodic revocations/restorations after electoral shifts) withdrew **"general consent"**, forcing the CBI to seek case-by-case permission. On **10 July 2024**, in ***State of West Bengal v. Union of India***, the Supreme Court held West Bengal's `Article 131` original suit challenging the Union's alleged unilateral CBI registrations post-withdrawal to be legally **maintainable** (clearing the suit for merits adjudication).

#### 4. Administrative Centralisation During Emergencies

Emergencies dramatically convert India's administrative structure from federal to unitary without a single formal constitutional amendment:
- **National Emergency (`Article 353(a)`):** The Union's executive power expands to giving directions to any State as to the **manner in which its executive power is to be exercised on ANY matter**—bringing State governments under complete central administrative control, though State ministries remain in office.
- **President's Rule (`Article 356`):** The President assumes all or any executive functions of the State Government and powers of the Governor (excluding the High Court), administering the State directly through the Governor assisted by Advisors.
- **Financial Emergency (`Article 360`):** The Centre directs States to observe specified canons of financial propriety and may mandate reductions in salaries and allowances of State personnel.

#### 5. Extra-Constitutional & Statutory Consultative Devices

Complementing constitutional provisions, India relies on a dense ecosystem of extra-constitutional and statutory bodies to foster day-to-day administrative harmony:
- **Advisory & Planning Bodies:** **NITI Aayog** (established 1 January 2015 as India's premier cooperative-federalism think tank, featuring a **Governing Council** comprising the Prime Minister, all State Chief Ministers, and UT Chief Ministers/Lieutenant Governors—replacing the Planning Commission and National Development Council); **National Integration Council (NIC)**; **Central Council of Health and Family Welfare**; **Central Council of Local Government**; **University Grants Commission (UGC)**; and **North-Eastern Council** (statutory under NEC Act, 1971).
- **Zonal Councils (Statutory):** Five Zonal Councils (Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, Southern) established under the **States Reorganisation Act, 1956**—each chaired by the **Union Home Minister** with State Chief Ministers rotating annually as Vice-Chairmen (detailed in **Chapter 9**).
- **Periodic All-India Conferences:** **Governors' Conference** (presided over by the President), **Chief Ministers' Conference** (presided over by the Prime Minister), **Chief Secretaries' Conference** (presided over by the Cabinet Secretary), **Conference of DGPs/IGPs** (internal security coordination), **Chief Justices' Conference** (presided over by the CJI), and **Home/Finance/Food Ministers' Conferences**.

## 3. Financial Relations (Part XII, Articles 268–293)

Finance is the lifeblood of any federation: legislative and administrative autonomy quickly becomes illusory if States lack predictable, buoyant revenues to execute their constitutional responsibilities. `Part XII` of the Constitution (`Articles 268–293`) governs the intricate financial architecture between the Union and the States—spanning the allocation of taxing powers, revenue-sharing mechanisms, statutory and discretionary grants-in-aid, intergovernmental borrowing, mutual tax immunities, and emergency financial adjustments.

Deliberately modelled on the Government of India Act, 1935, India's fiscal blueprint embeds a structural **Vertical Fiscal Imbalance (VFI)**: to preserve a unified national economic market, capture economies of scale in collection, and avoid destructive tax competition among provinces, the Constitution assigns the most buoyant, mobile, and broad-based taxes (income tax, corporation tax, customs duties, central excise, and now central/inter-state GST) to the **Union**, while assigning the heaviest social, developmental, and human-capital expenditure duties (agriculture, policing, public health, rural roads, irrigation, and shared education) to the **States**, which together incur **over 60 per cent of India's total general government expenditure**. Compounding this vertical gap is a sharp **Horizontal Fiscal Imbalance (HFI)**—vast inter-state disparities in per capita Gross State Domestic Product (GSDP), industrial density, topography, and tax capacity—requiring continuous constitutional equalisation through revenue devolution and grants.

### Allocation of Taxing Powers and Constitutional Restrictions

The Constitution enforces four foundational principles governing who may levy what tax across India's federal tiers:

- **No Tax Without Authority of Law (`Article 265`):** *"No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law."* Standing as a vital constitutional right outside Part III, `Article 265` bars the executive from imposing or collecting any tax through administrative orders, resolutions, or notifications alone—every tax demands a valid statute passed by a legislature possessing Seventh Schedule competence.
- **Strict Separation of Taxing and Regulatory Entries — No Concurrent Taxation Except GST:** As settled by the Supreme Court in ***M.P.V. Sundararamier & Co. v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (1958)**, the Seventh Schedule rigorously segregates general **regulatory entries** from **taxing entries**; legislative power to regulate an activity never implies ancillary power to tax it. Even more distinctively, to prevent chaotic overlapping taxation across tiers, **the Concurrent List (List III) contains NO taxing entries whatsoever** (Entry `44` deals only with mechanisms of stamp duties, whose rates are split between List I Entry `91` and List II Entry `63`, while Entry `47` covers *fees*, not taxes). Prior to 2016, taxation was strictly mutually exclusive between Union and States. The **`101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016`** created the **sole historic exception** via **`Article 246A`**, granting Parliament and State Legislatures **simultaneous concurrent taxing power over Goods and Services Tax (GST)**.
- **Residuary Power of Taxation (`Article 248(2)` & Entry `97`, List I):** Exclusively vested in **Parliament**. Using this residuary authority, Parliament historically enacted Gift Tax, Wealth Tax, Expenditure Tax, and—prior to the 88th Amendment—Service Tax.
- **Four Express Constitutional Restrictions on State Taxing Powers:** Even inside the State List, States operate under four binding constitutional ceilings:
  1. **Profession Tax Cap (`Article 276`):** A State Legislature may levy taxes on professions, trades, callings, and employments (for the State or local bodies), **provided the total tax payable by any one person does NOT exceed `₹2,500 per annum`** (raised from the original ₹250 cap by the **`60th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1988`**).
  2. **Bar on Out-of-State & Import/Export Taxation (`Article 286`):** Amended by the `101st Amendment`, `Article 286` prohibits any State from taxing the supply of goods or services taking place **outside the State** or in the course of **import into or export out of India**. Parliament formulates principles for determining when a supply occurs outside a State or in international trade.
  3. **Exemption of Union & Railway Electricity (`Article 287`):** No State law may impose a tax on the consumption or sale of electricity consumed by the **Government of India** or used in the construction, maintenance, or operation of any **railway**, unless expressly authorised by Parliament.
  4. **Inter-State River Authority Water/Electricity (`Article 288`):** Any State law imposing taxes on water or electricity stored, generated, consumed, distributed, or sold by an authority established by Parliament for regulating an inter-state river or river valley is **invalid unless reserved for and assented to by the President**.

#### The Mineral Taxation Watershed: MADA v. SAIL (2024) and the MMDR Amendment Act (2026)

For thirty-five years following ***India Cement Ltd. v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1989, 7-Judge Bench)**, mineral-rich States such as Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, and Rajasthan were barred from levying taxes on mineral rights because the Court had treated mining **royalty** under `Section 9` of the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957 (MMDR Act) as a "tax", holding that Parliament had occupied the field. On **25 July 2024**, a landmark **nine-judge Constitution Bench** (8–1 majority authored by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud; Justice B.V. Nagarathna dissenting) in ***Mineral Area Development Authority (MADA) v. Steel Authority of India Ltd.*** (`2024 INSC 554`) dramatically overturned *India Cement* and revitalised States' fiscal sovereignty:

- **Royalty Is NOT a Tax:** The Bench held that mineral royalty is a **contractual consideration** paid by a mining lessee to the lessor (proprietor of the mineral) for parting with mineral privileges—not a compulsory exaction for public revenue.
- **Dual State Taxing Competence Confirmed:** States possess plenary constitutional competence to tax **mineral rights (`List II, Entry 50`)** as well as **mineral-bearing lands (`List II, Entry 49`)**, and may even use mineral value or royalty as a *measure* for computing land tax under `Entry 49`. The MMDR Act (`List I, Entry 54`—a regulatory entry) imposes no existing limitation on that taxing power.
- **Staggered Recovery of Past Arrears (`14 August 2024` Order):** Rejecting the Centre's plea for purely prospective operation, the Bench allowed States to recover valid past mineral-tax dues dating back to **1 April 2005**, payable by mining companies and PSUs in interest-free, penalty-free **staggered instalments over 12 years commencing 1 April 2026**.

Crucially, however, `Entry 50` of List II qualifies State taxing power as **"subject to any limitations imposed by Parliament by law relating to mineral development."** Exercising that constitutional opening, Parliament enacted the **Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2026** (Presidential assent **17 August 2026**), inserting **`Section 9D`** into the MMDR Act and expanding `Section 2` to encompass "mineral-bearing lands." `Section 9D` prospectively **prohibits States from imposing any tax, cess, or levy on mineral rights OR mineral-bearing lands** except subject to conditions prescribed by the Central Government (under amended `Section 13(ta)`), and retrospectively deems all uncollected past State levies invalid while protecting already-recovered sums from refund. Because `Entry 49` (taxes on lands and buildings) contains **no** textual subordination to Parliament unlike `Entry 50`, whether `Section 9D` can constitutionally fetter States' `Entry 49` land-tax competence has immediately emerged as India's defining fiscal-federalism dispute of 2026.

### Distribution of Tax Revenues: Constitutional Evolution and Articles 268–274

Unlike classic federations where each tier lives solely off its own taxes, the Indian Constitution creates a sophisticated **tax-sharing pipeline** whereby the Union levies and collects major taxes uniformly nationwide and shares the proceeds with States. Three landmark constitutional amendments fundamentally reshaped this pipeline:

1. **`80th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2000` (The "Alternative Scheme of Devolution"):** Under the original 1950 Constitution, only **Income Tax** (`Art. 270`, compulsory) and **Union Excise Duties** (`Art. 272`, permissive) were shared with States—excluding buoyant Corporation Tax and Customs Duties and encouraging the Centre to focus revenue effort on non-shared taxes. Implementing the **10th Finance Commission's (K.C. Pant)** landmark recommendation, the `80th Amendment` repealed `Article 272` and recast `Article 270` to create a single, unified **Divisible Pool** embracing **almost ALL Central taxes and duties** (retrospectively from 1 April 1996, initially setting States' share at 29%).
2. **`88th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003` (Service Tax):** Inserted **`Article 268A`** and `List I, Entry 92C` for **Service Tax** (levied by Centre, collected and appropriated by both Centre and States)—yet `Article 268A` was never formally notified into force and was subsequently **omitted altogether by the `101st Amendment Act, 2016`** when services were absorbed into GST!
3. **`101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016` (GST Overhaul):** Deleted `Article 268A`, removed duties of excise on medicinal and toilet preparations from `Article 268`, inserted **`Article 269A`** for Integrated GST (IGST), revised `Article 270` to bring **CGST** and the Union's share of IGST into the Divisible Pool, and amended **`Article 271`** to expressly **prohibit the Centre from levying surcharges on GST (`Article 246A`)**.

Post the `101st Amendment`, Union tax revenue sharing operates across **five core constitutional categories (`Articles 268–271`)**, guarded procedurally by **`Articles 274` and `279`**:

| Article | Levied By | Collected By | Appropriated / Shared By | Specific Taxes Covered (Post-101st Amendment) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **`Art. 268`** | Centre | **States** (within their borders; Centre in UTs) | **Wholly by States** *(does NOT enter Consolidated Fund of India)* | **Stamp duties** on bills of exchange, cheques, promissory notes, bills of lading, letters of credit, insurance policies, transfer of shares, debentures, proxies & receipts *(excise on medicinal/toilet preparations omitted by 101st Amdt)* |
| **`Art. 269`** | Centre | Centre | **Assigned wholly to States** (per Parliamentary principles) | Taxes on **inter-state sale or purchase of non-GST goods** (5 petroleum products, alcoholic liquor for human consumption) & taxes on **inter-state consignment of goods** |
| **`Art. 269A`** | Centre | Centre | **Apportioned between Centre & States** (by Parliament on **GST Council** recommendations) | **Integrated GST (IGST)** on inter-state supplies of goods/services and on **imports** into India *(State's share does NOT form part of CFI)* |
| **`Art. 270`** | Centre | Centre | **Divisible Pool** distributed between Centre & States by Presidential order on **Finance Commission** advice | Nearly **all Central taxes** (Income tax, Corporation tax, Customs, Central Excise, **CGST**, Union's share of IGST) **EXCEPT** `Arts. 268, 269, 269A` taxes, **`Art. 271` surcharges**, and **specific-purpose cesses** |
| **`Art. 271`** | Centre | Centre | **Exclusively Centre** (zero share to States) | **Surcharge** on taxes in `Arts. 269` and `270` for Union purposes — **strictly PROHIBITED on GST (`Art. 246A`)!** |

#### The Surcharge & Cess Grievance (`Article 271`) and Divisible Pool Erosion

Although `Article 271` prohibits surcharges on GST, Parliament retains unrestricted authority to levy **surcharges** on income tax, corporation tax, customs, and excise (`Arts. 269–270`), as well as **earmarked cesses** (such as the Health and Education Cess, Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess, Road and Infrastructure Cess, and the 2026 Health Security se National Security Cess on pan masala)—**none of which enter the `Article 270` Divisible Pool**! Because the Centre keeps 100 per cent of cesses and surcharges (minus collection costs), heavy reliance on them directly shrinks the fiscal pie shared with States. As documented by the **16th Finance Commission (2026)**, the **Divisible Pool shrank from roughly 89 per cent** of the Union's Gross Tax Revenue (GTR) during the 13th FC period to **about 82 per cent** under the 14th FC and **roughly 78 per cent** under the 15th FC—meaning that while States nominally received **41 per cent of the Divisible Pool**, their effective share of total Union Gross Tax Revenue fell to **roughly 32 per cent**.

#### Procedural & Accounting Safeguards (`Articles 274` & `279`)

- **Protection of States' Interest (`Article 274`):** **Prior recommendation of the President** is mandatory before introducing or moving in either House of Parliament any Bill or amendment that (i) imposes or varies any tax in which States are interested; (ii) varies the meaning of **"agricultural income"** for income-tax enactments; (iii) affects the principles on which monies are distributable to States; or (iv) imposes any `Article 271` surcharge for Union purposes.
- **Certification of "Net Proceeds" (`Article 279`):** "Net proceeds" means gross tax receipts minus the **cost of collection**. Crucially, net proceeds are **ascertained and certified by the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) of India**, whose certificate is **final**! (Accepting the 16th FC's recommendation in February 2026, the Union Government committed to publishing CAG-certified `Article 279` net proceeds data annually for full transparency.)

> [!prelims]
> **Part XII Tax Distribution Traps — Five Classic UPSC Eliminate-on-Sight Facts:**
> 1. **Outside the CFI:** Proceeds collected by States under `Article 268` (stamp duties) and the States' apportioned share of IGST under `Article 269A` **do NOT form part of the Consolidated Fund of India**.
> 2. **No Surcharge on GST:** Under `Article 271` (as amended by `101st Amendment`), Parliament **cannot** levy a central surcharge on GST (`Art. 246A`), though it can levy surcharges on Income Tax, Corporation Tax, Customs, and Excise.
> 3. **Ghost Article Alert (`Art. 268A`):** Service Tax (`88th Amendment, 2003`) was **omitted** by the `101st Amendment, 2016`—do not mark it as active!
> 4. **Who Certifies Net Proceeds?** The **CAG** under `Article 279`—*not* the Finance Commission or Finance Ministry—and the CAG's certificate is final.
> 5. **Prior Presidential Recommendation (`Art. 274`):** Required even for redefining **"agricultural income"** or imposing an `Article 271` surcharge!

### Non-Tax Revenues and Grants-in-Aid to States (Articles 275 & 282)

Alongside taxes, both tiers derive **non-tax revenues** from distinct sovereign and commercial streams: the **Union** collects receipts from posts and telegraphs, railways, RBI dividends and currency/coinage, broadcasting, spectrum auctions, Central PSUs, and escheat/lapse; **States** collect receipts from irrigation, forests, fisheries, State PSUs, mining royalties, and escheat/lapse. Because tax devolution alone cannot eliminate fiscal deficits in structurally disadvantaged or hilly States, the Constitution provides two sharply distinct grant channels—**Statutory Grants (`Article 275`)** and **Discretionary Grants (`Article 282`)**—plus a now-expired historical jute grant (`Article 273`, paid for 10 years until 1960 to Assam, Bihar, Orissa, and West Bengal in lieu of jute export duties).

- **Statutory Grants (`Article 275`):** Empowers **Parliament** to make grants-in-aid out of the Consolidated Fund of India to such States as Parliament determines to be **"in need of assistance"**, with different sums fixed for different States. These grants are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** (non-votable) and are determined on the recommendations of the **Finance Commission**. Crucially, `Article 275(1)` contains **two mandatory constitutional provisos**: Parliament *must* pay statutory grants (i) for promoting the welfare of **Scheduled Tribes** or raising the level of administration of **Scheduled Areas** in any State, and (ii) specifically to **Assam** to meet administrative and development costs of its Sixth Schedule tribal areas.
- **Discretionary Grants (`Article 282`):** Placed under the heading *"Miscellaneous Financial Provisions"*, `Article 282` empowers **BOTH the Union AND a State** to make any grants for **"any public purpose"**, **notwithstanding that the purpose is NOT one with respect to which Parliament or the State Legislature may make laws!** These grants are non-obligatory, **votable**, and outside the Finance Commission's statutory mandate. Originally intended by the framers as a narrow residual provision for unforeseen contingencies, `Article 282` evolved into the massive constitutional conduit first for **Planning Commission Plan grants** (1950–2014) and today for **Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS)**—such as MGNREGA, PM-AWAS, Jal Jeevan Mission, and Samagra Shiksha—allowing the Union to spend heavily on State List domains like agriculture, rural development, and public health with matching cost-sharing conditions (60:40 for general States, 90:10 for NE and Himalayan States).

```compare
# Statutory Grants (Article 275) vs Discretionary Grants (Article 282)
Statutory Grants (Article 275) | Discretionary Grants (Article 282)
Who Can Grant :: Parliament ONLY (Union → States) | BOTH the Union AND any State Government
Constitutional Basis :: States determined by Parliament to be "in need of assistance" | "Any public purpose" — EVEN OUTSIDE the granting tier's Seventh Schedule legislative competence
Institutional Channel :: Recommended by the Finance Commission (Art. 280(3)(b)) | Executive / Union Ministries (historically Planning Commission; now Centrally Sponsored Schemes)
Budgetary Nature :: Charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (non-votable) | Votable expenditure; purely discretionary, no legal obligation on Centre to grant
Mandatory Provisos :: Specific mandatory grants for ST welfare / Scheduled Areas & Assam's 6th Schedule tribal areas | None — entirely conditional upon Union executive policy and matching ratios (60:40 / 90:10)
```

### Borrowing Powers of the Centre and States (Articles 292 & 293)

Public debt management in a federation poses a classic macroeconomic dilemma: unchecked sub-national borrowing can trigger sovereign credit downgrades and nationwide inflation, yet excessive central control can choke States' capital investment. `Articles 292` and `293` resolve this tension firmly in favour of **central macroeconomic supervision**:

- **Borrowing by the Government of India (`Article 292`):** The Union's executive power extends to borrowing **within India OR outside India** upon the security of the Consolidated Fund of India, and giving guarantees, within limits (if any) fixed by Parliament (presently governed by the **Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management [FRBM] Act, 2003**).
- **Borrowing by States (`Article 293`):** Governed by four strict federal constraints:
  1. **Domestic Borrowing Only (`Article 293(1)`):** A State may borrow **ONLY within the territory of India** upon the security of its Consolidated Fund, within limits fixed by its State Legislature (via State FRBM Acts). **States CANNOT borrow directly from foreign governments or international capital markets**; external multilateral loans (World Bank, ADB, JICA) must be contracted through and routed via the Government of India.
  2. **Union Loans & Guarantees (`Article 293(2)`):** The Union Government may extend loans to any State or give guarantees for State loans (subject to conditions set by Parliament).
  3. **Mandatory Union Consent When Indebted (`Article 293(3)`):** A State **may NOT raise any loan without the consent of the Government of India** if there is still outstanding **any part of a loan** made to the State by the Union (or its predecessor) or in respect of which a Union guarantee remains outstanding.
  4. **Power to Impose Conditions (`Article 293(4)`):** The Government of India may grant its consent under `Article 293(3)` **subject to such conditions as it thinks fit to impose**.

Because virtually every Indian State carries legacy central loans (including 50-year interest-free capital expenditure loans and back-to-back GST compensation loans), `Article 293(3)` operates permanently as a master lever: every fiscal year, the Union Ministry of Finance fixes each State's **Net Borrowing Ceiling (NBC)**—ordinarily pegged to **3 per cent of GSDP** (with conditional 0.5% power-sector reform top-ups under the 15th FC).

#### Off-Budget Borrowings and State of Kerala v. Union of India (2024)

To circumvent annual 3% GSDP borrowing ceilings, several States increasingly routed infrastructure and welfare financing through **Off-Budget Borrowings (OBBs)**—loans raised by State-Owned Enterprises, Special Purpose Vehicles, or statutory boards (such as Kerala's KIIFB and KSSPL) whose principal and interest were serviced directly from the State Budget or backed by State guarantees. In 2022–2023, the Union Finance Ministry tightened `Article 293(3)` enforcement by **deducting State-enterprise OBBs and net Public Account liabilities directly from States' annual Net Borrowing Ceilings**.

In early 2024, Kerala filed an **`Article 131` Original Suit (`Original Suit No. 1 of 2024`)** challenging the Union's borrowing cap letters and the 2018 FRBM amendment (setting a combined Centre–State debt target of 60% of GDP) as unconstitutional encroachments on State fiscal autonomy. On **1 April 2024** (***State of Kerala v. Union of India***, `2024 INSC 253`), Justices Surya Kant and K.V. Viswanathan **declined Kerala's plea for interim additional borrowing**—observing that fiscal indiscipline in one State affects national macroeconomic stability and sovereign credit—but noted that **`Article 293` had never once received an authoritative interpretation by the Supreme Court**, formally referring the constitutional questions (whether fiscal decentralisation is an aspect of federalism, the scope of `Article 293(3)–(4)` conditions, and the RBI's role as State debt manager) to a **five-judge Constitution Bench** (pending adjudication as of late 2026).

```compare
# Borrowing Powers: Union (Article 292) vs States (Article 293)
Union Government (Article 292) | State Governments (Article 293)
Geographical Reach :: Can borrow BOTH within India AND outside India (sovereign/bilateral/multilateral) | Can borrow ONLY within the territory of India — NO direct foreign borrowing
Security Offered :: Security of the Consolidated Fund of India | Security of the Consolidated Fund of the State
Statutory Ceiling :: Fixed by Parliament (FRBM Act, 2003) | Fixed by State Legislature (State FRBM Acts)
Inter-Governmental Consent :: Requires NO consent from States | Requires PRIOR Union consent under Art. 293(3) if ANY Union loan or guarantee is outstanding
Conditionalities :: Self-governed by Parliamentary law & macro-fiscal policy | Union may attach binding conditions under Art. 293(4) (Net Borrowing Ceilings, OBB deductions)
```

### Inter-Governmental Tax Immunities (Articles 285 & 289) and Effects of Emergencies

To prevent one tier of government from crippling the other through predatory taxation—the classical principle of **"immunity of instrumentalities"** pioneered in the US case *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819)—the Constitution codifies explicit, yet noticeably asymmetric, **inter-governmental tax immunities** under `Articles 285` and `289`:

- **Exemption of Union Property from State Taxation (`Article 285`):** All **property of the Union** is completely exempt from **all taxes imposed by a State or any authority within a State** (such as municipalities, district boards, or panchayats), save insofar as Parliament by law otherwise provides. Crucially, the word *"property"* is comprehensive—covering movable and immovable assets whether used for **sovereign OR commercial** purposes (e.g., Railways). However, a vital **Prelims exception** applies: **corporations and statutory companies** created by the Central Government (such as SAIL, ONGC, LIC, FCI, or Coal India) are separate legal entities distinct from the Union—their property is **NOT immune** from State or municipal taxation!
- **Exemption of State Property and Income from Union Taxation (`Article 289`):** State immunity is significantly narrower than Union immunity, governed by three rules:
  1. **Direct Tax Shield Only (`Article 289(1)`):** The property and income of a State are exempt from Union **direct taxation** (such as income tax). In the celebrated nine-judge advisory opinion ***In re Sea Customs Act* (1963)**, the Supreme Court held that `Article 289(1)` does **NOT** exempt States from **indirect taxes**—the Union can validly levy **customs duties** on goods imported/exported by a State or **central excise** on goods manufactured by a State (and today GST applies to taxable supplies by State bodies).
  2. **Commercial Ventures Taxable (`Article 289(2)`):** Parliament is expressly empowered to tax **any trade or business of any kind carried on by or on behalf of a State Government**, or any property/income connected with such commercial operations—**unless** Parliament by law declares a particular trade or business to be **incidental to the ordinary functions of government** (`Article 289(3)`).
  3. **Local Bodies Excluded:** Property and income of **local authorities** situated within a State (municipal corporations, panchayats) enjoy **NO immunity** from Union taxation under `Article 289`—and State-owned separate companies/corporations are likewise fully taxable by the Centre.

```compare
# Inter-Governmental Tax Immunities: Article 285 vs Article 289
Union Immunity from State Taxes (Article 285) | State Immunity from Union Taxes (Article 289)
What Is Protected :: All property of the Union | Property AND income of a State
Commercial Activities :: Union commercial & sovereign property BOTH immune (unless Parliament permits) | State trade/business is TAXABLE by Parliament (Art. 289(2)), unless declared incidental to ordinary governance (Art. 289(3))
Direct vs Indirect Taxes :: Exempt from all State & local taxes on property | Exempt ONLY from Union direct taxes; NOT exempt from indirect taxes like customs/excise (In re Sea Customs Act, 1963)
Local Authorities :: State local bodies (municipalities/panchayats) CANNOT tax Union property | Property/income of State local authorities are NOT immune from Union taxes
Government Companies / PSUs :: Central PSUs & statutory corporations are separate legal entities — NOT immune | State PSUs & statutory corporations are separate legal entities — NOT immune
```

#### Effects of Emergencies on Centre–State Financial Relations (`Articles 354` & `360`)

- **During a National Emergency (`Article 354`):** While a Proclamation of National Emergency (`Article 352`) operates, the President may by order **modify the constitutional distribution of revenues between the Union and the States (`Articles 268–279`)**—reducing or even suspending tax devolution and statutory grants-in-aid to States so resources can be marshalled for national defence. Every such Presidential order cannot extend beyond the **end of the financial year** in which the Emergency ceases to operate, and must be **laid before each House of Parliament** as soon as possible.
- **During a Financial Emergency (`Article 360`):** The Centre gains sweeping financial superintendence over States: the Union may direct any State to (i) observe specified **canons of financial propriety**; (ii) **reduce the salaries and allowances** of all or any class of persons serving in connection with the affairs of the State (the President may similarly cut Union salaries, **explicitly including Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts!**); and (iii) **reserve all Money Bills and other Financial Bills** for Presidential consideration after passage by the State Legislature. (India has never proclaimed a Financial Emergency—even during the 1991 Balance of Payments crisis.)

## 4. The Finance Commission (Articles 280 & 281)

Because India's constitutional allocation of taxing powers and spending duties inherently produces both vertical and horizontal fiscal imbalances, the framers refused to freeze intergovernmental transfer shares rigidly in constitutional text—recognising that economic demographics, tax buoyancies, and developmental priorities shift every decade. Instead, they created an independent, non-partisan, periodic constitutional arbiter under **`Article 280`**: the **Finance Commission of India**, widely hailed as the **"balancing wheel of fiscal federalism."** Constituted every five years by the President, the Finance Commission replaces political horse-trading with formula-driven, rule-based transfers from the Union to the States and onward to India's third tier of local self-government.

### Constitutional Mandate, Composition and Statutory Qualifications

Under **`Article 280(1)`**, the **President of India** must constitute a Finance Commission within two years from the commencement of the Constitution (the **First Finance Commission**, chaired by **K.C. Neogy**, was appointed on **22 November 1951**) and thereafter at the expiration of **every fifth year**, or at such **earlier time** as the President considers necessary. Sixteen Finance Commissions have been constituted and have submitted reports to date—most recently the **16th Finance Commission (2026–31)** chaired by **Dr. Arvind Panagariya**.

#### Composition, Tenure & Reappointment

The Commission consists of a **Chairman and four other members**, all appointed by the **President**. They hold office for the period specified by the President in his order (ordinarily until submission of the report) and are **eligible for reappointment** (for instance, Shri Ajay Narayan Jha served as a full-time member on the 15th Finance Commission and was initially reappointed to the 16th Commission). Members may serve on either a full-time or part-time basis depending on the Presidential order.

#### Statutory Qualifications — Set by Parliament, NOT the Constitution (`Article 280(2)`)

One of the highest-frequency UPSC Prelims traps lies in **`Article 280(2)`**: the Constitution **does NOT itself lay down the qualifications** of the Chairman or members of the Finance Commission! Instead, `Article 280(2)` empowers **Parliament by law** to determine their qualifications and the manner in which they shall be selected. Exercising this mandate, Parliament enacted the **Finance Commission (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1951** (amended in 1955), which prescribes:

- **Chairman:** Must be a person having **experience in public affairs**.
- **Four Other Members:** Must be selected from among persons who satisfy one of four specialised profiles:
  1. **Judicial Qualification:** Are, or have been, or are qualified to be appointed as a **Judge of a High Court**;
  2. **Government Finance & Accounts:** Have **specialised knowledge of the finance and accounts of the Government**;
  3. **Financial Administration:** Have had **wide experience in financial matters and in administration**;
  4. **Economics:** Have **special knowledge of economics**.

The 1951 Act further specifies **four grounds of disqualification**: being of unsound mind, being an undischarged insolvent, having been convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude, or having such financial or other interest as is likely to affect prejudicially one's functions as a member.

#### Quasi-Judicial Status

The Finance Commission is explicitly a **quasi-judicial constitutional body**. Under the 1951 Act, it determines its own procedure and exercises all the powers of a **civil court** under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908—including summoning and enforcing the attendance of witnesses, requiring the production of any document or public record from any court or office, and receiving evidence on affidavits—making it legally deemed a civil court for specified proceedings.

### Constitutional Functions (Article 280(3)) and Parliamentary Accountability (Article 281)

Under **`Article 280(3)`**, it is the constitutional duty of the Finance Commission to make recommendations to the President across **four core mandates** (the third having been inserted by the `73rd` and `74th Amendments, 1992` to integrate India's local bodies into the fiscal-federal chain):

1. **Tax Devolution — Vertical & Horizontal (`Article 280(3)(a)`):** The distribution between the Union and the States of the **net proceeds of taxes** (ascertained and certified by the CAG under `Article 279`) which are to be, or may be, divided between them under Chapter I of Part XII (`Article 270` Divisible Pool — **vertical devolution**), and the allocation between the States of the respective shares of such proceeds (**horizontal devolution**).
2. **Statutory Grants-in-Aid (`Article 280(3)(b)`):** The principles which should govern the **grants-in-aid of the revenues of the States** out of the Consolidated Fund of India under **`Article 275`**.
3. **Augmenting Resources for Panchayats and Municipalities (`Article 280(3)(bb)` & `(c)`):** The measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of **Panchayats** (`clause (bb)`, inserted by **`73rd Amendment, 1992`**) and **Municipalities** (`clause (c)`, inserted by **`74th Amendment, 1992`**) in the State, **on the basis of the recommendations made by the Finance Commission of the State (SFC)** constituted under `Articles 243-I` and `243-Y`.
4. **Any Other Matter for Sound Finance (`Article 280(3)(d)`):** Any other matter referred to the Commission by the President in the interests of **sound finance**. Through this elastic clause in the Presidential Terms of Reference (ToR), recent Finance Commissions have been tasked with charting FRBM debt-consolidation roadmaps, reviewing Disaster Management Act (2005) funding, evaluating power-sector DISCOM reforms, examining subsidy rationalisation, and assessing defence/internal security financing.

#### Advisory Nature vs Federal Convention & `Article 281` Accountability

Crucially, the recommendations made by the Finance Commission are strictly **advisory in nature—NOT legally binding** on the Union Government. As **Dr. P.V. Rajamannar** (Chairman of the 4th Finance Commission) famously observed, nothing in the Constitution compels the Government of India to accept the Commission's award, nor does a State acquire a justiciable legal right to demand grants recommended by it. In practice, however, an exceptionally strong **constitutional convention** governs Indian fiscal federalism: the Union Government almost invariably accepts the Commission's core recommendations on vertical and horizontal tax devolution and main statutory grants, declining occasionally only secondary sector-specific or state-specific recommendations (as seen with parts of the 15th FC's sector grants).

To guarantee democratic scrutiny of what the Union accepts or declines, **`Article 281`** mandates that the **President shall cause every recommendation** made by the Finance Commission under the Constitution, **together with an Explanatory Memorandum as to the action taken thereon**, to be **laid before EACH House of Parliament**.

> [!prelims]
> **Finance Commission UPSC Prelims Trap Checklist (`Arts. 280–281`):**
> 1. **Who fixes qualifications?** **Parliament** (via the *Finance Commission [Miscellaneous Provisions] Act, 1951*)—**NOT** the Constitution and **NOT** the President!
> 2. **Reappointment:** Both the Chairman and members are **eligible for reappointment**.
> 3. **Legal Force:** Recommendations are **advisory, not binding** (UPSC tested this directly in Prelims!).
> 4. **Parliamentary Tabling (`Art. 281`):** The **President** causes the report + **Explanatory Memorandum on action taken** to be laid before **both Houses of Parliament** (not just Lok Sabha, and not directly by the Commission Chairman).
> 5. **Local Body Mandate:** Clauses `(bb)` (Panchayats) and `(c)` (Municipalities) were added by the **`73rd` & `74th Amendments (1992)`** and operate *"on the basis of the recommendations made by the State Finance Commission"*.

### Evolution Across Finance Commissions (1st to 16th): From Planning Overlap to 42% Devolution

For more than six decades (`1950–2014`), the constitutional primacy of the Finance Commission was significantly diluted by the parallel rise of an **extra-constitutional, non-statutory body**: the **Planning Commission**. An awkward **"dual-channel transfer regime"** emerged: the Finance Commission assessed only *Non-Plan* revenue requirements (under `Articles 270` and `275`), while the Planning Commission channelled vast discretionary *Plan* grants under `Article 282` (using the Gadgil–Mukherjee formula and centrally approved Five-Year Plans). Dr. P.V. Rajamannar (4th FC) lamented that this overlap narrowed the Finance Commission's intended constitutional role, while the Sarkaria Commission urged closer coordination.

The landscape transformed decisively during **2014–2016** through two historic milestones: first, the **abolition of the Planning Commission** and creation of **NITI Aayog** (`1 January 2015`), which possesses **no fund-allocating power**—restoring the Finance Commission as India's primary constitutional arbiter of federal transfers; and second, the **14th Finance Commission (Dr. Y.V. Reddy, 2015–20)**, which executed an unprecedented **10-percentage-point jump in vertical devolution** from **32% (13th FC) to 42%**, abolished the Plan vs Non-Plan dichotomy, ended separate Special Category State formula transfers, and dramatically increased States' untied fiscal autonomy.

### The 15th Finance Commission (N.K. Singh, 2020–21 & 2021–26)

Constituted on **27 November 2017** under Chairman **Shri N.K. Singh** (with members Ajay Narayan Jha [succeeding Shaktikanta Das], Prof. Anoop Singh, Dr. Ashok Lahiri, and Dr. Ramesh Chand), the **15th Finance Commission** uniquely submitted **two reports**: an interim report for **2020–21** and its main five-year report—titled ***"Finance Commission in COVID Times"***—covering **2021–26** (tabled 1 February 2021):

- **Vertical Devolution (`41%`):** Set States' share in the Divisible Pool at **41 per cent**—effectively preserving the 14th FC's 42% benchmark, with the **1 percentage point adjustment** earmarked solely to enable the Union to fund the newly reorganised Union Territories of **Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh** (carved out in October 2019).
- **Horizontal Devolution (`2021–26` — Six Criteria):** Facing sharp controversy over its Terms of Reference mandating exclusive use of the **2011 Census** (instead of the 1971 Census used since the 8th FC), the 15th FC introduced a **12.5% Demographic Performance** criterion (based on the **inverse of Total Fertility Rate**) to reward southern and smaller states that had successfully stabilised population growth, while retaining **Income Distance (45%)**, **Population 2011 (15%)**, **Area (15%)**, **Forest and Ecology (10%)**, and reintroducing **Tax and Fiscal Efforts (2.5%)**.
- **Grants-in-Aid (`₹10.33 lakh crore` across Five Windows):** Recommended (i) **Post-Devolution Revenue Deficit Grants (RDGs)** of **₹2,94,514 crore** to 17 States; (ii) **Local Body Grants** of **₹4,36,361 crore** (including **₹70,051 crore** health grants routed via local bodies and million-plus city air-quality/solid-waste grants, tied to audited accounts and property-tax floor rates); (iii) **Disaster Management Grants** of **₹1,22,601 crore** (Union share of SDRF/SDMF, operationalising Mitigation Funds under DMA 2005); (iv) **Sector-Specific Grants** (`₹1,29,987 crore` — notably including **₹45,000 crore** performance-based incentive for **agricultural reforms** and **₹4,800 crore** for **educational outcomes**, both directly tested in **UPSC Prelims 2025**!); and (v) **State-Specific Grants** (`₹49,599 crore`). Notably, the Union Government accepted tax devolution, RDGs, local body, and disaster grants, while declining or giving only "in-principle" consideration to most sector-specific and state-specific grants—and noted for examination the proposed non-lapsable **Modernisation Fund for Defence and Internal Security (MFDIS)**.

### The 16th Finance Commission (Dr. Arvind Panagariya, 2026–31)

Constituted by Presidential Notification `S.O. 5533(E)` on **31 December 2023** under Chairman **Dr. Arvind Panagariya** (former Vice-Chairman, NITI Aayog, and Professor of Economics at Columbia University), the **16th Finance Commission** marks the most consequential rebalancing of Indian fiscal federalism since 2015. At the submission of its report on **17 November 2025** (following a one-month extension), the Commission comprised full-time members **Smt. Annie George Mathew** and **Dr. Manoj Panda**, part-time members **Shri T. Rabi Sankar** (Deputy Governor, RBI — appointed June 2025 following the resignation of Shri Ajay Narayan Jha) and **Dr. Soumya Kanti Ghosh** (Group Chief Economic Adviser, SBI), with **Shri Ritvik Ranjanam Pandey** as Secretary. On **1 February 2026**, the Union Finance Minister tabled the **16th Finance Commission Report** alongside the **Explanatory Memorandum (Action Taken Report)** in Parliament under `Article 281` for the five-year award period **1 April 2026 to 31 March 2031**.

#### 1. Vertical Devolution (`41%` Retained) & the 'Grand Bargain' on Cesses

Despite **18 States** petitioning for a sharp increase in vertical devolution up to **50 per cent** to offset the shrinking Divisible Pool, the 16th FC **retained States' vertical share at `41 per cent`**, pointing to the Union's heavy sovereign commitments toward defence modernisation, national capital infrastructure, and macroeconomic stability. Crucially, the Commission addressed the festering cess-and-surcharge grievance with constitutional candour: noting that a Finance Commission lacks constitutional jurisdiction under `Article 280` to cap `Article 271` surcharges or forcibly merge cesses into the Divisible Pool, it instead proposed a political **"Grand Bargain"**—the Union folds most cesses and surcharges into ordinary shareable taxes, while States accept a modestly lower percentage share of the resulting much larger pool. On 1 February 2026, the Union Government formally **accepted the 41 per cent vertical share** and accepted the Commission's landmark transparency recommendation to **disclose CAG-certified `Article 279` net tax proceeds annually**.

#### 2. Horizontal Devolution Overhaul — Blending Equity with Growth Efficiency

To mediate the sharpening North–South clash between **equity** (equalising poorer populous states) and **efficiency** (rewarding economically dynamic states contributing heavily to national tax revenue), the 16th FC redesigned the horizontal formula around **six parameters**:

- **Introduction of `10%` "Contribution to GDP" (New Efficiency Metric):** Discontinuing the small 2.5% Tax and Fiscal Efforts criterion, the Commission introduced a groundbreaking **`10%` weight for a State's Contribution to GDP**—computed via the **square root of each State's share in national GSDP** (`√GSDP share`, averaged over 2018–19 to 2023–24 excluding COVID-hit 2020–21). Taking the square root ingeniously rewards economic scale and productivity while preventing giant states (Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Gujarat) from overwhelming smaller high-performing states.
- **Calibrated Equity & Need Adjustments:** **Income Distance** remains the largest criterion—trimmed modestly from **45.0% to 42.5%** (measured against the average per capita GSDP of the **top three large states**). **Population (2011 Census)** rose from **15.0% to 17.5%**; **Demographic Performance** adjusted from **12.5% to 10.0%** while shifting its metric from inverse TFR to **slower 1971–2011 population growth rate**; **Area** dropped from **15.0% to 10.0%** (with the minimum state floor cut from 2% to 1.5%); and **Forest** held steady at **10.0%** while broadening coverage to include **open forests** alongside a forest-gain bonus.
- **State-Wise Redistribution Impact:** Accepted in full by the Union Government, the new formula keeps **Uttar Pradesh** as the top recipient (**17.619%**), followed by **Bihar (9.948%)**, and **Sikkim** as the smallest (**0.335%**). Overall, **14 States**—predominantly higher-income, industrialised, and southern states (for instance, **Karnataka** rising notably from 3.647% to **4.131%**) **gained** inter-se share, whereas **14 States**—largely lower-income and north-eastern states—experienced reduced shares.

#### 3. Sweeping Overhaul of Article 275 Grants-in-Aid (`₹9,47,409 crore`)

The single most dramatic policy break of the 16th Finance Commission lies in its radical streamlining of **`Article 275` Grants-in-Aid** to **`₹9,47,409 crore` (₹9.47 lakh crore)** across 2026–31—more than halving grants' share in total Finance Commission transfers (from ~19.4% under the 15th FC to ~8.3%):

- **Total Discontinuation of RDGs, Sector-Specific & State-Specific Grants:** Breaking decades of precedent, the 16th FC **completely discontinued Post-Devolution Revenue Deficit Grants (RDGs)** (which had amounted to ₹2.95 lakh crore for 17 states under the 15th FC), arguing that gap-filling RDGs create moral hazard by rewarding weak own-tax mobilisation and unchecked revenue expenditure. It likewise dropped **sector-specific** and **state-specific grants**, restricting `Article 275` grants strictly to two constitutional-priority heads:
- **Local Body Grants (`₹7,91,493 crore` — Massive Third-Tier Boost):** Nearly **83.5%** of all 16th FC grants go directly to rural and urban local governments, split **60:40** across base components:
  - **Rural Local Bodies (RLBs — `₹4,35,236 crore`):** Divided **80:20** into Basic Grants (`₹3,48,188 crore`) and Performance Grants (`₹87,048 crore`); distributed among States on a **90:10 ratio of projected rural population (2026) and area**.
  - **Urban Local Bodies (ULBs — `₹3,56,257 crore`):** Comprising Basic Grants (`₹2,32,125 crore`), Performance Grants (`₹58,032 crore`), a dedicated **Special Infrastructure Component of `₹56,100 crore`** (for wastewater management in cities of 10–40 lakh population), and a **`₹10,000 crore` Urbanisation Premium**; inter-se allocation uses a **90:10 ratio of projected urban population (2026) and Own Source Revenue (OSR)**.
  - **Tied/Untied Split & Three Entry Conditions:** Half (`50%`) of Basic Grants are **untied**, while `50%` are **tied** to sanitation, solid waste management, and drinking water. Crucially, releasing *any* local body grant requires fulfilling **three strict entry conditions**: (i) duly constituted constitutional local bodies; (ii) public disclosure of provisional and audited local-body accounts; and (iii) timely constitution of the **State Finance Commission (SFC)**!
- **Disaster Management Grants (`₹1,55,916 crore` Union Share):** Out of a total five-year **₹2,04,401 crore** State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF) and Mitigation Fund (SDMF) corpus, retaining the established **90:10** Centre–State sharing pattern for North-Eastern and Himalayan States and **75:25** for all other States.

#### 4. Macro-Fiscal Consolidation Roadmap & Structural Reform Prescriptions

Invoking `Article 280(3)(d)`, the 16th FC laid down a rigorous fiscal-stability roadmap for 2026–31:
- **Deficit & Debt Path:** Bring the **Union fiscal deficit down to `3.5% of GDP` by 2030–31**; maintain **States' annual fiscal deficit ceiling at `3.0% of GSDP`**; and steer combined Centre–State general government debt downward from **`77.3%` of GDP (2026–27) to `73.1%` of GDP (2030–31)**.
- **Strict Ban on Off-Budget Borrowings:** Discontinue all State and Union **off-budget borrowings** and adopt uniform expanded definitions of fiscal deficit and debt capturing every off-budget liability.
- **Power-Sector DISCOM Privatisation:** Urged States to actively privatise loss-making electricity **DISCOMs**, creating **Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs)** to warehouse legacy debt so private operators start with clean balance sheets (permitting use of the Union's Special Assistance Scheme for Capital Investment post-privatisation).
- **Rationalising Unconditional Cash Transfers ("Freebies"):** Flagging that **unconditional cash transfers** surged from just **3% of total subsidy spending in 2018–19 to `20.2%`** (accelerated by JAM-trinity digital delivery), the Commission urged States to impose **sunset clauses**, enforce clear **exclusion criteria**, adopt uniform subsidy accounting, and strictly prohibit financing subsidies through off-budget debt.

## 5. The GST Council & Modern Fiscal Federalism (101st Amendment, Article 279A)

If the Finance Commission represents India's classic constitutional arbiter of *revenue distribution*, the **Goods and Services Tax (GST) Council** represents independent India's boldest institutional experiment in **cooperative fiscal federalism and "pooled sovereignty."** Before 2017, India's indirect-tax map resembled a fragmented customs union: the Union taxed manufacturing (Central Excise) and services (Service Tax), while States separately taxed sales (VAT), inter-state movement (CST), octroi, entry taxes, luxury taxes, and entertainment taxes—generating severe **"tax on tax" cascading**, state border check-posts, and massive compliance friction across a single domestic market.

First conceptualised by the **Kelkar Task Force on FRBM Implementation (2004)** and refined over a decade by the **Empowered Committee of State Finance Ministers** (chaired notably by Asim Dasgupta), the GST reform culminated in the landmark **`101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016`** (passed as the 122nd Constitutional Amendment Bill, ratified by more than half the State Legislatures under `Article 368(2)`, and granted Presidential assent on **8 September 2016**). Following enactment of the CGST, IGST, UTGST, Compensation, and State GST statutes, GST came into force nationwide on **1 July 2017**—transforming India's indirect tax regime from an origin-based, cascading silo model into a unified, **destination-based value-added consumption tax**.

### Constitutional Architecture of Dual GST

Because India is a federal republic where neither the Centre nor the States could surrender indirect taxation wholly to the other, India adopted a **Dual GST model** (broadly akin to Canada and Brazil)—under which **both** the Union and the States simultaneously levy tax on a common base of goods and services pursuant to **`Article 246A`**.

#### Four Pillars & Subsumed Taxes

- **Four Statutory Components:**
  1. **CGST (Central GST):** Levied and collected by the Union on **intra-state** supplies of goods and services (proceeds enter the `Article 270` Divisible Pool).
  2. **SGST (State GST):** Levied and collected by each **State** (and UTs with legislatures: Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu & Kashmir) on **intra-state** supplies, accruing exclusively to that State.
  3. **UTGST (Union Territory GST):** Levied in Union Territories without legislatures (Andaman & Nicobar, Chandigarh, DNH&DD, Lakshadweep, Ladakh).
  4. **IGST (Integrated GST — `Article 269A`):** Levied and collected exclusively by the **Union** on all **inter-state supplies** and **imports** into India (treated as inter-state supply under Explanation to `Art. 269A(1)`), and apportioned between the Union and the **destination (consuming) State**—ensuring seamless cross-border Input Tax Credit (ITC) without check-posts!
- **17 Taxes and 13 Cesses Subsumed:** GST absorbed **Central taxes** (Central Excise Duty, Additional Excise Duties, Excise Duty under Medicinal & Toilet Preparations Act, Service Tax, Additional Customs Duty [CVD], Special Additional Duty [SAD], and Central surcharges/cesses on goods/services) alongside **State taxes** (State VAT/Sales Tax, Central Sales Tax, Purchase Tax, Luxury Tax, Entry Tax/Octroi, Entertainment Tax *except* those levied by local bodies, Taxes on Advertisements, and Taxes on Lotteries, Betting, and Gambling).

#### Three Crucial Constitutional Exclusions & Exceptions (High-Yield Prelims Gold)

Aspirants frequently blur items that are **constitutionally outside GST** versus items merely **deferred** inside GST:

1. **Alcoholic Liquor for Human Consumption (Constitutionally Excluded):** Expressly excluded from the very definition of "Goods and Services Tax" under **`Article 366(12A)`** (*"any tax on supply of goods, or services or both except taxes on the supply of the alcoholic liquor for human consumption"*). Bringing potable liquor under GST is legally **impossible by GST Council resolution alone**—it requires a formal **`Article 368` Constitutional Amendment** ratified by half the States!
2. **Five Petroleum Products (Inside GST, Implementation Deferred — `Article 279A(5)`):** **Petroleum crude, high-speed diesel, motor spirit (petrol), natural gas, and aviation turbine fuel (ATF)** are constitutionally *included* in GST, but `Article 279A(5)` stipulates that GST shall apply to them **from such date as may be recommended by the GST Council**. Thus, **NO constitutional amendment is needed**—a consensus decision of the GST Council suffices (though States resist inclusion because petrol, diesel, and alcohol remain their last major autonomous own-tax revenue anchors!).
3. **Tobacco & Tobacco Products (Subject to BOTH GST AND Central Excise):** Tobacco remains taxable under **GST** *and* simultaneously continues to attract **Central Excise Duty** levied by the Union (and similarly, basic customs duty on imports, stamp duty on real estate, electricity duty, and local-body entertainment tax under List II `Entry 62` remain outside GST).

> [!example]
> **How Destination-Based Dual GST Eliminates Cascading ("Tax on Tax"):**
> Suppose a factory in **Maharashtra** manufactures machine components worth ₹1,000 and sells them inter-state to a distributor in **Karnataka** at 18% **IGST** (₹180). The Karnataka distributor adds ₹500 value and sells to a Bengaluru consumer for ₹1,500, charging 9% **CGST** (₹135) + 9% **SGST** (₹135) = ₹270. Rather than taxing the full ₹1,500 afresh, the distributor claims **Input Tax Credit (ITC)** of ₹180 paid on inputs and remits only the net ₹90. Behind the scenes, the GSTN clearinghouse transfers the SGST share of IGST directly from Maharashtra/Centre to **Karnataka**—the **destination State** where final consumption occurred—eliminating tax-on-tax cascading and border check-posts in one stroke.

### The GST Council (Article 279A): Composition, Quorum, Weighted Voting and Mandate

Inserted by the `101st Amendment`, **`Article 279A`** mandated the **President** to constitute a joint constitutional forum—the **Goods and Services Tax Council**—within 60 days of the Amendment's commencement (formally constituted in **September 2016**). Uniquely, `Article 279A` is entrenched inside the federal ratification proviso of **`Article 368(2)`**: Parliament **cannot** unilaterally amend `Article 279A` without a special majority **plus ratification by at least half the State Legislatures**.

#### Composition (33 Members) & Institutional Secretariat (`Article 279A(2)–(3)`)

The GST Council brings Union and State fiscal leadership around a single table:
- **Chairperson:** The **Union Finance Minister** (`ex-officio`).
- **Union Member:** The **Union Minister of State** in charge of Revenue or Finance.
- **State & UT Members (`31`):** The Minister in charge of Finance or Taxation—or any other Minister nominated by each State Government. Because **`Article 366(26B)`** defines "State" for GST purposes to include a **Union Territory with a Legislature**, all **28 States + 3 UTs with Legislatures (Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu & Kashmir)** sit as full voting members—bringing total Council strength to **`33` members (`2` Union + `31` State/UT)**.
- **Vice-Chairperson (`Article 279A(3)`):** The State/UT Ministers choose **one amongst themselves** to serve as Vice-Chairperson of the Council for such period as they may decide.
- **Secretariat & Non-Voting Invitee:** The **Union Revenue Secretary** serves as `ex-officio` Secretary to the Council, while the **Chairperson of the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC)** is a permanent **non-voting invitee** to all Council proceedings.

#### Quorum, Weighted Voting & the Mutual Veto Architecture (`Article 279A(7)–(10)`)

To prevent either central dictation or chaotic majoritarianism by a handful of States, the framers engineered a remarkable **mutual-veto weighted voting formula**:

```flow
# GST Council Decision-Making & Mutual Veto Mechanics (Article 279A)
Quorum Check (Art. 279A(7)) | At least 50% (1/2) of total members (17 of 33) must be present to transact business
Weighted Vote Pool (Art. 279A(9)) | Central Government = 1/3rd (33.33%) weight || All Voting States/UTs Combined = 2/3rd (66.67%) weight (equal per State!)
Passing Threshold | Requires ≥ 3/4th (75%) of weighted votes of members PRESENT AND VOTING
Federal Mutual Veto | States alone (max 66.67%) can NEVER outvote Centre → Centre holds absolute veto || Centre alone (33.33%) needs >62.5% of voting States (~20 of 31) → States hold collective veto
Living Convention | Decisions are taken almost universally by CONSENSUS (only 1 formal vote since 2017 — Dec 2019 on lottery)
```

| Constitutional Parameter (`Art. 279A`) | Exact Threshold / Provision | Operational Federal Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Quorum (`cl. 7`)** | **One-half (`50%`)** of total members (`17` of `33`) | Ensures broad Union–State attendance before any binding recommendation is framed |
| **Vote Weightage (`cl. 9`)** | **Union = `1/3rd` (`33.33%`)**; **All States/UTs combined = `2/3rd` (`66.67%`)** | Every State/UT carries **equal individual weight** regardless of population or GSDP (Sikkim = Uttar Pradesh!) |
| **Decision Majority (`cl. 9`)** | **Not less than `3/4th` (`75%`)** of weighted votes of members **present and voting** | **Mutual Veto:** Neither the Union (33.33%) nor all States united (66.67%) can pass a proposal unilaterally |
| **Vacancy / Defect Protection (`cl. 10`)** | No act or proceeding invalid merely due to vacancy, appointment defect, or procedural irregularity | Insulates GST rate and law changes from technical litigation |
| **Guiding Mandate (`cl. 6`)** | Strive for a **harmonised structure of GST** and development of a **harmonised national market** | Anchors cooperative balance between revenue protection and ease of doing business |

#### Functions & Mandate (`Article 279A(4), (5), (11)`)

The Council makes recommendations to the Union and the States on:
1. Taxes, cesses, and surcharges levied by Union, States, and local bodies to be **subsumed** into GST;
2. Goods and services that may be **subjected to or exempted** from GST;
3. **Model GST laws**, principles of levy, **apportionment of IGST** under `Article 269A`, and principles governing **place of supply**;
4. **Turnover threshold** below which goods and services may be exempted from GST;
5. **Rates** including floor rates with bands of GST;
6. Any **special rate or rates for a specified period** to raise additional resources during any **natural calamity or disaster**;
7. **Special provisions** with respect to **eleven special-category/hilly States**: **Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Jammu & Kashmir, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, Tripura, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand**;
8. The date from which GST shall be levied on the **five petroleum products** (`Art. 279A(5)`);
9. **Dispute Resolution Mechanism (`Article 279A(11)`):** The Council is mandated to establish a mechanism to adjudicate any dispute arising out of its recommendations (a) between the Government of India and one or more States; (b) between the Government of India and any State(s) on one side and one or more States on the other; or (c) between two or more States. (Notably, despite a decade of GST operation, this standing constitutional inter-governmental dispute mechanism has **still not been formally operationalised**!)

### Union of India v. Mohit Minerals (2022): Persuasive Value and Uncooperative Federalism

For the first five years of GST, a profound constitutional question hovered unanswered: *Are the recommendations of the GST Council legally binding on Parliament and State Legislatures, or do legislatures retain autonomous power to depart from them?* On **19 May 2022**, a three-judge Supreme Court Bench (**Justices D.Y. Chandrachud, Surya Kant, and Vikram Nath**) delivered a landmark verdict in ***Union of India v. M/s Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd.*** (`2022 INSC 596`) while striking down Union notifications imposing IGST under reverse charge on ocean freight in Cost-Insurance-Freight (CIF) import contracts.

> "The 'recommendations' of the GST Council are the product of a collaborative dialogue involving the Union and States. They are recommendatory in nature. To regard them as binding edicts would disrupt fiscal federalism, where both the Union and the States are conferred equal power to legislate on GST... Indian federalism is a dialogue between cooperative and uncooperative federalism where the federal units are at liberty to use different means of persuasion ranging from collaboration to contestation." — Justice D.Y. Chandrachud, *Union of India v. M/s Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd.*, 19 May 2022

The Court rested its historic holding on **five crisp constitutional pillars**:

1. **Simultaneous, Equal Legislative Power (`Article 246A`):** `Article 246A` confers simultaneous, co-equal legislative authority over GST on Parliament and every State Legislature, deliberately omitting any repugnancy clause (unlike List III's `Article 254`).
2. **Textual Architecture & Deletion of Draft `Article 279B`:** Neither does `Article 279A` begin with a *non-obstante* clause overriding `Article 246A`, nor is `Article 246A` made "subject to" `Article 279A`. Crucially, while drafting the `101st Amendment`, Parliament consciously **deleted draft `Article 279B`** (which had proposed a binding GST Dispute Settlement Authority) and replaced it with the advisory `Article 279A(11)`—proving legislative intent that Council advice carries **persuasive value**, not coercive force.
3. **Crucial Distinction — Primary Legislation vs Executive Subordinate Rules:** While Council recommendations are **NOT binding on Parliament or State Legislatures** enacting primary GST laws, the Court carefully clarified that when the **Central or State executive** exercises **delegated rule-making or notification power** under specific sections of the CGST/IGST/SGST Acts that statutorily condition executive action *"on the recommendations of the Council"*, those **subordinate notifications remain bound by statutory Council recommendations**!
4. **Safeguarding Democratic Federalism:** Because the Union holds a one-third unilateral veto in the Council, treating recommendations as legally binding edicts would effectively strip State Legislatures of fiscal sovereignty. Recognising **"uncooperative federalism"**—the legitimate right of States to contest, bargain, and dissent—actually strengthens genuine consensus.
5. **Merits Holding (Composite Supply):** Ocean freight in a CIF import contract forms an integral part of a **composite supply** of imported goods already taxed under IGST at the customs border; levying a separate reverse-charge IGST on ocean freight constituted unconstitutional double taxation violating `Section 8` of the CGST Act.

> [!case]
> ***Union of India v. M/s Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd.* (19 May 2022 — 3-Judge Bench):**
> - **Core Prelims Takeaway:** GST Council recommendations under `Article 279A` have **persuasive value** and are **NOT strictly binding** on Parliament or State Legislatures (though binding on the executive when issuing subordinate rules where the GST statute mandates Council recommendation).
> - **Practical Reality:** Despite legal non-bindingness, neither the Union nor any State has broken ranks on GST rates since 2022—proving that the shared economic incentive of maintaining India's seamless **Input Tax Credit chain** enforces compliance more powerfully than legal coercion ever could.

### Modern GST Milestones (2022–2026): Compensation Cess Exit, GST 2.0, GSTAT and 57th Council Reforms

Between 2022 and late 2026, India's GST framework passed through its most sweeping structural maturation since 2017—spanning four landmark developments essential for both Prelims and Mains:

- **End of GST Compensation Period (`2022`) & Formal Exit of Compensation Cess (`1 February 2026`):** Under `Section 18` of the `101st Amendment`, the Union guaranteed States compensation for any GST revenue shortfall against a **14 per cent annual compounded growth baseline** (base year 2015–16) for **five years (`1 July 2017 – 30 June 2022`)**, funded through a **GST Compensation Cess** on luxury and sin goods (tobacco, pan masala, aerated drinks, luxury cars, coal). When COVID-19 collapsed cess collections in 2020–21 and 2021–22, the Centre borrowed ~₹2.69 lakh crore as back-to-back loans to pay States and extended the Compensation Cess purely to retire those loans and interest (originally through March 2026). With loan liabilities discharged ahead of schedule, the Union Finance Ministry formally **ended the GST Compensation Cess effective `1 February 2026`** (budgeted at `₹0` in Union Budget 2026–27). Simultaneously, to prevent tax drops on demerit goods, two Parliament statutes came into effect on `1 February 2026` alongside the new 40% GST rate: (i) the **Central Excise (Amendment) Act, 2025**, introducing revised and machine-capacity-based **Central Excise Duties on cigarettes and tobacco products** (revenues from which **enter the `Article 270` Divisible Pool**, restoring fiscal share to States!); and (ii) the **Health Security se National Security Cess Act, 2025**, imposing a capacity-based **earmarked cess on pan masala** (non-shareable, dedicated to public health and national security).
- **56th GST Council Meeting (`3 September 2025`) — "GST 2.0" Rate Rationalisation:** Answering nearly eight years of complaints over inverted duty structures and classification litigation across India's complex four-slab rate grid (`5%, 12%, 18%, 28%`), the 56th Council approved transformative **"Next-Gen GST 2.0"** rate rationalisation (largely effective **`22 September 2025`**):
  1. Collapsed the four-tier system into a streamlined **two-rate grid**: a **`5%` Merit Rate** and an **`18%` Standard Rate**, accompanied by a **`40%` Special Demerit/Luxury Rate** (for mid/large cars & SUVs >1500cc or >4000mm, aerated/carbonated beverages, pan masala, and tobacco products—except bidis at 18%).
  2. **Exempted (`NIL` GST) ALL individual life insurance policies** (term, ULIP, endowment) and **all individual health insurance policies** (including family floater and senior citizen policies) and their reinsurance, alongside **NIL** GST on UHT milk, packaged paneer/chena, and all Indian breads (roti, chapati, paratha), while slashing rates on small cars (`28% → 18%`) and everyday household items (`18%/12% → 5%`).
- **Launch of the GST Appellate Tribunal — GSTAT (`24 September 2025`):** For eight years after 2017, taxpayers aggrieved by first-level departmental appellate orders had no operational second-level GST tribunal and were forced to flood High Courts with writ petitions. On **`24 September 2025`**, the Union Finance Minister formally launched the **Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT)** in New Delhi under its first President, **Justice (Retd.) Sanjaya Kumar Mishra**, establishing a **Principal Bench in New Delhi** and **31 State Benches across 45 locations**—creating the first unified, independent national appellate forum converging Central and State GST adjudications.
- **57th GST Council Meeting (`8 October 2026`) — Decriminalisation & Trust-Based Process Reforms:** Moving from rate simplification to legal/procedural ease of doing business (scheduled effective **`1 April 2027`**), the 57th Council recommended:
  1. **Abolishing pre-prosecution arrest powers** of GST tax officers by **omitting `Section 69`** of the CGST Act, 2017 (retaining criminal prosecution only via judicial process on prima facie fraud/evasion);
  2. **Raising the monetary threshold for criminal prosecution** five-fold from **`₹1 crore` to `₹5 crore`** and removing mandatory minimum prison sentences in favour of judicial discretion;
  3. **Cutting the general penalty** from **`₹25,000` to `₹10,000`** and establishing a **`₹10,000` minimum threshold** for issuing show-cause notices (applying retrospectively to pending small disputes).

> [!news]
> **The 2025–2026 GST Transformation Timeline (Prelims & Mains High-Yield):**
> - **`3 September 2025` (56th GST Council):** Approved **GST 2.0**—collapsed 4 slabs into **2 primary slabs (`5%` merit, `18%` standard) + `40%` demerit/luxury rate**; **exempted all individual life & health insurance** (effective `22 September 2025`).
> - **`24 September 2025`:** Formal launch of **GSTAT** under President **Justice Sanjaya Kumar Mishra** (Principal Bench + 31 State Benches across 45 locations).
> - **`1 February 2026`:** Complete exit of **GST Compensation Cess**; commencement of **Central Excise (Amendment) Act, 2025** (tobacco — shareable) and **Health Security se National Security Cess Act, 2025** (pan masala — earmarked cess).
> - **`8 October 2026` (57th GST Council):** Recommended **scrapping arrest powers (`Section 69` CGST Act)**, raising prosecution threshold (`₹1 cr → ₹5 cr`), and lowering general penalty (`₹25,000 → ₹10,000`) from `1 April 2027`.

### Structural Fiscal Federalism Challenges and Mains Way Forward

Despite institutional achievements like the Finance Commission's 41% devolution and GST 2.0's two-slab architecture, **six interconnected structural fault lines** continue to test India's fiscal federalism across both **GS-2 (Polity)** and **GS-3 (Economy)**:

1. **Chronic Divisible Pool Erosion via Cesses & Surcharges (`Article 271`):** Because non-shareable cesses and surcharges sit outside `Article 270`, the Divisible Pool shrank from ~89% of Union Gross Tax Revenue (13th FC) to **~78%** (15th FC), cutting States' real share to ~32% of GTR. Although the exit of the GST Compensation Cess (`1 Feb 2026`) and revival of shareable tobacco excise improve transparency, new earmarked central levies (such as the Health Security se National Security Cess) ensure the cess-discipline debate persists.
2. **Deepening Vertical Fiscal Imbalance & Post-GST State Revenue Autonomy Loss:** By pooling VAT, CST, and Entry Tax into GST, States surrendered nearly 44% of their autonomous own-tax base while continuing to finance **over 60% of general government expenditure** (health, schools, policing, water). With the 14% guaranteed GST compensation period long ended (`June 2022`), States' sole remaining flexible tax levers—petrol/diesel, alcohol, stamp duties, and mineral levies—face fresh pressure (amplified by Parliament's August 2026 `Section 9D` MMDR curb on *MADA v. SAIL*!).
3. **Sharpening North–South Equity vs Efficiency Divide & RDG Discontinuation:** Replacing the **1971 Census** with the **2011 Census** (15th/16th FCs) alarmed southern demographic-frontrunner States that felt penalised for stabilising population growth and driving GDP. While the 16th FC's new **`10%` Contribution to GDP** criterion partially redressed their grievance (lifting 14 mostly higher-income states), **14 lower-income and north-eastern States lost horizontal share**—and simultaneously lost the safety net of **Post-Devolution Revenue Deficit Grants**, exposing hill and fiscally fragile states to acute revenue strain.
4. **Proliferation & Conditionalities of Centrally Sponsored Schemes (`Article 282`):** Heavy central spending through discretionary `Article 282` CSS with rigid matching ratios (`60:40` / `90:10`), strict central guidelines, and mandatory branding narrows States' budgetary autonomy compared to untied `Article 270` tax devolution—especially when matching contributions crowd out State capital outlays.
5. **Populist Freebies, State Debt Stress & Off-Budget Borrowings (`Article 293`):** Competitive electoral populism—manifested in surging **unconditional cash transfers** (jumping from **3% to `20.2%`** of subsidy spending per the 16th FC), Old Pension Scheme (OPS) revivals, and chronic power **DISCOM losses**—has driven elevated State debt-to-GSDP ratios and tempted States into opaque SPV **Off-Budget Borrowings**. This directly collides with the Union's strict `Article 293(3)` Net Borrowing Ceilings (litigated before the pending 5-judge Constitution Bench in ***State of Kerala v. Union of India***, 2024).
6. **Fiscal Fragility of the Third Tier (Panchayats & Municipalities):** Nearly thirty-five years after the `73rd` and `74th Amendments`, delayed constitution of **State Finance Commissions (`Arts. 243-I & 243-Y`)**, unimplemented SFC recommendations, and dismal municipal **Own Source Revenue** (especially stagnant property taxes) starve urban and rural local bodies of autonomous capacity despite the 16th FC's massive ₹7.91 lakh crore local grant allocation.

#### Balanced Institutional Way Forward

Forging a resilient, twenty-first-century **"Viksit Bharat" fiscal federalism** demands symmetric institutional reforms anchored in recommendations of the **Sarkaria Commission (1988)**, **Punchhi Commission (2010)**, **N.K. Singh FRBM Review Committee (2017)**, and the **15th & 16th Finance Commissions**:

- **Enforce Cess & Surcharge Discipline:** Operationalise the 16th FC's **"Grand Bargain"** by progressively folding permanent cesses/surcharges into standard divisible tax rates, attaching mandatory **statutory sunset clauses** to any new earmarked cess, or introducing a constitutional sharing trigger whenever cesses/surcharges exceed a fixed cap (e.g., 10% of Union GTR)—backed by the newly accepted annual CAG-certified `Article 279` disclosures.
- **Create a Permanent Finance Commission Secretariat & Independent Fiscal Council:** Upgrade the temporary Finance Commission office into a **permanent institutional research cell** (recommended by the 14th & 15th FCs) to maintain continuous Centre–State fiscal databases; simultaneously establish an **independent, non-partisan Fiscal Council** (recommended by the **N.K. Singh FRBM Review Committee, 2017**) reporting to Parliament to evaluate **both Union and State** debt trajectories symmetrically—replacing perceived executive policing under `Article 293(3)` with transparent rule-based oversight.
- **Complete the GST 2.0 Institutional Architecture:** Formally operationalise the **`Article 279A(11)` Dispute Resolution Mechanism**; scale **GSTAT** benches rapidly to clear legacy backlogs; and build consensus inside the GST Council on a phased, revenue-protected roadmap to bring **electricity, real estate, and the five petroleum products** into GST while preserving States' fiscal buffers.
- **Rationalise CSS & Revitalise Cooperative Political Forums:** Convert fragmented `Article 282` Centrally Sponsored Schemes into fewer, outcome-oriented **flexible block grants** allowing regional adaptation; convene regular quarterly sittings of the **`Article 263` Inter-State Council** and **NITI Aayog Governing Council** for mandatory pre-legislative consultation on Concurrent List bills, mineral-taxation norms (reconciling *MADA* and `Section 9D`), and IAS central deputation cadres.
- **Restore Sub-National & Third-Tier Fiscal Credibility:** Implement uniform on-budget fiscal accounting eliminating off-budget borrowings across *both* Centre and States; subject unconditional cash transfers to transparent fiscal-impact statements and sunset reviews; incentivise **DISCOM governance/privatisation**; and enforce strict synchronisation of five-year **State Finance Commission** cycles alongside digital property-tax valuation to empower India's municipalities and panchayats.

## 1. Legislative Relations (Part XI, Articles 245–255)

- **Three Axes, No Judicial Split:** Legislative (`Arts. 245–255`), Administrative (`Arts. 256–263`), Financial (`Arts. 268–293`); **single integrated judiciary** enforcing both Union and State laws (Ambedkar, 4 Nov 1948). Modelled on GoI Act 1935 & Canada.
- **Territorial Extent (`Art. 245`):** Only **Parliament** makes **extra-territorial laws** (`Art. 245(2)`; nexus with India required — *GVK Industries*, 2011). State laws operate outside State borders only via **Doctrine of Territorial Nexus** (real connection + pertinent liability — *State of Bombay v. R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala*, 1957; *TISCO v. Bihar*, 1958).
- **4 Territorial Exceptions on Parliament:** (1) **`Art. 240`:** President's regulations (equal to Parliamentary Act; can repeal/amend Parliamentary Acts) for A&N, Lakshadweep, DNH&DD, Ladakh, and **Puducherry ONLY when Assembly suspended/dissolved**; (2) **5th Schedule:** Governor bars/modifies Parliamentary & State Acts in Scheduled Areas; (3) **6th Schedule:** **Governor** modifies Parliamentary Acts in **Assam**, while **President** does so in **Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram** (Governor handles State Acts); (4) **`Arts. 371A` (Nagaland) & `371G` (Mizoram)** Assembly resolution locks.
- **Seventh Schedule (`Art. 246`):** Precedence: **`Union > Concurrent > State`**. Active entry counts post-`101st Amendment` (omitting List I `92, 92C` & List II `52, 55`): **Union `98`** (orig. 97, pre-GST 100), **State `59`** (orig. 66, pre-GST 61), **Concurrent `52`** (orig. 47). Parliament legislates on State List for UTs (`Art. 246(4)`).
- **`42nd Amendment (1976)` — 5 Subjects Shifted List II → List III (`E-F-W-W-A`):** **E**ducation, **F**orests, **W**eights & Measures, **W**ild Animals & Birds, **A**dministration of Justice (lower courts). Also added List I `Entry 2A` (CAPFs) & List III `Entry 20A` (Population Control; Census = List I `Entry 69`).
- **`Art. 246A` (`101st Amend, 2016`) & Residuary Powers (`Art. 248` / List I `Entry 97`):** `Art. 246A` grants simultaneous co-equal GST taxing power (no repugnancy clause), with inter-state supply & imports exclusively Parliamentary. Residuary powers—**including residuary taxes (`Art. 248(2)`)**—vest solely in **Parliament** (Canadian model; vs States in USA/Australia & Viceroy in 1935 Act; *H.S. Dhillon*, 1971).
- **5 Extraordinary Parliamentary Routes on State List:**
  1. **`Art. 249` (National Interest):** **Rajya Sabha** resolution by **2/3rd present & voting** (NO >50% total strength rule!); valid **1 yr** (renewable 1 yr at a time); law expires **6 months** after resolution ends; State retains concurrent power (`Art. 251`).
  2. **`Art. 250` (National Emergency):** Active during `Art. 352`; expires **6 months** after Emergency ceases; State retains concurrent power (`Art. 251`).
  3. **`Art. 252` (2+ States' Request):** Applies to requesting/adopting States; **requesting States surrender power — ONLY Parliament can amend/repeal** (e.g., Wildlife Act 1972, Water Act 1974, Human Organs Act 1994).
  4. **`Art. 253` (International Treaties/Conventions):** No State consent needed; permanent override (e.g., EPA 1986, Geneva Conventions Act 1960, Biodiversity Act 2002).
  5. **`Art. 356` / `357` (President's Rule):** Parliament/President legislates for State; law **SURVIVES after President's Rule ends** until repealed/altered by restored State Legislature (`Art. 357(2)`).
- **5 Judicial Doctrines:** **Pith & Substance** (*F.N. Balsara*, 1951; *Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee*, 1947); **Colourable Legislation** (tests **competence, NOT motive** — *Gajapati Narayan Deo*, 1953; *Kameshwar Singh*, 1952); **Incidental/Ancillary Powers** (*Atiqa Begum*, 1940); **Harmonious Construction** (*Calcutta Gas*, 1962); **Repugnancy (`Art. 254` — Concurrent List only; *M. Karunanidhi*, 1979):** `254(1)` Parliamentary law prevails; `254(2)` State law with **Presidential assent** prevails *in that State*; **Proviso:** Parliament may still later amend, vary, or **directly repeal** that assented State law (*Zaverbhai*, 1954).
- **2024–2026 Federal Rulings & Counter:** ***MADA v. SAIL* (25 Jul 2024, 9-Judge 8–1)** overruled *India Cement* (1989) — royalty is NOT a tax, States tax mineral rights (`Entry 50`) & mineral-bearing land (`Entry 49`), arrears from `1 Apr 2005` over 12 yrs from `1 Apr 2026` → countered by **MMDR Amendment Act, 2026 (`Section 9D`, assent 17 Aug 2026)** barring State levies without Central rules & invalidating uncollected past levies (live Entry 49 constitutional clash!). ***State of U.P. v. Lalta Prasad Vaish* (23 Oct 2024, 9-Judge 8–1)** overruled *Synthetics & Chemicals* (1990) — **industrial alcohol** falls within "intoxicating liquor" (`List II, Entry 8`).
- **Centre's Control Over State Bills:** `Arts. 200–201` (Governor reservation — mandatory if endangering HC; President holds **absolute veto**, not bound even on re-passed bill); `Art. 304(b)` (prior Presidential sanction for trade restrictions — cured by later assent under `Art. 255`); `Art. 360` (Money Bills during Financial Emergency); `Art. 288(2)` (inter-state river authority taxes). ***Special Reference No. 1 of 2025* (20 Nov 2025, 5-Judge unanimous):** overruled rigid 1–3 month timelines & Art. 142 "deemed assent" of *Tamil Nadu v. Governor* (8 Apr 2025), while permitting limited review against prolonged unexplained inaction.

## 2. Administrative Relations (Part XI, Articles 256–263)

- **Executive Power (`Arts. 73 & 162`):** Co-extensive with legislative power (`Art. 73` Union List + treaties; `Art. 162` State List). **Crucial Rule:** Executive power on **Concurrent List** subjects **ordinarily rests with STATES** unless Constitution or Parliament expressly confers it on the Centre (`Art. 73(1) proviso`).
- **Union Directions & Coercive Sanction:** `Art. 256` (compliance with Parliamentary laws); `Art. 257` (non-impediment + directions on national/military **communications** & **railway protection** — extra costs reimbursed by Union, CJI-appointed arbitrator if disputed). Other directions: `Art. 339(2)` (ST welfare schemes), `Art. 350A` (mother-tongue primary education). **`Art. 365` (Part XVIII):** Disobeying *any* constitutional Union direction lets President hold State governance cannot run per Constitution → **triggers `Art. 356` President's Rule!**
- **Asymmetric Mutual Delegation (`Arts. 258 & 258A`):** `Art. 258(1)` **President → State WITH State consent**; `Art. 258(2)` **Parliament by law → State WITHOUT State consent** (Union pays extra cost per `258(3)`). `Art. 258A` (**`7th Amend, 1956`**) **Governor → Union WITH Union consent** (State Legislature CANNOT unilaterally delegate to Union).
- **Cooperation:** `Art. 261` (Full Faith & Credit — final **civil** judgments executable nationwide, **NOT criminal sentences**); `Art. 262` (Inter-state river water disputes — Parliament can bar SC jurisdiction); `Art. 263` (Inter-State Council, est. 1990 on Sarkaria advice); `Art. 307` (trade authority — unconstituted).
- **All-India Services (`Art. 312`):** **IAS, IPS, IFoS (`1966`)**; `42nd Amend (1976)` added **AIJS** (posts ≥ District Judge; not yet formed; not an `Art. 368` amendment). Creation requires **Rajya Sabha resolution by 2/3rd present & voting** + Parliamentary law. **Dual Control:** immediate control (posting/transfer/suspension) = **State**; ultimate control (dismissal/removal) = **Centre**. Defended by Patel (10 Oct 1949) & Ambedkar (4 Nov 1948); opposed by Rajamannar Committee (1969). **2022–2025 Row:** DoPT's draft **IAS Cadre Rules `Rule 6(1)`** amendments seeking mandatory Central Deputation release met strong multi-State resistance.
- **Integrative Machinery & Flashpoints:** State PSC members appointed by Governor, **removed ONLY by President** (`Art. 317`); Joint PSC appointed & removed by President. Single **Integrated Judiciary** (Common HCs under `Art. 231`), **ECI** (`Art. 324`), **CAG** (`Arts. 148–151`). **Security:** `Art. 355` + List I `Entry 2A` (CAPF deployment); BSF jurisdiction extended 15→50 km (2021); State withdrawals of **CBI "general consent"** (`Section 6, DSPE Act 1946` — *West Bengal v. UoI*, Jul 2024 upheld `Art. 131` suit maintainability). Extra-constitutional forums: NITI Aayog Governing Council, Zonal Councils (SRA 1956), NIC, CMs'/Governors'/Chief Secretaries' Conferences.

## 3. Financial Relations (Part XII, Articles 268–293)

- **Taxing Principles:** `Art. 265` (no tax without authority of law — right outside Part III). Strict separation of regulatory vs taxing entries (*M.P.V. Sundararamier*, 1958); **NO concurrent taxing power** except **GST (`Art. 246A`)**; residuary tax power solely with **Parliament (`Art. 248(2)`)**.
- **4 Constitutional Caps on State Taxes:** `Art. 276` profession tax capped at **`₹2,500/year`** (`60th Amend, 1988`); `Art. 286` bars taxing out-of-state & import/export supplies; `Art. 287` exempts Union/Railway electricity; `Art. 288` needs Presidential assent to tax inter-state river authorities.
- **3 Landmark Revenue Amendments:** **`80th Amend (2000)`** (10th FC Alternative Scheme — pooled nearly all Union taxes under `Art. 270`, repealed `Art. 272`); **`88th Amend (2003)`** (`Art. 268A` Service Tax — omitted by 101st Amend); **`101st Amend (2016)`** (GST — inserted `Art. 269A`, omitted `Art. 268A`, brought CGST into `Art. 270`, barred `Art. 271` surcharge on GST).
- **Exact Distribution Map (`Arts. 268–279`):**
  - **`Art. 268`:** Levied by Centre, collected & kept wholly by States (**outside CFI**) — **stamp duties** on bills/cheques/insurance/shares.
  - **`Art. 269`:** Levied & collected by Centre, assigned wholly to States — inter-state sale of **non-GST goods** & consignment tax.
  - **`Art. 269A`:** **IGST** on inter-state supply & imports — levied/collected by Centre, apportioned per GST Council (**State share outside CFI**).
  - **`Art. 270`:** **Divisible Pool** (Income, Corp, Customs, Excise, CGST, Union IGST share) shared via **Finance Commission** — **EXCLUDES** `Arts. 268/269/269A`, **`Art. 271` surcharges**, & **cesses**.
  - **`Art. 271`:** Union **surcharges** (100% Centre; **barred on GST**). Divisible Pool shrank **~89% (13th FC) → ~82% (14th) → ~78% (15th)** of Union GTR (effective State share ~32% of GTR).
  - **`Art. 274`:** **Prior Presidential recommendation** for bills affecting State-interest taxes, "agricultural income", or `Art. 271` surcharges. **`Art. 279`:** **CAG** certifies final **"net proceeds"**.
- **Grants-in-Aid (`Art. 275` vs `Art. 282`):** **`Art. 275` Statutory Grants:** Parliament → needy States, FC-recommended, **charged on CFI**, mandatory provisos for **ST welfare/Scheduled Areas** & **Assam 6th Schedule**. **`Art. 282` Discretionary Grants:** **Both Centre & States**, "any public purpose" **even outside legislative list**, votable, backbone of **Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS)**.
- **Borrowing (`Arts. 292–293`):** Centre borrows **within OR outside India** (`Art. 292`). States borrow **ONLY within India** (`Art. 293(1)`), and need **Union consent + conditions (`Art. 293(3)–(4)`)** whenever any central loan/guarantee is outstanding → annual Net Borrowing Ceilings (3% GSDP). ***State of Kerala v. Union of India* (1 Apr 2024):** refused interim relief on OBB deductions; referred `Art. 293` interpretation to **5-Judge Constitution Bench**.
- **Tax Immunities (`Arts. 285 & 289`) & Emergencies:** `Art. 285` exempts Union property (sovereign & commercial, **NOT Central PSUs**) from State/local taxes. `Art. 289` exempts State property/income from Union **direct** taxes only — **NOT indirect taxes** (*In re Sea Customs Act*, 1963), **NOT commercial ventures** (`289(2)` unless declared incidental), **NOT local bodies/State PSUs**. Emergencies: `Art. 354` (National Emergency revenue modification till end of financial year, laid before Parliament); `Art. 360` (Financial Emergency canons, salary cuts **including SC/HC judges**, State Money Bill reservation).

## 4. The Finance Commission (Articles 280 & 281)

- **Constitutional Profile:** **Quasi-judicial** body ("balancing wheel of fiscal federalism") constituted by **President** every **5 years** (or earlier). 1st FC: **K.C. Neogy** (`22 Nov 1951`). **Chairman + 4 Members**, **eligible for reappointment**, civil-court powers.
- **Qualifications (`Art. 280(2)`):** Fixed by **Parliament** via **Finance Commission (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1951** (NOT Constitution/President): Chairman = **public affairs**; 4 Members = (1) **HC Judge/qualified**, (2) **govt finance & accounts**, (3) **financial matters & administration**, (4) **economics**.
- **Functions (`Art. 280(3)`):** `(a)` Vertical & horizontal tax devolution; `(b)` `Art. 275` grants principles; `(bb)` & `(c)` augmenting State Consolidated Fund for **Panchayats & Municipalities** based on **SFCs** (`73rd/74th Amends, 1992`); `(d)` any other matter for **sound finance**.
- **Advisory Status & `Art. 281`:** Recommendations are **advisory, NOT legally binding** (accepted by strong convention). Under **`Art. 281`**, President lays report + **Explanatory Memorandum on action taken** before **BOTH Houses of Parliament**.
- **14th & 15th FCs:** 14th FC (**Dr. Y.V. Reddy**, 2015–20) jumped vertical share **32% → 42%**, ended Plan/Non-Plan & Special Category formula. 15th FC (**N.K. Singh**, 2020–21 & 2021–26) set **41%** (1% for J&K & Ladakh UTs); horizontal weights: **Income Distance 45%, Population (2011) 15%, Area 15%, Demographic Performance 12.5%, Forest & Ecology 10%, Tax Effort 2.5%**; ₹10.33 lakh cr grants (incl. ₹2.95 lakh cr RDG, ₹4.36 lakh cr local bodies, ₹45,000 cr agri & ₹4,800 cr education incentives — **Prelims 2025**).
- **16th Finance Commission (2026–31 — Verified 2026 Facts):** Constituted **31 Dec 2023**; Chair **Dr. Arvind Panagariya**; Members **Annie George Mathew, Dr. Manoj Panda, T. Rabi Sankar, Dr. Soumya Kanti Ghosh** (Secy **Ritvik Ranjanam Pandey**); submitted **17 Nov 2025**; tabled with ATR **1 Feb 2026**.
  - **Vertical:** Retained **`41%`** + accepted annual **CAG-certified `Art. 279` disclosure** + proposed **'Grand Bargain'** on cesses/surcharges.
  - **Horizontal Weights:** **Income Distance `42.5%`**, **Population (2011) `17.5%`**, **Demographic Performance `10%`** (1971–2011 growth rate), **Area `10%`** (floor 1.5%), **Forest `10%`** (incl. open forests), **Contribution to GDP `10%` (NEW — `√GSDP share`)**, **Tax Effort `0%` (dropped)**. Largest: **UP (17.619%)**, Bihar (9.948%); smallest: **Sikkim (0.335%)**; 14 gainers (e.g., Karnataka 3.647%→4.131%), 14 losers.
  - **Grants (`₹9,47,409 cr`):** **DISCONTINUED RDGs, sector & state-specific grants!** **Local Bodies `₹7,91,493 cr`** (RLB ₹4,35,236 cr + ULB ₹3,56,257 cr incl. ₹56,100 cr urban wastewater & ₹10,000 cr Urbanisation Premium; 80:20 Basic/Performance; 3 entry conditions: constituted bodies, audited accounts, timely SFCs) + **Disaster `₹1,55,916 cr`** Union share (90:10 NE/Himalayan, 75:25 others).
  - **Fiscal Roadmap:** Union deficit **3.5% GDP** by 2030–31; States **3% GSDP**; combined debt **77.3% → 73.1%**; ban **Off-Budget Borrowings**; **DISCOM privatisation** (debt SPVs); rationalise **unconditional cash transfers (20.2% of subsidies)**.

## 5. The GST Council & Modern Fiscal Federalism (101st Amendment, Article 279A)

- **Dual GST (`101st Amendment, 2016`; effective `1 July 2017`):** Kelkar Task Force (2004). Destination-based consumption tax with seamless ITC: **CGST, SGST, UTGST, IGST (`Art. 269A`)**; subsumed **17 taxes & 13 cesses**.
- **3 Exclusions/Nuances:** (1) **Alcoholic liquor for human consumption:** **constitutionally excluded (`Art. 366(12A)`)** — needs `Art. 368` amendment; (2) **5 Petroleum products** (crude, petrol, diesel, natural gas, ATF): inside GST but **deferred** pending GST Council date (`Art. 279A(5)`); (3) **Tobacco:** attracts **BOTH GST AND Central Excise**.
- **GST Council (`Art. 279A` — entrenched in `Art. 368` state ratification):** **33 members** = **Union FM (Chair)** + **MoS Finance** + **31 State/UT Finance Ministers** (28 States + Delhi, Puducherry, J&K per `Art. 366(26B)`). **Vice-Chair** elected by States from among themselves; Revenue Secy = ex-officio Secy; CBIC Chair = non-voting invitee.
- **Quorum & Mutual Veto (`Art. 279A(7)–(9)`):** **Quorum = `50%`** (17/33). **Majority = `≥ 3/4th (75%)` weighted votes present & voting** — **Union = `1/3rd (33.33%)`**, **States combined = `2/3rd (66.67%)`** (equal per State). Neither Union alone nor all States united can outvote the other → **mutual veto**; consensus convention. Special provisions for **11 States**; `Art. 279A(11)` dispute body still not operationalised.
- ***Union of India v. Mohit Minerals* (`19 May 2022`):** GST Council recommendations carry **persuasive value** and are **NOT binding** on Parliament or State Legislatures (equal simultaneous power under `Art. 246A`; deletion of draft `Art. 279B`; dialogue of cooperative & **"uncooperative" federalism**), though statutory subordinate rules remain bound. Struck down reverse-charge IGST on CIF ocean freight.
- **2025–2026 GST Milestones:**
  - **56th Council (`3 Sep 2025`, effective `22 Sep 2025`) — "GST 2.0":** Collapsed 4 slabs into **2 primary rates (`5%` merit, `18%` standard) + `40%` demerit/luxury rate**; **exempted ALL individual life & health insurance** & staples (UHT milk, paneer, Indian breads).
  - **GSTAT Launched (`24 Sep 2025`):** President **Justice Sanjaya Kumar Mishra**; Principal Bench (New Delhi) + **31 State Benches across 45 locations**.
  - **Compensation Cess Exited (`1 Feb 2026`):** Ended after COVID loan repayment (guaranteed 5 yrs had ended June 2022); replaced on sin goods by 40% GST + **Central Excise (Amendment) Act, 2025** (tobacco — shareable) & **Health Security se National Security Cess Act, 2025** (pan masala — earmarked cess).
  - **57th Council (`8 Oct 2026`, effective `1 Apr 2027`):** Recommended **removing arrest powers (omitting `Section 69` CGST Act)**, raising prosecution threshold **`₹1 cr → ₹5 cr`**, cutting general penalty **`₹25,000 → ₹10,000`**, & `₹10,000` show-cause notice floor.
- **Structural Challenges & Way Forward:** Shrinkage of divisible pool (~78% GTR), VFI (States incur >60% spend), North–South equity vs efficiency & RDG exit, CSS rigidity (`Art. 282`), populist cash transfers (20.2% subsidies) & OBB debt (`Art. 293`), weak SFCs. **Fixes:** Cess sunset clauses/'Grand Bargain', **Independent Fiscal Council** (N.K. Singh FRBM Committee, 2017) & permanent FC Secretariat, operationalise `Art. 279A(11)`, flexible block CSS, regular `Art. 263` Inter-State Council, timely SFCs.
