## 1. The constitutional question at the heart of the doctrine

Can Parliament, acting by a special majority under **Article 368**, abolish elections, dismantle federalism, or extinguish Fundamental Rights? For twenty-three years after 1950, Parliament and the Supreme Court wrestled over that question. Its answer—the **doctrine of basic structure**, laid down on **24 April 1973** in ***Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala***—is India's defining contribution to global constitutional law.

The rule is simple: Parliament may amend any provision of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights in Part III, **but cannot alter or destroy its basic structure or essential framework**. Nowhere does the constitutional text use or define "basic structure"; it is a **judicial innovation**, inferred from a written Constitution and developed case by case.

The clash arose between **Article 13(2)**—barring the State from making any **"law"** abridging Part III—and **Article 368**, conferring amending power. Does **"law" in Article 13(2) include a constitutional amendment?** Beneath lay the line between **ordinary legislative power** (Articles 245–246) and **constituent power**. While the **Constituent Assembly** held *original* constituent power, Parliament under Article 368 holds *derived* constituent power: to "amend" preserves constitutional identity rather than abrogating it.

> **Prelims pointer:** **"Basic structure"** is **neither mentioned nor defined** anywhere in the Constitution. The textual clash that produced it was whether an **Article 368** amendment counts as **"law"** under **Article 13(2)**.

## 2. Evolution: from *Shankari Prasad* to *Kesavananda Bharati*

The doctrine evolved through five dramatic stages between 1951 and 1973, triggered by the tussle between Parliament's agrarian land reforms and the right to property (then under Articles 19(1)(f) and 31).

```text
 1951  Shankari Prasad  Art. 368 != Art. 13; FRs amendable
            │
 1965  Sajjan Singh     Upheld; Hidayatullah & Mudholkar
                        JJ. doubt it: "basic features"
            │
 1967  Golaknath (6:5)  FRs immutable; Art. 368 procedural;
       (11 judges)      prospective overruling applied
            │
 1971  24th & 25th      Art. 13(4), 368(1)/(3); mandatory
       Amendments       assent; Art. 31C shields 39(b)-(c)
            │
 1973  Kesavananda      Golaknath overruled; FRs amendable
       (24 Apr, 7:6)    EXCEPT basic structure inviolable
```

### *Shankari Prasad* (1951) and *Sajjan Singh* (1965)

When High Courts struck down zamindari-abolition laws, Parliament passed the **1st Amendment (1951)** adding Articles 31A, 31B and the Ninth Schedule. In ***Shankari Prasad v. Union of India* (1951)**, a unanimous five-judge bench held that **"law" in Article 13(2) covers only ordinary statutes**, not constitutional amendments enacted under constituent power (Article 368)—so Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights.

When the **17th Amendment (1964)** placed 44 more land-reform laws in the Ninth Schedule, ***Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan* (1965)** (decided 30 October 1964) again upheld the amendment. Chief Justice Gajendragadkar, for three of the five judges, followed *Shankari Prasad*. The other two agreed that the petitions must fail, but wrote separately to doubt that ruling, and their doubts broke the consensus. **Justice M. Hidayatullah** found it hard to believe that the assurances of Part III were "the play things of a special majority". **Justice J.R. Mudholkar** was the first to speak of the Constitution's **"basic features"**, citing the Pakistan Supreme Court under Chief Justice A.R. Cornelius in ***Fazlul Quader Chowdhry v. Mohd. Abdul Haque* (1963)**:

> "It is also a matter for consideration whether making a change in a basic feature of the Constitution can be regarded merely as an amendment or would it be, in effect, rewriting a part of the Constitution; and if the latter, would it be within the purview of Article 368?" — Justice J.R. Mudholkar, *Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan* (1965)

In **February 1965**, German scholar **Dieter Conrad** lectured at **Banaras Hindu University** on *"Implied Limitations of the Amending Power"*, drawing on Weimar Germany's legal self-destruction and post-war Germany's **Eternity Clause (Article 79(3))**. Advocate M.K. Nambiyar introduced Conrad's paper in *Golaknath*, and **Nani Palkhivala** deployed it decisively in *Kesavananda*.

### *I.C. Golaknath* (1967) and the 24th and 25th Amendments (1971)

In ***I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab* (1967)**, an **11-judge bench** under Chief Justice K. Subba Rao reversed *Shankari Prasad* by **6:5**: Fundamental Rights were **"transcendental and immutable"**; Article 368 prescribed only *procedure* (not *power*, traced to residuary legislative power under Article 248 and Entry 97, List I); hence amendments were "law" under Article 13(2). To save past agrarian reforms (1st, 4th and 17th Amendments), it applied the American **doctrine of prospective overruling**, barring Parliament from abridging Part III only prospectively.

Parliament hit back with the **24th Amendment (1971)**—inserting **Articles 13(4) and 368(3)** to exempt Article 368 from Article 13, renaming Article 368's heading (*"Power of Parliament to amend the Constitution and procedure therefor"*), inserting **Article 368(1)** affirming "constituent power", and making presidential assent mandatory—and the **25th Amendment (1971)**, replacing "compensation" with "amount" in Article 31(2) and inserting **Article 31C** to shield **Article 39(b)–(c)** laws from Articles 14, 19 and 31 while barring judicial review of legislative declarations.

### *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (24 April 1973)

Heard by the **largest bench ever—13 judges** under **Chief Justice S.M. Sikri**—over 68 days (Nani Palkhivala against H.M. Seervai and Attorney General Niren De), *Kesavananda Bharati* produced **11 opinions** (and a one-page *"View by the majority"* signed by 9 judges). By **7:6**, the Court:

1. **Overruled *Golaknath*** and upheld the **24th Amendment**: Parliament can amend **any part** of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights.
2. **Laid down the basic structure limit:** Article 368 does **not** empower Parliament to alter or destroy the Constitution's **"basic structure or framework"**.
3. **Trimmed Article 31C:** Upheld its first limb shielding Article 39(b)–(c) laws, **but struck down its second limb ousting judicial review**.

**Justice H.R. Khanna** cast the decisive **7th vote**. Six judges (Sikri CJ, Shelat, Grover, Hegde, Mukherjea, Reddy JJ) found implied limits protecting Part III; six (Ray, Palekar, Mathew, Beg, Dwivedi, Y.V. Chandrachud JJ) found no substantive limits. Justice Khanna upheld the 24th Amendment and excluded property from basic structure, yet agreed—quoting Conrad—that "amend" cannot destroy the Constitution's basic structure.

Next day (25 April 1973), three senior majority judges (Shelat, Hegde, Grover JJ) were superseded to make **Justice A.N. Ray** Chief Justice, and an Emergency bid in November 1975 to review *Kesavananda* dissolved after Palkhivala's defence. Crucially, **24 April 1973** became the bright-line cut-off date for testing amendments and Ninth Schedule insertions (*Waman Rao*, *I.R. Coelho*).

> **Remember:** **Justice Mudholkar** coined "basic features" (*Sajjan Singh*, 1965, citing Pakistan's *Fazlul Quader Chowdhry* case); **Dieter Conrad** theorised "implied limitations" (1965 BHU lecture); and **Justice H.R. Khanna** cast the decisive 7th vote in *Kesavananda Bharati* (24 April 1973).

## 3. Landmark judgments after *Kesavananda*

What began as a fragile 7:6 compromise hardened into constitutional orthodoxy during the Emergency and expanded over five decades.

In ***Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975)**—the **first time the Court struck down a constitutional amendment** via basic structure—Parliament had passed the **39th Amendment (1975)** adding **Article 329A(4)** to place the Prime Minister's election beyond judicial review and retroactively validate Indira Gandhi's Rae Bareli win. Four of the five judges on the bench had dissented in *Kesavananda*, yet all treated it as binding. Justices Khanna, Mathew and Y.V. Chandrachud struck down clause (4) for damaging the basic structure: **free and fair elections**, **democracy**, the **rule of law** and the **separation of powers**. Chief Justice Ray held it invalid because it declared the election valid without applying any law, and Justice Beg read it narrowly so that the Court could still hear the appeal. The same judgment said that the basic structure test applies to constitutional amendments, not to ordinary laws.

Parliament retaliated via the **42nd Amendment (1976)**, adding **Articles 368(4)–(5)** to claim unlimited constituent power and bar judicial review, while extending **Article 31C** to all Directive Principles. After the Rajya Sabha blocked their repeal in the 44th Amendment, a five-judge bench led by **Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud** struck down **Articles 368(4)–(5)** unanimously and, by **4:1** (Justice Bhagwati dissenting), the widening of **Article 31C**, in ***Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (31 July 1980)**:

> "Parliament cannot, under Article 368, expand its amending power so as to acquire for itself the right to repeal or abrogate the Constitution or to destroy its basic and essential features. The donee of a limited power cannot by the exercise of that power convert the limited power into an unlimited one." — Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud, *Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980)

Alongside **limited amending power** and **judicial review**, *Minerva Mills* held that **harmony between Part III and Part IV** belongs to basic structure. Next year, ***Waman Rao* (1981)** fixed **24 April 1973** as the bright line: pre-cut-off amendments and Ninth Schedule laws stay immune; post-cut-off insertions remain reviewable.

| Case & Year | Provision tested | Core holding & basic structure principle |
|---|---|---|
| ***Raj Narain* (1975)** | 39th Amdt (Art. 329A(4)) | First amendment struck down; **free & fair elections**, democracy, rule of law, separation of powers |
| ***Minerva Mills* (1980)** | 42nd Amdt (Art. 368(4)–(5), 31C) | Both voided; **limited amending power**, **judicial review**, **FR–DPSP balance** |
| ***Waman Rao* (1981)** | Arts. 31A–31B, Ninth Schedule | Drew **24 April 1973** bright line for Ninth Schedule & amendment review |
| ***Sampath Kumar* (1987)** | Art. 323A tribunals | Accepted tribunals as HC substitutes only if independent; **judicial review** |
| ***Kihoto Hollohan* (1992)** | 52nd Amdt (Tenth Schedule) | Upheld the Tenth Schedule as consistent with democracy; **struck down Para 7** (bar on courts) because half the states had not ratified it; Speaker's decision open to **judicial review** |
| ***Indra Sawhney* (1992)** | OBC quota (Art. 16(4)) | Reaffirmed **rule of law** and substantive **equality** (including creamy layer) |
| ***S.R. Bommai* (1994)** | Art. 356 President's Rule (9 JJ) | **Federalism, secularism, democracy, unity & integrity**; applied to **Art. 356 executive action** |
| ***L. Chandra Kumar* (1997)** | Arts. 323A–323B (7 judges) | Overruled *Sampath Kumar*; **Art. 32 & 226 review** inviolable; tribunals only supplemental |
| ***M. Nagaraj* (2006)** | 77th, 81st, 82nd, 85th Amdts | Upheld SC/ST promotion amendments; laid down **width test** and **identity test** |
| ***I.R. Coelho* (2007)** | Ninth Schedule (9 judges) | Post-24 April 1973 Ninth Schedule laws face **rights test** & **essence of rights** (Arts. 14, 19, 21) |

| ***SCAORA* (NJAC, 2015)** | 99th Amdt & NJAC Act (4:1) | Struck down NJAC; **independence of judiciary** & judicial primacy in appointments |

Three post-1990 doctrinal tools matter most for exams. First, ***S.R. Bommai* (1994)** applied basic structure (**secularism**) to an **executive proclamation under Article 356**, holding that an anti-secular state regime invites constitutional dismissal. Second, ***M. Nagaraj* (2006)** framed the **width test** (checking whether an amendment erases ceilings like backwardness, inadequate representation, the 50 per cent current-vacancy cap, and Article 335 efficiency) and **identity test** (checking whether it alters the equality code's core identity). Third, ***I.R. Coelho* (2007)** closed the Ninth Schedule's "black hole": laws added after **24 April 1973** face the **rights test** and **essence of rights test** against the **Golden Triangle (Articles 14, 19, 21)** and Article 15.

## 4. Elements of the basic structure

The Supreme Court has deliberately avoided a closed list, identifying basic elements inductively whenever removing a principle would leave the 1949 Constitution unrecognisable.

| Pillar | Recognised elements of basic structure | Illustrative judgments |
|---|---|---|
| **Supremacy & polity** | Supremacy of the Constitution; sovereign, democratic, republican polity; **limited amending power**; **rule of law**; **separation of powers**; parliamentary system | *Kesavananda* (1973), *Raj Narain* (1975), *Minerva Mills* (1980), *Bommai* (1994), *Kuldip Nayar* (2006) |
| **Democracy & polls** | **Free and fair elections**; independence of the Election Commission; electoral equality | *Raj Narain* (1975), *Kihoto Hollohan* (1992), *Anoop Baranwal* (2023) |
| **Federalism & unity** | **Federal character**; **secularism**; **unity and integrity** of the nation | *Kesavananda* (1973), *Bommai* (1994), *Dr. Balram Singh* (2024) |
| **Rights & welfare** | **FR–DPSP harmony**; welfare state and social justice; individual freedom and dignity; **equality** and the **Golden Triangle (Arts. 14, 19, 21)** | *Kesavananda* (1973), *Minerva Mills* (1980), *Indra Sawhney* (1992), *M. Nagaraj* (2006), *I.R. Coelho* (2007) |
| **Judiciary & review** | **Judicial review** (Arts. 32, 136, 141, 142, 226, 227); **independence of the judiciary**; effective access to justice | *Minerva Mills* (1980), *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997), *Madras Bar Association* (2014, 2025), *NJAC* (2015) |

> **Prelims pointer:** Three frequent traps: (1) **Right to Property** (Article 300A) is **not** basic structure; (2) British-style **parliamentary sovereignty** is **not** basic structure—**constitutional supremacy** and **limited amending power** are; (3) *Janhit Abhiyan* (2022, 3:2) held that the **103rd Amendment's** economic reservation does **not** breach basic structure.

## 5. Ordinary laws, executive acts and recent rulings (2023–2026)

Can an **ordinary statute** be struck down for violating the basic structure? The orthodox answer—set out in ***Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975)**, ***State of Karnataka v. Union of India* (1977)**, and a five-judge bench in ***Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India* (2006)** (upholding open-ballot Rajya Sabha voting)—is **no**. Basic structure tests **constitutional amendments**, whereas an ordinary law falls only for **(i) lack of legislative competence** or **(ii) violating Part III or another specific constitutional provision**. Yet ***S.R. Bommai* (1994)** applied basic structure to an **Article 356 executive proclamation**, and the **tribunal rulings** (*Madras Bar Association*, 2014–2021) struck down ordinary statutes undermining judicial independence.

Five Supreme Court verdicts between 2023 and November 2026 sharpened this frontier:

- ***Anoop Baranwal* (March 2023) & *Electoral Bonds* (*ADR*, February 2024):** Five-judge benches grounded **free and fair elections** and an **independent Election Commission** in basic structure—*Anoop Baranwal* setting an interim PM–LoP–CJI panel until Parliament passed the 2023 CEC Act (replacing the CJI with a Union minister; on 23 September 2026 a two-judge bench sent the challenge to that Act to the Chief Justice for a Constitution Bench), and *Electoral Bonds* voiding anonymous corporate funding under Articles 19(1)(a) and 14.
- ***Anjum Kadari* (UP Madarsa Act, 5 November 2024):** Reversing the Allahabad High Court, CJI D.Y. Chandrachud's bench reaffirmed *Kuldip Nayar*: **ordinary statutes cannot be invalidated solely on an abstract basic-structure ground** like undefined "secularism" without proving legislative incompetence or breach of a specific Article.
- ***Property Owners Association* (5 November 2024, 9 judges):** Unanimously held that once *Minerva Mills* voided the 42nd Amendment's expanded **Article 31C**, the **original narrower Article 31C** (protecting Articles 39(b)–(c)) **automatically revived**; by 8:1, held not all private property counts as "material resources of the community".
- ***Dr. Balram Singh* (25 November 2024):** CJI Sanjiv Khanna's bench dismissed pleas against inserting **"socialist"** and **"secular"** into the Preamble—holding Article 368 reaches the Preamble despite the 26 November 1949 adoption date, reaffirming **secularism as a basic feature**, and clarifying Indian "socialism" denotes a welfare State without restricting mixed-economy enterprise.
- ***Madras Bar Association* (19 November 2025):** CJI B.R. Gavai and Justice K. Vinod Chandran struck down re-enacted appointment and tenure clauses of the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** for violating **separation of powers** and **judicial independence**. The Court said these principles are "firmly embedded in the text, structure, and spirit of the Constitution", and that Parliament cannot override a judgment by re-enacting what was struck down without curing the defect. That sits comfortably with *Anjum Kadari*: a statute falls for breaching principles the Constitution itself contains, not an abstract appeal to basic structure. Parliament responded with the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026** (assent 13 August 2026), which repeals the 2021 Act and sets up the National Tribunals Commission the Court had asked for.

> "The basic structure of our Constitution, like the north star, guides and gives certain direction to the interpreters and implementers of the Constitution when the path ahead is convoluted." — Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud, 18th Nani Palkhivala Memorial Lecture, Mumbai, 21 January 2023

> **In the news:** Chief Justice Chandrachud's "north star" defence replied directly to Vice-President **Jagdeep Dhankhar's** Jaipur address on **11 January 2023** (83rd All-India Presiding Officers Conference), where he questioned *Kesavananda* and the 2015 NJAC invalidation, arguing that the supremacy of the people's mandate, and so the sovereignty of Parliament, is "the basic" of any "basic structure". That tension stayed live through the **November 2025 Tribunals Reforms judgment** and ongoing JPC scrutiny of the **129th Amendment Bill, 2024** (*One Nation, One Election*).

## 6. Evaluating the doctrine: constitutional shield or judicial veto?

**Strengths.** Basic structure enforces **constitutional supremacy**, stopping transient two-thirds majorities from dismantling democracy—as proven during the Emergency (*Raj Narain*, *Minerva Mills*). It shields India's pluralist federal-secular compact (*Bommai*, *I.R. Coelho*) without paralysing governance: Parliament has passed over seventy amendments since 1973 (including the 73rd/74th, 86th, 101st, 103rd and 106th) with very few clauses struck down. Globally, it is **India's signature constitutional export**—adopted by **Bangladesh** (*Anwar Hossain Chowdhury*, 1989) and Belize, engaged cautiously in **Pakistan** (*District Bar Association Rawalpindi*, 2015) and Malaysia, and debated in **Kenya** (where its Supreme Court declined to import it 6:1 in March 2022 given Kenya's mandatory referendum)—paralleling **Germany's textual Eternity Clause (Article 79(3))**.

**Criticisms.** Critics highlight the **counter-majoritarian difficulty** of unelected judges vetoing parliamentary supermajorities, lack of textual entrenchment, and vagueness. Objections peaked in the **2015 NJAC verdict (4:1)**, where elevating judge-made **judicial primacy** into basic structure voided a near-unanimous 99th Amendment, drawing Justice J. Chelameswar's lone dissent warning against insulating the collegium from reform.

**Way forward.** Basic structure remains vital if paired with **judicial self-restraint**—serving strictly as a high-threshold safety valve for constitutional identity, anchored by *M. Nagaraj*'s **width and identity tests** and *Anjum Kadari*'s textual discipline.

> **Mains angle:** Frame GS-2 answers on four axes: (1) **Constitutionalism vs. parliamentary sovereignty** (derived constituent power); (2) **Doctrinal arc** (*Shankari Prasad* → *Sajjan Singh*/Conrad → *Golaknath* → *Kesavananda* → *Raj Narain*, *Minerva Mills*, *Bommai*, *I.R. Coelho*, *NJAC*, *Janhit Abhiyan*); (3) **Recent refinement** (*Anjum Kadari* 2024 vs. *Madras Bar Association* 2025, *Balram Singh* 2024, "North Star" exchange); and (4) **Balanced way forward** pairing global validation with judicial restraint.

- **Core rule:** Parliament can amend any provision under **Art. 368** (including FRs) **except** altering/destroying the **basic structure** (judge-made; undefined in the text).
- **Root clash:** Does **Art. 13(2)** "law" cover **Art. 368** amendments? Derived (Parliament) vs. original (Constituent Assembly) constituent power.
- ***Shankari Prasad* (1951, 1st Amdt):** Art. 368 amendment ≠ Art. 13 "law"; FRs amendable.
- ***Sajjan Singh* (1965, 17th Amdt):** Followed *Shankari Prasad*; in separate opinions **Hidayatullah J.** doubted it and **Mudholkar J.** first spoke of **"basic features"** (citing Pakistan's *Fazlul Quader Chowdhry*, 1963); **Dieter Conrad** lectured at BHU (Feb 1965).
- ***Golaknath* (1967, 11 JJ, 6:5):** FRs "transcendental and immutable"; Art. 368 procedural; **prospective overruling**.
- **24th & 25th Amdts (1971):** 24th added **Arts. 13(4) & 368(3)**, renamed Art. 368 heading, affirmed constituent power, made assent mandatory; 25th added **Art. 31C** (Arts. 39(b)–(c)).
- ***Kesavananda Bharati* (24 April 1973, 13 JJ, 7:6, CJI Sikri):** Overruled *Golaknath*, upheld 24th Amdt & Art. 31C first limb, voided Art. 31C review bar, birthed **basic structure** via **H.R. Khanna J.'s** 7th vote (**24 April 1973** = cut-off).
- ***Raj Narain* (1975):** **First** amendment voided (**39th Amdt**, Art. 329A(4)); free & fair elections, rule of law, separation of powers.
- ***Minerva Mills* (1980):** Voided **Art. 368(4)–(5)** & widened **Art. 31C** (4:1); **limited amending power**, **judicial review**, **FR–DPSP balance**.
- ***Waman Rao* (1981) & *I.R. Coelho* (2007, 9 JJ):** **24 April 1973** Ninth Schedule cut-off; post-1973 laws face **rights test** & **essence of rights** (**Arts. 14, 19, 21**).
- ***L. Chandra Kumar* (1997, 7 JJ) & *Kihoto Hollohan* (1992):** **Arts. 32 & 226** review inviolable (overruling *Sampath Kumar*); Tenth Schedule **Para 7** voided.
- ***S.R. Bommai* (1994, 9 JJ):** Federalism, secularism, democracy, unity; applied to **Art. 356 executive action**.
- ***M. Nagaraj* (2006):** Upheld 77th, 81st, 82nd, 85th Amdts; crafted **width test** and **identity test**.
- ***NJAC* (2015, 4:1) & *Janhit Abhiyan* (2022, 3:2):** Voided **99th Amdt** (judicial primacy); upheld **103rd Amdt** (EWS quota).
- **Ordinary laws (2024–25):** *Kuldip Nayar* (2006) & ***Anjum Kadari* (Nov 2024)** bar striking statutes on abstract basic structure; ***Madras Bar Assn* (Nov 2025)** voided **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** clauses breaching judicial independence; Parliament replaced it with the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026** (National Tribunals Commission).
- **Nov 2024 landmarks:** ***Property Owners Assn* (9 JJ)** revived narrower **Art. 31C**; ***Dr. Balram Singh*** upheld **"socialist"** and **"secular"** in Preamble, affirming secularism as basic feature.
- **Traps & global lens:** Property (**Art. 300A**) & British parliamentary sovereignty are **NOT** basic structure; CJI Chandrachud called it a **"north star"** (Jan 2023) after VP Dhankhar's critique; adopted by **Bangladesh** (1989), akin to **Germany's Art. 79(3)**.
