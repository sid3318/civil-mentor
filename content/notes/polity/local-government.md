## 1. Evolution of Panchayati Raj and Reform Committees (1957–1992)

Local self-government represents the foundational bedrock of India's three-tier constitutional democracy. While the Union Parliament and State Legislatures embody representative governance at national and provincial scales, Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) and Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) translate democratic sovereignty into everyday citizenship—placing drinking water, rural roads, public sanitation, primary schools, and neighbourhood planning within direct reach of the people. By enacting the **`73rd` and `74th Constitutional Amendment Acts, 1992`**, India accomplished an unprecedented constitutional transformation: converting a classical two-tier federation (Union and States) into a legally protected **three-tier federal polity** embracing over **2.5 lakh Gram Panchayats**, **nearly 5,000 urban local bodies**, and more than **31 lakh elected grassroots representatives**.

Yet the architecture of local self-government in India did not emerge overnight in 1992. It reflects an intricate historical dialogue stretching across ancient indigenous village assemblies, British colonial experiments in fiscal and administrative decentralisation, the intense ideological collision between Mahatma Gandhi and Dr. B.R. Ambedkar in the Constituent Assembly, four decades of post-Independence committee experimentation, and hard-won parliamentary consensus. Mastering this historical trajectory is indispensable for both UPSC Prelims—where committee chronologies, colonial resolutions, and constitutional bills are tested with surgical precision—and Mains GS-2, where evaluating the philosophy of **democratic decentralisation** anchors every analytical answer on grassroots governance.

### Historical Roots: Ancient Village Assemblies and Colonial Decentralisation

Indigenous traditions of rural self-management run deep across Indian history, although modern scholarship cautions against romanticising pre-modern assemblies as egalitarian democracies. In the Vedic era, deliberative assemblies such as the *sabha* (a smaller council of elders and notables) and the *samiti* (a broader folk assembly) exercised significant social and political restraint over early chieftains. During the Mauryan and Gupta epochs, Kautilya's *Arthashastra* and epigraphic records describe village headmen (*gramika* or *gramabhojaka*) assisted by local elders in maintaining irrigation works, settling petty disputes, and collecting agrarian revenue.

The most celebrated and best-documented institutional model of ancient rural self-government flourished in early medieval South India under the **imperial Cholas** (9th to 13th centuries CE). Chola rural administration differentiated settlements cleanly by social and economic composition: ordinary peasant villages functioned through the ***ur***, merchant towns operated through the ***nagaram***, and tax-free Brahmin settlements (*brahmadeyas*) governed themselves through the ***sabha*** or ***mahasabha***. The famous **Uttaramerur inscriptions** dated **919 CE and 921 CE**—engraved on the walls of the Vaikuntha Perumal temple in present-day Kanchipuram district of Tamil Nadu during the reign of **Parantaka I**—lay out an astonishingly detailed written constitution for Uttaramerur's thirty wards (*kudumbus*).

Under this Chola constitution, functional standing committees known as ***variyams***—including the *eri variyam* (irrigation tank committee), *thotta variyam* (garden committee), *pon variyam* (gold and currency oversight), and *samvatsara variyam* (annual supervisory committee)—were constituted every year through the ***kudavolai*** system. Names of eligible candidates from each ward were inscribed on palm-leaf tickets, placed inside an earthen pot, shaken publicly in the assembly hall, and drawn out by a young boy to prevent manipulation. Eligibility carried strict property, educational, age, and ethical filters: candidates had to be between **35 and 70 years** of age, own taxable land, possess a house built on their own site, know Vedic texts and administrative business, and hold a clean record; anyone who had failed to submit committee accounts—along with their close relatives—was permanently disqualified from contesting again.

#### British Colonial Evolution (1687–1947)

When British colonial administration consolidated power after 1757, early revenue centralisation—especially the Zamindari and Ryotwari land settlements alongside British judicial courts—severely eroded traditional village corporate life. Nevertheless, fiscal pressures after the Revolt of 1857 compelled the colonial state to construct modern statutory local bodies, initially in presidency cities and gradually across rural districts:

- **First Municipal Corporation at Madras (1687–88):** Established under a Royal Charter of the East India Company, Madras became India's first modern municipal corporation, followed by municipal corporations in **Bombay and Calcutta in 1726**.
- **Lord Mayo's Resolution of 1870:** Driven primarily by the need to relieve imperial finances after the 1857 fiscal deficit, Viceroy Lord Mayo initiated **financial decentralisation** by transferring provincial responsibility for local services (education, roads, medical relief) and authorising local taxation through local boards.
- **Lord Ripon's Resolution of 1882 ("Magna Carta of Local Self-Government"):** Marking a watershed shift from mere administrative convenience to political philosophy, Viceroy **Lord Ripon** issued his landmark resolution on **18 May 1882**, earning the title **"Father of Local Self-Government in India."** Ripon insisted that local boards be nurtured primarily as *"an instrument of political and popular education"* rather than purely for administrative efficiency. He recommended a network of taluka/tehsil and district boards with a **majority of non-official members** (elected wherever feasible), **non-official chairpersons** in place of the District Collector, reduced bureaucratic interference, and dedicated local revenue sources. Although provincial bureaucracies largely diluted Ripon's progressive vision on the ground, the 1882 Resolution laid the conceptual foundation for modern local self-rule.
- **Royal Commission on Decentralisation (1907–1909):** Chaired by **C.E.H. Hobhouse**, this Commission diagnosed the failure of existing district boards as excessive Collector dominance and remoteness from villages. It explicitly recommended reviving **village panchayats** with minor judicial powers, village sanitation duties, elementary school management, and a share of land cess, alongside establishing sub-district boards in every tehsil and empowering municipalities with elected non-official presidents.
- **Government of India Act, 1919 (Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms):** Under provincial **Dyarchy**, **local self-government became a "Transferred" subject** placed under the charge of elected Indian ministers responsible to provincial legislatures. This triggered a wave of provincial Village Panchayat Acts across Bengal, Madras, Bombay, the United Provinces, Bihar, and Punjab during the 1920s, while simultaneously removing the official Collector from the chairmanship of most urban municipalities.
- **Government of India Act, 1935:** With the abolition of provincial dyarchy and the introduction of **Provincial Autonomy**, local self-government was firmly placed in the **Provincial Legislative List**. That federal allocation passed directly into Independent India's Constitution as **Entry 5 of the State List** in the **Seventh Schedule**, giving State Legislatures plenary legislative competence over *"Local government, that is to say, the constitution and powers of municipal corporations, improvement trusts, district boards, mining settlement authorities and other local authorities for the purpose of local self-government or village administration."*

> [!prelims]
> **Four Classic Historical Traps Tested by UPSC:**
> 1. **Chola *Kudavolai* ≠ Universal Adult Suffrage:** The Uttaramerur *kudavolai* (919/921 CE, Parantaka I) operated exclusively inside a Brahmin *brahmadeya* (*sabha*) via **drawing palm-leaf lots** among propertied, educated men aged 35–70—it was neither universal suffrage nor ballot voting.
> 2. **Lord Mayo (1870) vs Lord Ripon (1882):** Pair **Mayo (1870)** strictly with **financial decentralisation**; pair **Ripon (1882)** with the **"Magna Carta of Local Self-Government"** and **"Father of Local Self-Government in India."**
> 3. **GoI Act 1919 vs GoI Act 1935:** Local self-government became a **"Transferred" subject** under the **1919 Act** (Dyarchy) and a **Provincial subject** under the **1935 Act** (now **Entry 5, State List**).
> 4. **First Municipal Corporation:** **Madras (1687–88)** came nearly four decades before **Bombay and Calcutta (1726)**.

### Gandhi's Gram Swaraj vs Ambedkar's Village Critique and Article 40

No debate in the Constituent Assembly captured the philosophical tension of modern Indian state-building more starkly than the clash over the constitutional status of the village. Outside the Assembly, **Mahatma Gandhi** had long insisted that genuine political freedom (*Purna Swaraj*) was meaningless unless power flowed upward from self-reliant village republics (*Gram Swaraj*). Writing in *Harijan* during the Quit India movement and on the eve of Independence, Gandhi articulated an anti-hierarchical vision of decentralised democracy structured as an "oceanic circle" rather than a centralised state pyramid:

> "My idea of village swaraj is that it is a complete republic, independent of its neighbours for its own vital wants, and yet interdependent for many others in which dependence is a necessity... Independence must begin at the bottom. Thus, every village will be a republic or panchayat having full powers... Life will not be a pyramid with the apex sustained by the bottom. But it will be an oceanic circle whose centre will be the individual always ready to perish for the village." — Mahatma Gandhi, *Harijan*, 26 July 1942 and 28 July 1946

Gandhi was under no illusion about rural caste oppression—he expressly stated in *Harijan* (1942 and 1946) that village swaraj could never coexist with graded untouchability—yet he believed ethical regeneration and village self-rule were inseparable. Inside the Constituent Assembly, however, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**, Chairman of the Drafting Committee, brought the lived sociological critique of Dalits and marginalised castes to the constitutional table. When the initial Draft Constitution circulated in February 1948 omitted any mention of village panchayats, Gandhian members protested vigorously. Introducing the Draft Constitution on **4 November 1948**, Ambedkar responded to romanticised colonial tributes (such as Sir Charles Metcalfe's 1830 description of Indian villages as *"little republics, having nearly everything they can want within themselves"*) with one of the most quoted passages in Indian constitutional history:

> "I hold that these village republics have been the ruination of India. What is the village but a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness and communalism? I am glad that the Draft Constitution has discarded the village and adopted the individual as its unit." — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, 4 November 1948

Ambedkar's apprehension was deeply institutional: empowering un-democratised villages before dismantling feudal land hierarchies and caste dominance risked handing formal state coercion to dominant rural elites, crushing Dalit rights under the banner of local autonomy. Yet Gandhian delegates—including K. Santhanam, Mahavir Tyagi, Shibban Lal Saxena, and Ananthasayanam Ayyangar—argued that refusing to trust rural citizens with local self-rule would perpetuate colonial bureaucratic paternalism. On **22 November 1948**, **K. Santhanam** moved a pragmatic compromise amendment, which Ambedkar accepted without forcing a division, inserting **`Article 40`** into **Part IV (Directive Principles of State Policy)**: *"The State shall take steps to organise village panchayats and endow them with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as units of self-government."*

> [!debate]
> **Gandhi vs Ambedkar — Did the 73rd Amendment Synthesise Both Visions?**
> - **The Gandhian Triumph:** The 73rd Amendment finally redeemed `Article 40`, embedding the **Gram Sabha** (`Art. 243A`) and three-tier Panchayats into the constitutional core as permanent instruments of grassroots participatory democracy.
> - **The Ambedkarite Safeguard:** Crucially, Part IX did **not** revive traditional caste-bound panchayats! Instead, it constitutionally embedded **mandatory proportional reservation for SCs and STs** and **at least one-third reservation for women** (`Art. 243D`), universal adult franchise, independent State Election Commissions (`Art. 243K`), and subordination to Part III Fundamental Rights—directly addressing Ambedkar's fear of elite capture by democratising the village from within.

Because Directive Principles are **non-justiciable** (`Article 37`), `Article 40` could not compel State Governments to hold regular elections or share revenue. Consequently, for over forty years after 1950, local self-government survived strictly at the mercy of State Legislatures and State executives, setting the stage for an extraordinary cycle of rural development experiments and high-level reform committees.

### Community Development Programme (1952) and National Extension Service (1953)

To translate India's rural reconstruction ambitions into operational reality during the First Five-Year Plan, Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru's government launched two ambitious state-led rural programmes:

1. **Community Development Programme (CDP), 2 October 1952:** Initiated on Gandhi's birth anniversary across 55 selected project blocks (with technical and financial support from the Ford Foundation and US Technical Cooperation Mission), the CDP sought integrated multi-sectoral rural upliftment—agriculture, animal husbandry, rural roads, health, education, and village industries—through a new territorial unit, the **Development Block**, headed by a **Block Development Officer (BDO)** assisted by Extension Officers and Village Level Workers (*Gram Sevaks*).
2. **National Extension Service (NES), 2 October 1953:** Because the intensive CDP model proved too expensive to replicate rapidly across all villages, the government introduced the less capital-intensive NES a year later as a permanent nationwide extension agency, gradually upgrading NES blocks into intensive CDP blocks.

Within four years, both programmes ran into a structural wall. Though physical infrastructure expanded modestly, **popular participation collapsed**. Designed, funded, and executed exclusively by state bureaucrats without democratically elected grassroots organs, rural citizens viewed the CDP and NES as top-down *sarkari* welfare schemes rather than community-owned endeavours. Recognising that bureaucratic administration could never ignite rural self-help, the Planning Commission's Committee on Plan Projects appointed a Study Team in **January 1957** under **Balwantrai G. Mehta** to diagnose the failures of CDP and NES—a fateful mandate that birthed modern Panchayati Raj.

### Complete Chronology of Panchayati Raj Reform Committees (1957–1988)

Between 1957 and 1988, a succession of Union and Planning Commission committees—often tested in chronological sequence and distinctive recommendations in UPSC Prelims—transformed the idea of Panchayati Raj from an administrative delivery vehicle into a constitutional third tier of government.

#### 1. Balwantrai Mehta Committee (1957) — The Architect of Panchayati Raj

Appointed in **January 1957** and submitting its landmark report in **November 1957**, the Balwantrai Mehta Study Team concluded bluntly that community development could never succeed without transferring real developmental initiative to elected local institutions. Coining the iconic phrase **"democratic decentralisation"** (a direct UPSC Prelims 2015 question!), the Committee made six foundational recommendations:

- **Three-Tier Organically Linked Structure:** Establish **Gram Panchayat** at the village level, **Panchayat Samiti** at the block level, and **Zila Parishad** at the district level, linked organically through indirect elections at the upper tiers.
- **Mode of Election:** Village-level **Gram Panchayat** members should be **directly elected** by universal adult suffrage, whereas **Panchayat Samiti** and **Zila Parishad** members should be **indirectly elected** (comprising ex-officio lower-tier chairpersons and co-opted representatives).
- **Operational Division of Roles:** Crucially, the **Panchayat Samiti (block level)** should be the **executive body** charged with all planning and developmental implementation, while the **Zila Parishad (district level)** should function merely as an **advisory, coordinating, and supervisory body**.
- **Role of the District Collector:** The **District Collector** should serve as the **Chairman of the Zila Parishad**, linking state administration with district coordination.
- **Genuine Devolution of Power and Resources:** Real transfer of authority and adequate financial resources to these bodies, with all rural welfare and developmental schemes channelled through them.
- **Future Trajectory:** Progressive further devolution of authority as local institutions matured.

The **National Development Council (NDC)** endorsed the recommendations in **January 1958**, wisely allowing states flexibility to adapt the structural pattern to local conditions rather than enforcing a rigid single mould. On **2 October 1959**, Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru formally inaugurated India's first Panchayati Raj system at **Nagaur district in Rajasthan**—making Rajasthan the **first state** to establish Panchayati Raj—followed shortly thereafter by **Andhra Pradesh** (which launched its state-wide scheme in **November 1959**, after an earlier pilot in Shadnagar block). By the mid-1960s, most Indian states had enacted Panchayati Raj legislation, though structures diverged sharply: Rajasthan adopted the classical 3-tier model, **Tamil Nadu** adopted a 2-tier system, **West Bengal** experimented with a 4-tier system (*Gram Panchayat, Anchal Panchayat, Anchalik Parishad, Zila Parishad*), and **Maharashtra and Gujarat** vested executive muscle in the Zila Parishad rather than the block Samiti.

*(Note: During the 1960s, two specialised study teams chaired by **K. Santhanam** also examined key bottlenecks: the **Santhanam Committee on PRI Finances (1963)** urged empowering panchayats with land revenue shares, matching grants, and a Panchayati Raj Finance Corporation; and the **Santhanam Committee on PRI Elections (1965)** reviewed indirect vs direct election mechanics and warned against official party interference.)*

#### 2. Ashok Mehta Committee (1977–1978) — The Second-Generation Blueprint

After initial enthusiasm (1959–64), Panchayati Raj entered a long phase of **stagnation (1965–69)** and **decline (1969–77)**. State politicians viewed empowered local leaders as electoral rivals; state bureaucracies starved PRIs of funds through parallel Centrally Sponsored Schemes (such as SFDA, MFAL, and DPAP); and state governments indefinitely postponed panchayat elections or superseded elected councils at will. To arrest this decay, the newly elected Janata Party Government appointed a high-powered committee in **December 1977** chaired by **Ashok Mehta**, which submitted its 132-recommendation report in **August 1978**. Reconceptualising PRIs from mere development-delivery agents into genuine institutions of **political and democratic self-governance**, its signature proposals included:

- **Two-Tier System:** Replace the 3-tier structure with a **2-tier system**—**Zila Parishad** at the district level and, below it, a **Mandal Panchayat** grouping clusters of villages covering a population of **15,000 to 20,000** (arguing that single tiny villages lacked economic viability).
- **District as First Point of Decentralisation:** Make the **Zila Parishad the executive body** responsible for district planning and all development administration below the state level (reversing Balwantrai Mehta's block-centric model!).
- **Official Participation of Political Parties:** Welcome **open participation of political parties at all levels of panchayat elections**—arguing that party politics already operated informally behind the scenes and formal contestation sharpened programmatic accountability.
- **Compulsory Taxation Powers:** Confer mandatory local taxation authority on PRIs so they mobilise independent financial resources rather than relying solely on state handouts.
- **Social Audit and Electoral Safeguards:** Establish regular **social audits** by a district-level agency to verify whether funds allocated to vulnerable groups reached them; prohibit arbitrary state supersession, and require **fresh elections within six months** if a PRI is ever superseded.
- **Nyaya Panchayats:** Maintain **Nyaya Panchayats** as separate judicial bodies distinct from developmental panchayats, presided over by a qualified judge.
- **Dedicated Institutional Machinery:** Entrust PRI elections to the Chief Electoral Officer in consultation with the **Election Commission of India**; appoint a **Minister for Panchayati Raj** in the State Council of Ministers; reserve SC/ST seats in proportion to population; and involve voluntary agencies in rural mobilisation.
- **Constitutional Recognition (First Formal Recommendation!):** For the **first time in an official committee report**, Ashok Mehta recommended **accordance of constitutional recognition to Panchayati Raj Institutions** to guarantee sanctity, stature, and continuous survival.

Although the Janata Government collapsed before acting on the report nationally, three states—**Karnataka (1983 Act under Ramakrishna Hegde and Abdul Nazir Sab)**, **West Bengal**, and **Andhra Pradesh (Mandal Praja Parishads under N.T. Rama Rao)**—adopted major elements of the Ashok Mehta architecture, proving that empowered PRIs could transform grassroots governance.

#### 3. Dantwala Committee (1978) and Hanumantha Rao Committee (1984) — Decentralised Planning

Before turning to the mid-1980s PRI revival panels, two Planning Commission working groups shaped the intellectual architecture of district and block planning (frequently asked in state PCS and UPSC chronological matching):

- **Dantwala Committee — Working Group on Block-Level Planning (1978):** Chaired by **Prof. M.L. Dantwala**, this group advocated block-level micro-planning to bridge village realities and district plans, recommending dedicated multi-disciplinary planning teams while favouring professional technical expertise over placing planning entirely under weak PRIs.
- **Hanumantha Rao Committee — Working Group on District Planning (1984):** Chaired by **C.H. Hanumantha Rao**, this Committee advocated comprehensive **decentralisation of functions, powers, and finances to the district level**. Crucially, however, Hanumantha Rao differed from Ashok Mehta and G.V.K. Rao on institutional control: it advocated creating **separate District Planning Bodies** headed by a **Minister or the District Collector** (with the Collector acting as coordinator of all district developmental and planning activities), keeping district planning somewhat insulated from PRIs until local capacity matured.

#### 4. G.V.K. Rao Committee (1985) — "Grass Without Roots"

Appointed by the **Planning Commission in 1985** to review existing *Administrative Arrangements for Rural Development and Poverty Alleviation Programmes (CAARD)*, the **G.V.K. Rao Committee** delivered a blistering diagnosis of bureaucratic centralisation. Observing that the proliferation of vertical line-department agencies and District Rural Development Agencies (DRDAs) had completely sidelined elected local bodies, the Committee famously described Panchayati Raj as **"grass without roots."** Reversing the Collector-centric slant of Hanumantha Rao (1984), G.V.K. Rao proposed:

- **Revitalised Zila Parishad as Principal Planning Agency:** Make the **Zila Parishad** the pivotal democratic body managing all district development programmes, supported by planning units at both district and block levels.
- **District Development Commissioner (DDC):** Create a senior IAS post of **District Development Commissioner (DDC)** to serve as the **Chief Executive Officer of the Zila Parishad**, cleanly separating developmental administration (under DDC + Zila Parishad) from regulatory and law-and-order administration (under the District Collector).
- **Regular Elections:** Mandate periodic, timely elections to PRIs—noting with alarm that 11 states had left panchayat polls overdue for years.

#### 5. L.M. Singhvi Committee (1986) — Constitutionalisation and the Gram Sabha

In **1986**, Prime Minister **Rajiv Gandhi's** government appointed a committee under eminent jurist **Dr. L.M. Singhvi** on **"Revitalisation of Panchayati Raj Institutions for Democracy and Development."** The Singhvi Committee furnished the decisive legal and philosophical charter that directly anchored the 73rd Amendment:

- **Constitutional Recognition via a New Chapter:** PRIs must be **constitutionally recognised, protected, and preserved** by inserting a dedicated **new Chapter into the Constitution of India**, guaranteeing regular, free, and fair elections.
- **Gram Sabha as Direct Democracy:** Emphasised the primacy of the **Gram Sabha** as the *"embodiment of direct democracy"* at the village base, recommending village reorganisation where necessary to make Gram Panchayats economically and administratively viable.
- **Nyaya Panchayats and Judicial Tribunals:** Recommended establishing **Nyaya Panchayats** for clusters of villages and setting up **independent judicial tribunals** in each state exclusively to adjudicate disputes relating to PRI elections, dissolution, and functioning.
- **Enhanced Financial Resources:** Urged stronger financial devolution so villages were not reduced to supplicants of state secretariats.

#### 6. P.K. Thungon Sub-Committee (1988) and V.N. Gadgil Committee (1988) — The Direct Blueprint

In **1988**, two pivotal political panels translated the Singhvi vision into concrete constitutional clauses:

- **P.K. Thungon Committee (1988):** A sub-committee of the Consultative Committee of Parliament (attached to the Ministry of Personnel), chaired by **P.K. Thungon**, examined district-level political and administrative structures. It recommended: (1) **constitutional recognition** for PRIs; (2) a **three-tier structure** with Zila Parishad as the pivot of district planning; (3) a **fixed five-year tenure** with supersession capped at six months; (4) **reservations for SCs, STs, and women** across all three tiers; (5) a **State Finance Commission** in every state; (6) a **Planning and Coordination Committee** at the state level chaired by the Planning Minister with Zila Parishad presidents as members; and (7) making the **District Collector the CEO of the Zila Parishad**.
- **V.N. Gadgil Committee (1988) — Committee on Policy and Programmes:** Constituted by the Indian National Congress under **V.N. Gadgil** to determine *"how best Panchayati Raj institutions could be made effective,"* this report served as the **direct working blueprint for the 64th Amendment Bill and ultimately the 73rd Amendment Act**. Its eight sharp recommendations read almost word-for-word like Part IX: (1) **constitutional status** for PRIs; (2) a **three-tier system** (village, block, district); (3) a **fixed five-year term**; (4) **direct elections to ALL three tiers**; (5) **reservations for SCs, STs, and women**; (6) empowerment to prepare and implement socio-economic development plans based on a **dedicated constitutional list of subjects**; (7) power to **levy, collect, and appropriate local taxes**; and (8) constitution of a **State Finance Commission (SFC)** and a **State Election Commission (SEC)**.

| **P.K. Thungon (1988)** | Parliamentary Consultative Sub-Committee | **3-tier** | **Zila Parishad** (Collector as CEO) | Constitutional status; **fixed 5-year term**; max 6-month supersession; SC/ST/women quota; **SFC**; State Planning & Coordination Committee |
| **V.N. Gadgil (1988)** | INC Policy Committee | **3-tier** | Elected 3-tier PRIs | **Direct blueprint for 73rd Amendment**: **direct elections to ALL 3 tiers**, 5-year term, SC/ST/women quota, dedicated subject list, taxing powers, **SFC & SEC** |

> **Committee Memory Anchors for Prelims Elimination:**
> - **Two Mehtas:** **Balwantrai (1957)** = **3-tier**, **Panchayat Samiti** executive, **no parties**; **Ashok (1977)** = **2-tier** (**Mandal Panchayat**), **Zila Parishad** executive, **official party participation**, **first constitutional status call**.
> - **Two Raos (1984 vs 1985):** **Hanumantha Rao (1984)** = District Planning under **Collector/Minister** (separate from PRIs); **G.V.K. Rao (1985)** = **"grass without roots"** + **DDC** (separated development from Collector!).
> - **Singhvi (1986) vs Gadgil (1988):** **Singhvi** championed the **Gram Sabha** + **judicial tribunals** + new constitutional chapter; **Gadgil** gave the exact **73rd Amendment formula** (**direct polls at all 3 tiers + SEC + SFC + subject list**).

### The Legislative Journey to Constitutionalisation (1989–1993)

Translating committee consensus into constitutional text took three separate Union governments and four turbulent years of federal negotiation:

1. **Rajiv Gandhi's 64th & 65th Constitutional Amendment Bills (1989):** Drawing directly on the Gadgil report, Rajiv Gandhi's government introduced the **64th Constitutional Amendment Bill** (Panchayats, July 1989) and the **65th Constitutional Amendment Bill** (Municipalities, August 1989). Both Bills sailed through the Lok Sabha in **August 1989**, but were **defeated in the Rajya Sabha in October 1989** (falling short by a narrow margin). Opposition parties and non-Congress State Governments resisted the Bills fiercely, alleging that direct Union-to-Panchayat funding and mandatory uniform mandates sought to **centralise power by bypassing State Governments**, violating Entry 5 of the State List and India's federal balance.
2. **V.P. Singh's 74th Constitutional Amendment Bill (1990):** Following a two-day Chief Ministers' Conference chaired by Prime Minister V.P. Singh in June 1990, the National Front Government introduced a combined **74th Amendment Bill** in the Lok Sabha in **September 1990** covering both rural and urban bodies. Before Parliament could vote on it, the government fell in November 1990 and the Bill **lapsed upon the dissolution of the 9th Lok Sabha**.
3. **P.V. Narasimha Rao's 73rd & 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts (1992–1993):** Returning to office in 1991, Prime Minister P.V. Narasimha Rao stripped out the controversial centralising clauses—notably restoring clear State-level legislative discretion on devolving functions and routing Union grants through the State Consolidated Fund—and introduced the **72nd (Panchayats)** and **73rd (Municipalities) Amendment Bills** in **September 1991**. Both Bills underwent exhaustive cross-party scrutiny by Joint Parliamentary Committees (the Panchayats JPC chaired by **Nathuram Mirdha**; the Municipalities JPC chaired by **K.P. Singh Deo**). Renumbered as the **73rd** and **74th Constitutional Amendment Acts, 1992**, they passed the Lok Sabha on **22 December 1992** and the Rajya Sabha on **23 December 1992**. Because they modified federal provisions (including `Article 280`), they required ratification by at least **half of the State Legislatures** under `Article 368(2)`; after **17 State Assemblies** ratified them, President Shankar Dayal Sharma granted assent on **20 April 1993**.
   - The **`73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992`** came into force on **`24 April 1993`**—commemorated annually since **2010** as **National Panchayati Raj Day**. **Karnataka** became the first state to enact a conforming Panchayat Act (May 1993), and **Madhya Pradesh** became the first state to hold Panchayat elections under the new constitutional regime (1994).
   - The **`74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992`** came into force on **`1 June 1993`**.

> [!mains]
> **Evolutionary Insight for GS-2 Answers:** Do not treat the 73rd and 74th Amendments merely as technical statutory upgrades. Frame them as a **constitutional settlement of two historical tensions**: (1) resolving the **Gandhi–Ambedkar dilemma** by democratising village republics through mandatory SC/ST and women's quotas; and (2) resolving the **Centre–State federal friction** (which doomed the 1989 Bills) by separating **compulsory democratic minimums** (regular elections, 5-year tenure, reservations, SEC, SFC) from **voluntary devolution space** left to State Legislatures—a compromise that secured federal passage in 1992, yet ironically created the discretionary `"may"` loophole that constrains the 3Fs today.

## 2. The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 — Panchayats (Part IX and Eleventh Schedule)

The **`73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992`** gave practical constitutional teeth to **`Article 40`** (Directive Principle of State Policy) by inserting a brand-new **Part IX** titled *"The Panchayats"* (**`Articles 243` to `243O`**) and adding the **`Eleventh Schedule`** enumerating **29 functional items** (`Article 243G`). It also amended **`Article 280(3)`** by inserting clause **`(bb)`**, obliging the Union Finance Commission to recommend measures to augment a State's Consolidated Fund to supplement Panchayat resources on the basis of State Finance Commission recommendations. Crucially, the Act converted Panchayat governance from a **non-justiciable** State discretion into a **justiciable constitutional obligation**: State Governments are constitutionally duty-bound to adopt the mandatory framework of Part IX, irrespective of political regime changes.

```flow
# Three-Tier Panchayati Raj Institutional Architecture (Part IX, Articles 243–243O)
Gram Sabha (Art. 243A) | Foundation of Direct Democracy: ALL Registered Voters in Village Electoral Roll (Powers fixed by State Law)
Gram Panchayat (Village Tier — Art. 243B) | ALL Ward Members Directly Elected · Sarpanch/Chairperson Elected as State Legislature Decides
Panchayat Samiti (Intermediate / Block Tier — Art. 243B) | Optional ONLY if State Population ≤ 20 Lakh · Members Directly Elected · Chairperson Indirectly Elected || Zila Parishad (District Tier — Art. 243B) | Mandatory Across All States · Members Directly Elected · Chairperson Indirectly Elected
Constitutional Safeguards & Oversight | State Election Commission (Art. 243K: Elections) · State Finance Commission (Art. 243I: 5-Yr Fiscal Devolution) · Eleventh Schedule (Art. 243G: 29 Functional Items)
```

### Deep Analysis of Key Provisions (Articles 243A to 243O)

#### 1. The Gram Sabha (`Articles 243(b)` and `243A`)

The **Gram Sabha** is the bedrock of direct democracy in the Indian rural polity. Crucially, `Article 243(b)` defines a Gram Sabha precisely as **a body consisting of persons registered in the electoral rolls relating to a village comprised within the area of Panchayat at the village level**. Aspirants must avoid two recurrent Prelims traps here: first, membership is strictly restricted to **registered voters** on the electoral roll—*not* all adult residents above 18 years; second, the Constitution deliberately does **not** enumerate the specific powers of the Gram Sabha, declaring under `Article 243A` that it *"may exercise such powers and perform such functions at the village level as the Legislature of a State may, by law, provide."* Similarly, what constitutes a "village" (a single revenue village or a cluster of hamlets) is specified by the **Governor** via public notification (`Article 243(g)`). Across states, Gram Sabhas typically approve village development budgets, verify beneficiary lists for welfare schemes (such as MGNREGA and PMAY-G), conduct social audits, and hold the elected Gram Panchayat accountable—making it the rural counterpart of a direct town-hall legislature.

#### 2. Three-Tier Structure (`Article 243B`)

To eliminate the chaotic diversity of 2-tier, 3-tier, and 4-tier structures that prevailed between 1959 and 1992, `Article 243B(1)` mandates a uniform **three-tier system** of Panchayats in every State: at the **village** (Gram Panchayat), **intermediate** (Panchayat Samiti / Mandal Parishad / Taluka Panchayat / Block Development Council), and **district** (Zila Parishad) levels. However, `Article 243B(2)` carves out one sensible population exception: **a State having a population not exceeding 20 lakh may NOT constitute Panchayats at the intermediate level** (exercised by smaller states such as **Goa, Sikkim, and Manipur**). Notice the exact constitutional wording: the threshold is **"not exceeding 20 lakh"** (i.e., `≤ 20 lakh`), and the intermediate tier is *optional* (not prohibited) for such states.

#### 3. Composition, Elections and Voting Rights (`Article 243C`)

`Article 243C` resolves the longstanding debate between direct and indirect democracy through an intricate constitutional design:

- **Direct Election of ALL Members:** Every seat in a Panchayat at **all three levels** (village, intermediate, and district) MUST be filled by **direct election** from territorial constituencies within the Panchayat area, preserving a broadly uniform ratio between population and seats throughout the State.
- **Election of Chairpersons:** The Chairperson of Panchayats at the **intermediate and district levels** MUST be elected **indirectly**—by and from amongst the directly elected members thereof (`Article 243C(5)(b)`). By contrast, the mode of electing the **Chairperson of a Village Panchayat (Sarpanch / Pradhan / Mukhiya)** is left entirely to the **State Legislature** (`Article 243C(5)(a)`)—hence states such as Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu, and Uttar Pradesh opt for direct Sarpanch elections, whereas states like West Bengal, Kerala, and Maharashtra (varied periodically) opt for indirect election by ward members.
- **Ex-Officio Representation of Lower Tiers and Legislators (`Article 243C(3)`):** State Legislatures *may* (voluntarily) provide for representation of: (a) Village Panchayat chairpersons in the Intermediate Panchayat (or District Panchayat where no intermediate tier exists); (b) Intermediate Panchayat chairpersons in the District Panchayat; (c) **MPs (Lok Sabha) and MLAs** representing constituencies wholly or partly within the Panchayat area; and (d) **Rajya Sabha MPs and State MLCs** who are registered as electors within the Panchayat area.
- **Crucial Voting Right Nuance (`Article 243C(4)`):** In a classic high-frequency UPSC distinction, **`Article 243C(4)`** grants the **Chairperson AND all other members of a Panchayat—whether chosen by direct election OR ex-officio (MPs, MLAs, MLCs, lower-tier chairpersons)—the RIGHT TO VOTE in meetings of the Panchayat**! The sole exception lies in `Article 243C(5)` / State statutes regarding voting on the election or removal (no-confidence motion) of the intermediate/district Chairperson, which is confined to elected members. Contrast this sharply with **nominated municipal experts under `Article 243R(2)(a)(i)`**, who are constitutionally barred from voting in municipal meetings!

#### 4. Reservations, Sunset Clause and the OBC Triple Test (`Article 243D`)

`Article 243D` represents one of the boldest affirmative-action experiments in global constitutional history, structurally reshaping rural social power:

- **SC and ST Reservation (`Art. 243D(1)` & `(4)`):** Mandatory reservation of seats for **Scheduled Castes (SCs)** and **Scheduled Tribes (STs)** in every Panchayat at **all three levels**, strictly in **proportion to their population** in that Panchayat area. Similarly, offices of **Chairpersons** at village, intermediate, and district levels are reserved for SCs and STs in proportion to their state-wide population. Reserved seats and Chairperson offices may be **allotted by rotation** across different constituencies/Panchayats.
- **Women's Reservation (`Art. 243D(2)–(4)`):** **Not less than one-third (`≥ 33.3%`)** of the total seats filled by direct election—*including the number of seats reserved for women belonging to SCs and STs*—MUST be reserved for women at every tier. Likewise, **not less than one-third** of the total number of **Chairperson offices** at each level across the State MUST be reserved for women. Because this is a constitutional *floor* ("not less than"), **21 States and 2 Union Territories** (pioneered by **Bihar in 2006**, followed by Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, Odisha, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, Assam, West Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Punjab, Tripura, Sikkim, and Puducherry/J&K/Ladakh frameworks) have voluntarily raised women's reservation in PRIs to **50 per cent**, bringing **over 15 lakh Women Elected Representatives (~46.6% of India's ~31–32 lakh PRI representatives)** into rural governance!
- **Crucial Sunset Clause Trap (`Article 243D(5)`):** Under `Article 243D(5)`, the reservation of seats and Chairperson offices for **SCs and STs** ceases to have effect upon the expiration of the period specified in **`Article 334`** (currently extended to **25 January 2030** via the `104th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019`). **HOWEVER, women's reservation under `Article 243D` is NOT linked to `Article 334`—it has NO constitutional sunset clause and is permanent!**
- **83rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2000 (`Article 243M(3A)`):** Because **Arunachal Pradesh** is inhabited overwhelmingly by indigenous Scheduled Tribes with virtually no Scheduled Caste population, Parliament enacted the `83rd Amendment Act, 2000`, inserting `Article 243M(3A)` to **exempt Arunachal Pradesh specifically from mandatory SC reservation** in Panchayats.
- **Backward Classes (OBC) Reservation (`Article 243D(6)`):** Unlike SC/ST and women's quotas, reservation for **Backward Classes (OBCs)** in seats or Chairperson offices is **strictly voluntary**—left to State Legislatures. Over the past decade, however, the Supreme Court has subjected state OBC quotas in both PRIs and ULBs to strict constitutional discipline via the **Triple Test**.

> [!case]
> **The OBC Reservation Triple Test — *Dr. K. Krishna Murthy* (2010), *Vikas Kishanrao Gawali* (2021) & *Suresh Mahajan* (2022)**
> - **Constitutional Distinction (*Dr. K. Krishna Murthy v. Union of India*, 5-Judge Constitution Bench, 11 May 2010):** Upholding `Articles 243D(6)` and `243T(6)`, the Court clarified that reservations in local self-government (`Part IX/IXA`) pursue **political representation**, NOT educational or employment upliftment (`Articles 15(4)/16(4)`). Therefore, social/educational backwardness lists cannot be mechanically copied into local polls; local political backwardness must be empirically established, and aggregate vertical reservation (`SC + ST + OBC`) **cannot exceed 50 per cent** (except in Fifth Schedule tribal areas where PESA permits `≥50%` ST quota).
> - **The Mandatory Triple Test (*Vikas Kishanrao Gawali v. State of Maharashtra*, 3-Judge Bench, 4 March 2021):** Reading down Section 12(2)(c) of the Maharashtra Zilla Parishads and Panchayat Samitis Act, 1961 (which gave a flat 27% OBC quota exceeding the 50% ceiling in several districts), Justice A.M. Khanwilkar's bench laid down **three mandatory pre-conditions** before any State can notify OBC quotas in PRIs or ULBs:
>   1. **Set up a dedicated Commission** to conduct a contemporaneous, rigorous empirical inquiry into the nature and implications of **political backwardness** qua local bodies within the State;
>   2. **Specify the proportion of reservation required local-body-wise** in light of the Commission's recommendations (preventing blanket state-wide percentages); and
>   3. **Ensure aggregate reservation for SCs, STs, and OBCs does NOT exceed 50 per cent** of total seats in any local body.
> - **No Election Delays (*Suresh Mahajan v. State of Madhya Pradesh*, 10 May 2022):** When states delayed over 23,000 local body elections citing incomplete OBC Triple Test commissions, the Supreme Court issued a stern nationwide mandamus: **constitutional election timelines (`Arts. 243E/243U`) are inviolable**. If a State fails to complete the Triple Test before elections fall due, SECs MUST notify polls immediately by treating proposed OBC seats as **general/open category seats**—delaying polls indefinitely is *"bordering on breakdown of rule of law."*

#### 5. Duration, Premature Dissolution and Remainder Term (`Article 243E`)

`Article 243E` guarantees institutional continuity so State executives can no longer keep Panchayats dissolved for decades:

- **Fixed Five-Year Term:** Every Panchayat continues for **five years from the date appointed for its first meeting and no longer**—unless sooner dissolved under applicable State law (`Art. 243E(1)`). Furthermore, no amendment of any State law can prematurely dissolve an existing Panchayat before its 5-year tenure ends (`Art. 243E(2)`).
- **Mandatory Election Timelines (`Art. 243E(3)`):** Elections to constitute a new Panchayat MUST be completed: (a) **before the expiry of its five-year duration**; OR (b) in case of premature dissolution, **before the expiration of six months from the date of its dissolution**.
- **The Classic "Remainder Term" Trap (`Art. 243E(3)` Proviso & `Art. 243E(4)` — Asked in UPSC Prelims 2016!):** If a Panchayat is dissolved prematurely, the **reconstituted Panchayat does NOT get a fresh full five-year term**—it continues **ONLY for the REMAINDER of the period** for which the dissolved Panchayat would have continued! Moreover, **where the remainder of the period is LESS THAN SIX MONTHS, it is NOT necessary to hold any election** to reconstitute the Panchayat for that brief remaining spell. (Contrast this sharply with the Lok Sabha or State Legislative Assembly, where premature dissolution always yields a fresh full 5-year House!)

#### 6. Disqualifications and Minimum Age (`Article 243F`)

Under `Article 243F(1)`, a person is disqualified from being chosen as, or continuing as, a Panchayat member if disqualified: (a) under any law for the time being in force for elections to the **State Legislature**, subject to one vital constitutional relaxation—**no person shall be disqualified on the ground of being less than 25 years of age if they have attained the age of 21 years**; OR (b) under any additional law enacted by the **State Legislature** (e.g., two-child norms upheld in *Javed v. State of Haryana*, 2003, or minimum educational/functional toilet norms upheld in *Rajbala v. State of Haryana*, 2015—though the latter drew sharp Mains criticism for disenfranchising poor Dalit and women aspirants). All questions of disqualification are referred for final decision to **such authority and in such manner as the State Legislature may by law determine** (`Art. 243F(2)`—*not* automatically the Governor or SEC unless the State law designates them!).

#### 7. State Finance Commission (`Article 243I`) and Audit (`Article 243J`)

To break local bodies' humiliating dependency on ad hoc State executive doles, **`Article 243I`** mandates that the **Governor** of every State shall constitute a **State Finance Commission (SFC)** within one year of the 73rd Amendment's commencement and thereafter **at the expiration of every fifth year** to review the financial position of Panchayats (and Municipalities under `Article 243Y`) and recommend:

1. **Distribution of Net Proceeds of State Taxes:** Principles governing the sharing of net proceeds of taxes, duties, tolls, and fees leviable by the State between the State Government and Panchayats, and allocation among Panchayats at all levels;
2. **Assigned Local Taxes:** Determination of taxes, duties, tolls, and fees that may be assigned to, or appropriated by, Panchayats;
3. **Grants-in-Aid:** Principles governing grants-in-aid to Panchayats from the **Consolidated Fund of the State**;
4. **Fiscal Strengthening Measures:** Measures needed to improve the overall financial health of Panchayats; and
5. **Any Other Matter** referred by the Governor in the interests of sound Panchayat finance.

The **State Legislature** prescribes the SFC's composition, member qualifications, and selection procedure (`Art. 243I(2)`), while the **Governor** must lay every SFC recommendation together with an **Explanatory Memorandum as to the Action Taken (ATR)** before the State Legislature (`Art. 243I(4)`). Under **`Article 243J`**, the State Legislature legislates on the maintenance and audit of Panchayat accounts (typically audited by State Local Fund Audit Departments under technical guidance from the CAG).

#### 8. State Election Commission (`Article 243K`) and Bar on Courts (`Article 243O`)

To guarantee free, fair, and timely grassroots elections insulated from State ruling-party interference, **`Article 243K`** vests the *"superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls for, and the conduct of, all elections to the Panchayats"* (and Municipalities under `Article 243ZA`) in an independent **State Election Commission (SEC)** consisting of a single **State Election Commissioner** appointed by the **Governor**. Note that the **Election Commission of India (`Article 324`) has ZERO jurisdiction over Panchayat or Municipal elections**—a perennial UPSC Prelims trap!

To protect the SEC's independence, `Article 243K(2)` incorporates two ironclad constitutional shields mirroring the Chief Election Commissioner:
1. **Removal ONLY Like a High Court Judge:** Even though the **Governor appoints** the State Election Commissioner and determines conditions of service and tenure (subject to State law), the Governor **CANNOT remove** the Commissioner! A State Election Commissioner can be removed from office **only in like manner and on the like grounds as a Judge of a High Court**—meaning by an order of the **President of India** following a Parliamentary address passed by special majority in both Houses on proved misbehaviour or incapacity!
2. **No Adverse Alteration of Service Conditions:** Conditions of service of a State Election Commissioner cannot be varied to their disadvantage after appointment. Additionally, the Governor must provide requisite election staff when requested by the SEC (`Art. 243K(3)`).

> [!case]
> **Protecting SEC Independence — *State of Goa v. Fouziya Imtiaz Sheikh* (12 March 2021)**
> When the Goa Government gave additional charge of State Election Commissioner to the sitting State Law Secretary (a serving government bureaucrat), the Supreme Court (3-Judge Bench led by Justice R.F. Nariman) delivered a landmark constitutional directive under `Articles 243K` and `243ZA`: **holding additional charge of SEC by a serving government officer is a "mockery of the Constitution."** The Court mandated across ALL States and Union Territories that **State Election Commissioners MUST be strictly independent persons who hold NO post or office under the Central or State Government**, reaffirming that SEC autonomy sits on par with the ECI under `Article 324`.

Finally, **`Article 243O`** shields the electoral calendar from dilatory litigation by imposing a dual bar on courts: (a) the validity of any law relating to **delimitation of constituencies** or **allotment of seats** to such constituencies **cannot be called in question in any court**; and (b) no Panchayat election can be questioned **except by an election petition** presented to such authority and in such manner as provided by State law.

### Exhaustive Compulsory vs Voluntary Provisions Matrix

One of the highest-yielding UPSC Prelims topics in Indian Polity is distinguishing **Compulsory (Mandatory)** provisions—which EVERY State conforming law *must* incorporate—from **Voluntary (Discretionary)** provisions—which States *may* adopt depending on local political, social, and administrative context.

```compare
# Compulsory vs Voluntary Provisions of the 73rd Amendment Act, 1992
Compulsory (Mandatory for All States) | Voluntary (Left to State Legislature Discretion)
Gram Sabha :: Organisation of Gram Sabha in a village or group of villages (Art. 243A) | Defining specific powers, functions & quorum of the Gram Sabha
Tier Structure :: Three-tier system: Village, Intermediate, District (2-tier allowed ONLY if State pop. ≤ 20 lakh — Art. 243B) | Delimitation of panchayat areas and naming of tiers
Direct vs Indirect Polls :: Direct election to ALL seats across all 3 tiers; INDIRECT election for Intermediate & District Chairpersons (Art. 243C) | Mode of election (direct OR indirect) of Village Panchayat Chairperson (Sarpanch/Pradhan)
Legislator Representation :: Ex-officio members, if included, hold right to vote in meetings (Art. 243C(4)) | Giving representation to MPs, MLAs, MLCs & lower-tier Chairpersons in upper tiers (Art. 243C(3))
Reservations :: Proportional SC/ST & ≥ 1/3rd Women reservation in ALL seats and Chairperson posts (Art. 243D) | OBC reservation (Art. 243D(6), subject to Triple Test); raising Women's quota above 1/3rd (e.g., to 50%)
```

### Exempted States and Areas (Article 243M) and Extension to UTs (Article 243L)

Recognising India's asymmetric federal design and the need to preserve indigenous tribal self-governing councils, **`Article 243M`** explicitly **exempts** the following territories from the automatic operation of Part IX:

1. **Three North-Eastern States:** **Nagaland, Meghalaya, and Mizoram** (which possess deeply entrenched traditional village councils and Sixth Schedule autonomous councils). *(Note: **Assam, Tripura, and Manipur** ARE covered by Part IX in their non-exempted plain areas—only their Sixth Schedule or Manipur Hill Areas are excluded!)*
2. **Scheduled Areas and Tribal Areas (`Article 244`):** Both **Fifth Schedule** (Scheduled Areas across 10 states) and **Sixth Schedule** (Tribal Areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram).
3. **Hill Areas of Manipur:** Areas for which **District Councils** exist under the Manipur (Village Authorities in Hill Areas) Act / District Councils Act.
4. **Darjeeling District (West Bengal):** The district-level Panchayat provisions do **not** apply to the hill areas of Darjeeling district for which the **Darjeeling Gorkha Hill Council** (now Gorkhaland Territorial Administration) exists—nor can anything in Part IX affect its powers and functions (`Art. 243M(3)`).
5. **Partial Exemption for Arunachal Pradesh (`Article 243M(3A)`):** Added by the **`83rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2000`**, exempting Arunachal Pradesh **only from SC reservation** under `Article 243D` (the rest of Part IX applies!).

Crucially, `Article 243M(4)` provides **two distinct opt-in mechanisms**:
- **State Legislative Opt-in (`Art. 243M(4)(a)`):** The Legislative Assemblies of **Nagaland, Meghalaya, or Mizoram** may themselves choose to extend Part IX to their State by passing a resolution supported by a **majority of total membership AND not less than two-thirds of members present and voting** (special majority).
- **Parliamentary Extension (`Art. 243M(4)(b)`):** **Parliament may, by ordinary law**, extend Part IX to the **Scheduled Areas (Fifth Schedule)** and **Tribal Areas (Sixth Schedule)** subject to specified exceptions and modifications—and **`Article 243M(4)(b)` expressly declares that NO such law shall be deemed to be a constitutional amendment under `Article 368`**! Using this exact constitutional gateway, Parliament enacted the **PESA Act, 1996** for Fifth Schedule areas (while Sixth Schedule areas remain outside PESA).

Meanwhile, under **`Article 243L`**, Part IX applies to **Union Territories**, subject to such exceptions and modifications as the **President of India** may specify by public notification (note that NCT of Delhi suspended rural panchayats in 1990 and Chandigarh has no rural panchayats following urbanisation).

> [!prelims]
> **Seven Classic UPSC Prelims Traps on the 73rd Amendment (`Part IX`):**
> 1. **Fundamental Objective (UPSC Prelims 2015):** The core purpose of Panchayati Raj is **Democratic Decentralisation** (and people's participation in development—NOT merely administrative efficiency or financial mobilisation!).
> 2. **Minimum Age (UPSC Prelims 2016):** Minimum age to contest Panchayat elections is **21 years** (`Art. 243F`), NOT 25 years!
> 3. **Premature Dissolution (UPSC Prelims 2016):** A reconstituted Panchayat serves **ONLY the remainder of the term**, NOT a fresh 5 years—and **no election** is needed if remainder `< 6 months` (`Art. 243E`).
> 4. **Gram Sabha Membership:** Consists strictly of **registered voters** in the village electoral roll (`Art. 243(b)`), NOT all adult residents; its powers are defined by the **State Legislature** (`Art. 243A`), NOT the Constitution!
> 5. **SEC Appointment vs Removal:** Appointed by the **Governor**, yet removed **ONLY like a High Court Judge** (by the **President** via Parliamentary address—`Art. 243K(2)`). The ECI has NO role!
> 6. **Women's Quota Sunset:** SC/ST quota ceases with `Art. 334` (**25 Jan 2030**), whereas **women's 1/3rd quota has NO sunset clause** (`Art. 243D(5)`).
> 7. **Exempted States Trap:** **Nagaland, Meghalaya, Mizoram** are wholly exempt; **Arunachal Pradesh** is exempt **ONLY from SC quota** (`83rd Amend 2000`); **Parliament's extension** under `Art. 243M(4)(b)` is an **ordinary law**, NOT an `Art. 368` amendment!

## 3. The PESA Act, 1996 — Tribal Self-Rule in Fifth Schedule Areas

While the 73rd Amendment democratised rural India across general areas, Parliament deliberately excluded **Fifth Schedule (Scheduled Areas)** and **Sixth Schedule (Tribal Areas)** under **`Article 243M(1)`**. Tribal communities across central and peninsular India possess distinct customary laws, communal resource-sharing ethics (*jal, jangal, zameen*), and consensus-oriented dispute resolution systems that could easily be shattered by imposing uniform revenue-village panchayat templates from above. Yet leaving Scheduled Areas permanently outside Part IX would deny Adivasi citizens the constitutional protection of grassroots self-government.

To resolve this delicate balance, the Union Government constituted a high-powered committee of Members of Parliament and experts in **1994** under **Dileep Singh Bhuria**—the **Bhuria Committee (1994–95)**—tasked with harmonising Part IX with tribal traditions. Submitting its landmark report in **January 1995**, the Bhuria Committee recommended extending Part IX to Fifth Schedule areas with radical modifications that elevated the traditional hamlet-level **Gram Sabha** into a sovereign participatory authority rather than a subordinate administrative appendage. Acting directly on these recommendations under **`Article 243M(4)(b)`** (an ordinary Act of Parliament, explicitly *not* an Article 368 amendment), Parliament enacted **The Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA)**, which received Presidential assent and came into force on **`24 December 1996`**.

### Territorial Scope: The Ten Fifth Schedule States

PESA applies **exclusively to Fifth Schedule Areas** declared under `Article 244(1)` of the Constitution—it does **NOT** apply to Sixth Schedule Tribal Areas (Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram, which are governed by Autonomous District Councils) nor to non-Scheduled rural areas. At present, Fifth Schedule Areas span **10 Indian States**: **Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan, and Telangana**. Crucially, PESA does not automatically cover every district inside these ten states; it operates strictly within notified Scheduled Areas encompassing **45 fully covered districts and 63 partially covered districts**—covering **663 blocks, 22,141 Gram Panchayats, and 77,564 villages** across India (Ministry of Panchayati Raj / PIB data, 2025–26).

### Philosophy and Architecture of Section 4: Gram Sabha Sovereignty

What makes PESA uniquely transformative—often hailed as a "Constitution within the Constitution" for Adivasi self-governance—is **Section 4**, which binds every State Legislature with a strict negative mandate: *no State law relating to Panchayats in Scheduled Areas shall be inconsistent with the mandatory features of Section 4*. Whereas general Part IX (`Article 243A`) leaves the powers of the Gram Sabha entirely to State legislative discretion, PESA **statutorily mandates** a formidable charter of rights directly into the Gram Sabha.

#### Redefining the "Village" and Customary Harmony (`Section 4(a)–(c)`)

- **Harmony with Tribal Customs (`Sec. 4(a)`):** State Panchayat legislation in Scheduled Areas MUST be in consonance with **customary law, social and religious practices, and traditional management practices of community resources**.
- **Habitation/Hamlet-Level Village (`Sec. 4(b)`):** Unlike general Part IX (where a revenue village or large cluster of distant settlements forms one Panchayat), PESA defines a *"village"* ordinarily as a **habitation or a group of habitations, or a hamlet or a group of hamlets** comprising a community and managing its affairs in accordance with traditions and customs. This guarantees face-to-face direct democracy within cohesive tribal communities.
- **Universal Voter Gram Sabha (`Sec. 4(c)`):** Every such hamlet/habitation has its own **Gram Sabha** consisting of all persons whose names are included in the electoral rolls for the village-level Panchayat.

#### Mandatory Powers of the Gram Sabha and Panchayats (`Section 4(d)–(m)`)

PESA distributes an extraordinary suite of governance, developmental, land, mineral, forest, and social-regulatory powers—vesting core direct powers exclusively in the **Gram Sabha**, and collaborative powers in the **Gram Sabha or Panchayats at the appropriate level**:

1. **Safeguarding Cultural Identity and Customary Justice (`Sec. 4(d)`):** Every Gram Sabha is declared competent to **safeguard and preserve the traditions and customs of the people, their cultural identity, community resources, and the customary mode of dispute resolution**.
2. **Plan Approval and Beneficiary Selection (`Sec. 4(e)`):** Every Gram Sabha MUST: (i) **approve plans, programmes, and projects for social and economic development** before the Village Panchayat takes them up for implementation; and (ii) **identify or select beneficiaries** under poverty alleviation and welfare programmes.
3. **Financial Accountability via Utilisation Certificates (`Sec. 4(f)`):** Every Village Panchayat is legally required to obtain a **certification of utilisation of funds** from the Gram Sabha for all plans, programmes, and projects executed under `Section 4(e)`—making the Panchayat directly accountable to the people's assembly.
4. **Mandatory Consultation Before Land Acquisition and Rehabilitation (`Sec. 4(i)`):** The Gram Sabha or the Panchayats at the appropriate level MUST be **consulted before making the acquisition of land** in Scheduled Areas for development projects, AND **before re-settling or rehabilitating persons affected by such projects**. Crucially, the actual planning and implementation of such projects in Scheduled Areas must be coordinated at the State level. *(Note the critical statutory evolution: while PESA `Section 4(i)` mandates **"consultation"**, **Section 41 of the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement (LARR) Act, 2013** subsequently upgraded this protection in Scheduled Areas to **prior CONSENT of the concerned Gram Sabha, Panchayats, or Autonomous District Councils**, establishing land acquisition in Scheduled Areas strictly as a "demonstrable last resort"!)*
5. **Planning and Management of Minor Water Bodies (`Sec. 4(j)`):** Entrusted exclusively to the Panchayats at the appropriate level.
6. **Prior Recommendation for Minor Minerals (`Sec. 4(k)–(l)`):** The **prior recommendation** of the Gram Sabha or the Panchayats at the appropriate level is **mandatory** for: (i) granting a **prospecting licence or mining lease for MINOR MINERALS** (`Sec. 4(k)`); and (ii) granting concessions for the exploitation of **minor minerals by auction** (`Sec. 4(l)`). *(Classic UPSC Prelims trap: PESA's mandatory mining gatekeeping covers **MINOR MINERALS ONLY**—such as sand, gravel, stone, murrum, and building stone—and does **NOT** legally extend to **major minerals** like coal, bauxite, iron ore, or uranium, which are regulated by the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957!)*
7. **Seven Core Endowments to End Tribal Exploitation (`Sec. 4(m)`):** While devolving powers to enable Panchayats to function as institutions of self-government, every State Legislature MUST specifically endow the Panchayats at the appropriate level and the Gram Sabha with:
   - **`Sec. 4(m)(i)` — Intoxicants:** Power to enforce **prohibition** or to **regulate or restrict the sale and consumption of any intoxicant**;
   - **`Sec. 4(m)(ii)` — Ownership of Minor Forest Produce (MFP):** Complete **ownership of Minor Forest Produce** (such as bamboo, *tendu* leaves, *mahua* flowers, *tamarind*, gum, honey, and lac—later reinforced by the Forest Rights Act, 2006);
   - **`Sec. 4(m)(iii)` — Tribal Land Alienation:** Power to **prevent alienation of land** in Scheduled Areas and to take appropriate action to **restore any unlawfully alienated land of a Scheduled Tribe**;
   - **`Sec. 4(m)(iv)` — Village Markets:** Power to **manage village markets** by whatever name called (*haats* and *melas*);
   - **`Sec. 4(m)(v)` — Moneylending Control:** Power to **exercise control over money lending to the Scheduled Tribes** (attacking generational debt bondage);
   - **`Sec. 4(m)(vi)` — Social-Sector Oversight:** Power to **exercise control over institutions and functionaries in all social sectors** (primary schools, anganwadis, primary health sub-centres); and
   - **`Sec. 4(m)(vii)` — Local Plans and Tribal Sub-Plans:** Power to **control local plans and resources for such plans, including Tribal Sub-Plans (TSP)**.

#### Tribal Representation, Chairpersons and Sixth Schedule Inspiration (`Section 4(g)–(h), 4(n)–(o)`)

To ensure Adivasi political leadership cannot be diluted by non-tribal demographic influx into Scheduled Areas, PESA alters the reservation mechanics of `Article 243D` decisively:

- **Minimum 50% ST Seat Reservation (`Sec. 4(g)`):** Reservation of seats in Scheduled Areas is proportional to community population, subject to the mandatory constitutional-statutory floor that **reservation for Scheduled Tribes shall NOT be less than ONE-HALF (`≥ 50%`) of the total number of seats** in every Panchayat!
- **100% ST Reservation for ALL Chairperson Posts (`Sec. 4(g)` Proviso):** **ALL seats of Chairpersons of Panchayats at ALL levels** (village, intermediate, AND district) in Scheduled Areas MUST be reserved exclusively for **Scheduled Tribes**! *(Note: In `Union of India v. Rakesh Kumar` [2010], a 3-Judge Supreme Court bench upheld both the `≥50%` ST seat quota and `100%` ST Chairperson reservation under PESA as constitutionally valid exceptional protections for Scheduled Areas, distinguishing them from the general 50% ceiling applicable to non-Scheduled areas!)*
- **Nomination of Unrepresented STs (`Sec. 4(h)`):** The State Government may **nominate Scheduled Tribes that have no representation** in the Panchayat at the intermediate or district level, provided such nominations do **NOT exceed one-tenth (`1/10th`) of the total elected members** in that Panchayat.
- **Anti-Usurpation Safeguard (`Sec. 4(n)`):** State laws must contain explicit safeguards ensuring that **Panchayats at higher levels do NOT assume the powers and authority of any Panchayat at the lower level or of the Gram Sabha**.
- **Sixth Schedule Pattern at District Level (`Sec. 4(o)`):** State Legislatures are directed to endeavour to follow the **pattern of the Sixth Schedule** while designing administrative arrangements in district-level Panchayats in Scheduled Areas.

### Landmark Jurisprudence, Grassroots Successes and Ground Reality (1996–2026)

Judicial interpretation has breathed vital legal force into PESA whenever state executives and extractive industries attempted to sidestep Adivasi consent.

> [!case]
> **Landmark Tribal Self-Rule Rulings — *Samatha* (1997) and *Orissa Mining Corporation / Niyamgiri* (2013)**
> - ***Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (3-Judge Bench, 11 July 1997):** Interpreting the Fifth Schedule alongside tribal land-transfer regulations, the Supreme Court held that the word *"person"* prohibiting land transfer in Scheduled Areas includes the **State Government itself**. Consequently, State Governments cannot lease tribal or government forest land in Scheduled Areas to private non-tribal mining corporations—mining may only be undertaken by a state instrumentality or a tribal cooperative society, with at least **20 per cent of net profits** set aside for tribal welfare.
> - ***Orissa Mining Corporation Ltd. v. Ministry of Environment and Forests* (Niyamgiri Case, 3-Judge Bench, 18 April 2013):** When Vedanta / Orissa Mining Corporation sought Stage-II forest clearance for bauxite mining atop the Niyamgiri hills (sacred to the **Dongria Kondh** and **Kutia Kondh**, Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups in Rayagada and Kalahandi districts of Odisha), the Supreme Court harmonised **PESA (`Sec. 4(d)`)**, the **Forest Rights Act, 2006 (`Sec. 6`)**, and **`Articles 25–26` (religious/cultural rights)**. Holding that the Gram Sabha possesses statutory competence to safeguard religious and cultural rights over sacred hills, the Court directed that the proposal be placed directly before **12 affected Gram Sabhas**. In **July–August 2013**, all **12 Gram Sabhas unanimously voted against mining** in India's first "environmental referendum," compelling the MoEF to reject Stage-II forest clearance in January 2014!

> [!example]
> **Mendha-Lekha Village (Gadchiroli, Maharashtra) — PESA and FRA in Action:** Long before state rules matured, the Gond Adivasi village of **Mendha-Lekha** coined the celebrated self-rule slogan *"Dilli-Mumbait aamche sarkar, aamchya gavat aamhich sarkar"* (*"In Delhi and Mumbai is our government, in our village WE are the government"*). Combining PESA's MFP ownership guarantee with Community Forest Resource (CFR) rights under the **Forest Rights Act, 2006**, Mendha-Lekha became the first Indian village to win collective transit-pass rights to harvest and auction **bamboo** directly in 2011, transforming village incomes, reversing distress migration, funding community grain banks, and conserving 1,800 hectares of forest sustainably.

#### Ground Reality, Implementation Gaps and 2025–2026 State Rules Status

Nearly three decades after enactment, PESA's promise remains uneven across the ten Fifth Schedule states. Although the Union Ministry of Panchayati Raj circulated **Model PESA Rules in 2009**, panchayats fall under **Entry 5 of the State List**, leaving operationalisation dependent on State rules and statutory harmonisation.

> [!news]
> **PESA Rules Reach 9 of 10 Fifth Schedule States (January–August 2026):**
> For nearly 29 years, **Jharkhand** and **Odisha** operated without notified state PESA Rules. Following a **September 2025 Jharkhand High Court order** freezing sand-ghat and minor-mineral auctions until rules were notified, the Government of **Jharkhand formally notified its PESA Rules on 2 January 2026** (covering 13 full and 3 partial Scheduled districts, 16,022 villages, and 2,074 panchayats). Confirming the national status in a **Lok Sabha reply on 4 August 2026**, Union Panchayati Raj Minister Rajiv Ranjan Singh stated that **9 out of 10 PESA States**—Andhra Pradesh (2011), Himachal Pradesh (2011), Rajasthan (2011), Maharashtra (2014), Telangana (2016), Gujarat (2017; revised 2022), Chhattisgarh (2022), Madhya Pradesh (2022), and Jharkhand (Jan 2026)—have now notified their PESA Rules, leaving **Odisha as the sole pending state** (draft rules framed, awaiting final notification).

Even in the nine states with notified rules, **five structural implementation gaps** blunt PESA's transformative power:
1. **Non-Harmonisation of Subject Laws:** States routinely fail to amend conflicting colonial and post-Independence subject statutes—namely the **Indian Forest Act, 1927**, **State Excise Acts**, **State Land Revenue Codes**, **Minor Mineral Concession Rules**, and **Moneylending Acts**. Where a State Forest or Excise statute conflicts with PESA, district bureaucracies enforce the departmental Act, rendering Gram Sabha resolutions paper tigers.
2. **Dilution of Gram Sabha into Higher Tiers:** Because PESA uses the phrase *"Gram Sabha or the Panchayats at the appropriate level"* in clauses `4(i)`, `4(k)`, `4(l)`, and `4(m)`, several State Acts deliberately shift critical powers (such as minor-mineral recommendation or land-acquisition consultation) away from the hamlet Gram Sabha up to the **Block Samiti or Zila Parishad**—easier for bureaucracies to influence.
3. **"Consultation" vs "Consent" and Major-Mineral Exclusion:** Outside the LARR Act, 2013, PESA's `Section 4(i)` requires only non-binding *"consultation"* (not veto *"consent"*), and `Sections 4(k)–(l)` omit **major minerals** (coal, bauxite, iron ore)—which account for the overwhelming majority of tribal displacement across the mineral-rich central Indian belt!
4. **Administrative Non-Compliance and Forged Gram Sabha Resolutions:** Parliamentary committees and the Xaxa Committee (2014) have documented recurring complaints of coerced or fabricated Gram Sabha consent minutes in mining and infrastructure clearances, alongside weak legal literacy and non-maintenance of hamlet-level electoral rolls.
5. **No Urban Counterpart (Missing MESA):** As rapid industrialisation urbanises Scheduled Area towns (mining townships and census towns), converting a PESA Gram Panchayat into a Municipality strips tribals of PESA protections because Parliament has **never enacted a Municipal Extension to Scheduled Areas (MESA) Act**!

> [!prelims]
> **Six Classic UPSC Prelims Traps on the PESA Act, 1996 (Tested in Prelims 2012 & 2013!):**
> 1. **Fifth Schedule ONLY:** PESA applies **ONLY to Fifth Schedule Areas in 10 states**—NEVER to Sixth Schedule states (Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram)!
> 2. **Origin & Legal Status:** Based on **Dileep Singh Bhuria Committee (1995)**, enacted **24 December 1996** under **`Art. 243M(4)(b)`** as an **ordinary Parliamentary law** (NOT an Art. 368 amendment).
> 3. **ST Quotas:** Minimum **50% of seats** reserved for STs AND **100% (ALL) Chairperson posts at ALL levels** reserved for STs; up to **1/10th** unrepresented STs may be nominated at intermediate/district tiers.
> 4. **Minor Minerals ONLY:** Mandatory Gram Sabha / Panchayat prior recommendation applies strictly to **minor minerals** (`Sec. 4(k)–(l)`), NOT major minerals like coal or iron ore!
> 5. **Consultation vs Consent:** PESA `Sec. 4(i)` mandates **consultation** before land acquisition; **prior consent** flows from **Section 41 of the LARR Act, 2013**.
> 6. **2013 PYQ Clarification:** Under PESA, the Gram Sabha IS empowered to prevent alienation of land in Scheduled Areas, has ownership of **minor forest produce** (NOT all forest produce!), and recommends licences for **minor minerals** (NOT all minerals!).

```cases
Orissa Mining Corp. Ltd. v. MoEF (2013) | 3-Judge Bench · Niyamgiri Case | Harmonised PESA & FRA 2006; empowered 12 Dongria Kondh Gram Sabhas to decide bauxite mining on sacred Niyamgiri hills—all 12 voted unanimously against mining.
Vikas Kishanrao Gawali v. State of Maharashtra (2021) | 3-Judge Bench · OBC Triple Test | Mandated 3 conditions for OBC local quotas: (1) dedicated empirical commission, (2) local-body-wise quota specification, (3) aggregate SC+ST+OBC ≤ 50%.
State of Goa v. Fouziya Imtiaz Sheikh (2021) | 3-Judge Bench · SEC Autonomy | Held appointing serving bureaucrats as State Election Commissioners is a 'mockery of the Constitution'; mandated strictly independent SECs nationwide.
Suresh Mahajan v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2022) | 3-Judge Bench · Timely Polls | Ruled local body polls CANNOT be postponed pending OBC Triple Test; non-compliant OBC seats must be notified as general seats immediately.
```

## 4. The 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 — Municipalities (Part IXA and Twelfth Schedule)

India is undergoing one of the largest urban transitions in human history. From roughly 17 per cent urbanisation at Independence to **31.16 per cent in the 2011 Census** and over **36–38 per cent** in current demographic projections (contributing more than **60 per cent of India's GDP**), India's economic destiny is inextricably tied to the vitality of its cities and towns. Yet for over four decades after 1950, urban local bodies (ULBs) had **zero constitutional recognition**—not even a non-justiciable Directive Principle equivalent to `Article 40`! State Governments routinely superseded elected municipal corporations for years on end, starved cities of fiscal autonomy, and fragmented urban planning across unaccountable parastatal agencies.

### Historical Evolution of Urban Local Government in India

Modern urban municipal institutions in India actually predate rural statutory panchayats by nearly two centuries:

- **1687–88 (Madras):** The East India Company established India's **first Municipal Corporation at Madras** under a Royal Charter.
- **1726 (Bombay and Calcutta):** Municipal corporations were established in the other two presidency towns of Bombay and Calcutta.
- **1870 (Lord Mayo's Resolution):** Initiated financial decentralisation and urban local taxation to support civic amenities.
- **1882 (Lord Ripon's Resolution):** The *"Magna Carta of Local Self-Government"* promoted elected non-official majorities and non-official chairpersons in municipal boards.
- **1907 (Royal Commission on Decentralisation):** Recommended stronger municipal autonomy, elected non-official presidents, and broader civic tax powers.
- **1919 and 1935 Government of India Acts:** Urban local self-government became a **"Transferred" subject** under elected provincial ministers in 1919 and a **Provincial subject** in 1935 (continuing in the 1950 Constitution as **Entry 5 of the State List**).
- **1924 (Cantonments Act):** Enacted by the Central Legislature to codify municipal administration in military stations (later replaced by the **Cantonments Act, 2006**).
- **Three Central Ministries Handling ULBs:** At the Union level, urban local government touches **three distinct Ministries** (a classic Prelims check!): (1) **Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs (MoHUA)** for general urban policy across States; (2) **Ministry of Defence** for **Cantonment Boards**; and (3) **Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA)** for **Union Territories**.

Following the defeat of Rajiv Gandhi's **65th Constitutional Amendment Bill (1989)** in the Rajya Sabha and the lapse of V.P. Singh's combined Bill in 1990, P.V. Narasimha Rao's government enacted the **`74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992`** (vetted by K.P. Singh Deo's JPC; passed Parliament on **22–23 December 1992**; Presidential assent on **20 April 1993**; came into force on **`1 June 1993`**). The Act inserted **Part IXA** titled *"The Municipalities"* (**`Articles 243P` to `243ZG`**), added the **`Twelfth Schedule`** (**18 functional items**, `Article 243W`), and inserted **`Article 280(3)(c)`** linking the Union Finance Commission to State Finance Commissions for municipal resource augmentation.

### Core Constitutional Mechanics of Part IXA (Articles 243Q to 243ZG)

#### 1. Three Constitutional Types of Municipalities and the Industrial Township Exception (`Article 243Q`)

`Article 243Q(1)` mandates a uniform three-fold constitutional taxonomy of municipalities across every State:
1. **Nagar Panchayat** *(by whatever name called)* — for a **transitional area**, meaning an area in transition from a rural area to an urban area;
2. **Municipal Council** — for a **smaller urban area**; and
3. **Municipal Corporation** — for a **larger urban area**.

Under `Article 243Q(2)`, it is the **Governor** who specifies a transitional, smaller urban, or larger urban area by public notification, taking into account **five constitutional criteria**: (i) population of the area; (ii) density of population therein; (iii) revenue generated for local administration; (iv) percentage of employment in non-agricultural activities; and (v) economic importance or such other factors as the Governor deems fit.

Crucially, the **proviso to `Article 243Q(1)`** creates the **Industrial Township Exception**: the Governor may specify an urban area (or part thereof) as an **Industrial Township**, taking into account the size of the area and the municipal services being provided or proposed to be provided by an industrial establishment—in which case a regular elected Municipality **need NOT be constituted**! The textbook illustration is **Jamshedpur** in Jharkhand (where Tata Steel / JUSCO has historically provided civic infrastructure through the Jamshedpur Notified Area Committee rather than an elected municipal corporation—a model periodically debated before the Supreme Court over democratic deficit versus high civic efficiency).

#### 2. Composition, Direct Ward Polls and the Voting-Right Trap (`Article 243R`)

Under `Article 243R(1)`, **all seats** in a Municipality MUST be filled by **direct election** from territorial constituencies known as **wards**. In addition, the **State Legislature** may by law provide for representation in a Municipality of:
- **Nominated Experts (`Art. 243R(2)(a)(i)`):** Persons having **special knowledge or experience in municipal administration**—**PROVIDED THAT SUCH NOMINATED PERSONS SHALL NOT HAVE THE RIGHT TO VOTE in the meetings of the Municipality!** *(Compare this directly with `Article 243C(4)` for Panchayats, where ex-officio members DO possess voting rights in Panchayat meetings!)*
- **Legislators (`Art. 243R(2)(a)(ii)–(iii)`):** Lok Sabha MPs and MLAs representing constituencies wholly or partly within the municipal area, and Rajya Sabha MPs and MLCs registered as electors within the municipal area; and
- **Committee Chairpersons (`Art. 243R(2)(a)(iv)`):** Chairpersons of Wards Committees or other committees constituted under `Article 243S(5)`.

Unlike Part IX (which mandates indirect elections for Intermediate and District Panchayat Chairpersons), **`Article 243R(2)(b)`** leaves the **manner of election of the Chairperson (Mayor / Municipal President) of ALL types of Municipalities entirely to the State Legislature**! Consequently, states experiment widely between indirect election by councillors (e.g., Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru) and direct popular election by city voters (e.g., Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Tamil Nadu historically, Madhya Pradesh periodically).

#### 3. Wards Committees (`Article 243S`)

To counter the impersonality of sprawling modern cities where a single Municipal Corporation may govern millions of citizens, **`Article 243S`** mandates the constitution of **Wards Committees** consisting of **one or more wards** within the territorial area of every Municipality having a **population of three lakh (`3,00,000`) or more**. The State Legislature prescribes the composition, territorial area, and seat-filling rules; where a Wards Committee covers a single ward, that ward's councillor automatically chairs it, and where it spans two or more wards, one of the representing councillors is elected Chairperson by the Committee.

#### 4. Reservations, Fixed Tenure, Disqualifications, Shared SEC/SFC and Exempted Areas (`Articles 243T–243ZC, 243ZG`)

- **Reservations (`Art. 243T`):** Mirrors `Art. 243D` closely—proportional reservation for **SCs and STs** (rotated across wards) and **not less than one-third (`≥ 33.3%`)** of total direct seats (including SC/ST women seats) for **women**. Two fine Prelims distinctions versus Part IX: (1) while `Art. 243D(4)` directly mandates proportional SC/ST and `≥ 1/3rd` women reservation for Panchayat Chairpersons, `Art. 243T(4)` leaves the **manner of reserving Municipal Chairperson (Mayor/President) offices for SCs, STs, and women to the State Legislature**; (2) just like Part IX, under `Art. 243T(5)`, SC/ST reservation lapses with **`Article 334` (25 January 2030)**, whereas **women's reservation has NO sunset clause**! Voluntary **OBC reservation (`Art. 243T(6)`)** is strictly bound by the Supreme Court's **Triple Test** (*Vikas Kishanrao Gawali* 2021; *Suresh Mahajan* 2022).
- **Duration (`Art. 243U`) and Disqualifications (`Art. 243V`):** Identical to Part IX—fixed **5-year term** (*"and no longer"*), re-poll within **6 months** of dissolution (reconstituted council serves **only the remainder**; no poll if remainder `< 6 months`), and **minimum contesting age of 21 years**.

> [!case]
> **Sanctity of the Five-Year Municipal Term — *Kishansing Tomar v. Municipal Corporation of the City of Ahmedabad* (5-Judge Constitution Bench, 19 October 2006)**
> When the Gujarat State Government and SEC delayed Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation elections beyond the expiry of its five-year tenure citing ward delimitation and census revisions, a unanimous **5-Judge Constitution Bench** led by CJI Y.K. Sabharwal held that the phrase *"before the expiry of its duration"* in **`Article 243U(3)`** is **strictly mandatory**, NOT directory! State Election Commissions enjoy constitutional independence equivalent to the ECI (`Art. 324`), must ignore stalling tactics by State executives, and MUST complete municipal elections **before** the 5-year term expires—save in genuine *"man-made calamities like rioting or breakdown of law and order, or natural calamities"* (Act of God).

- **Shared Constitutional Bodies (`Arts. 243Y` and `243ZA`):** A crucial structural economy—States do **NOT** create separate commissions for cities! The **exact same State Finance Commission** (`Art. 243I` / `243Y`) and **exact same State Election Commission** (`Art. 243K` / `243ZA`) serve **BOTH Panchayats and Municipalities**.
- **Exempted Areas and Missing MESA (`Art. 243ZC`):** Part IXA does **NOT** apply to **Fifth Schedule (Scheduled Areas)**, **Sixth Schedule (Tribal Areas)**, or the **Darjeeling Gorkha Hill Council**. Although `Article 243ZC(3)` empowers Parliament by ordinary law to extend Part IXA with modifications to Scheduled and Tribal Areas, **unlike rural PESA (1996), Parliament has NEVER enacted the Municipalities (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Bill (MESA Bill, 2001)**—leaving urbanised tribal towns in a constitutional limbo!

### Constitutional Regional Planning: District Planning Committee (DPC) vs Metropolitan Planning Committee (MPC)

One of the boldest spatial-planning innovations of the 74th Amendment was creating **two constitutional multi-tier planning committees**—**`Article 243ZD` (DPC)** and **`Article 243ZE` (MPC)**—to bridge the artificial administrative divide between rural Panchayats and urban Municipalities and end siloed, top-down departmental planning.

- **District Planning Committee (`Article 243ZD`):** Every State MUST constitute a **DPC at the district level** to consolidate the plans prepared by both **Panchayats AND Municipalities** in the district and prepare a unified **draft development plan for the district as a whole**. Crucially, **not less than four-fifths (`≥ 4/5ths`, i.e., `80%`)** of the total DPC members MUST be **elected by, and from amongst, the elected members of the District Panchayat and of the Municipalities in the district**, strictly in proportion to the ratio between the **rural and urban populations** of the district! In preparing the draft plan, the DPC must consider matters of common rural-urban interest (spatial planning, sharing of water and physical/natural resources, integrated infrastructure, and environmental conservation) alongside the extent and type of available financial and other resources. The Chairperson forwards the draft plan to the State Government.
- **Metropolitan Planning Committee (`Article 243ZE`):** Every **Metropolitan Area** MUST constitute an **MPC** to prepare a **draft development plan for the Metropolitan Area as a whole**. Under **`Article 243P(c)`**, a *"Metropolitan Area"* is constitutionally defined by **three strict criteria**: (1) an area having a **population of 10 lakh (`10,00,000`) or more**; (2) comprised in **one or more districts**; and (3) consisting of **two or more Municipalities or Panchayats or other contiguous areas**, notified by the Governor. Crucially, **not less than two-thirds (`≥ 2/3rds`, i.e., `66.7%`)** of MPC members MUST be **elected by, and from amongst, the elected members of the Municipalities AND Chairpersons of the Panchayats** in the Metropolitan Area, in proportion to the ratio of population of the Municipalities and Panchayats! The State Legislature also prescribes representation of the Union Government, State Government, and relevant parastatal agencies in the remaining one-third.

### All Eight Types of Urban Local Governments in India

Beyond the three constitutional municipalities created under `Article 243Q`, real-world urban administration in India operates through **eight distinct institutional forms**—each with a different legal origin, degree of democratic election, and supervisory ministry (a classic M. Laxmikanth Ch. 39 staple heavily tested in Prelims):

#### 1. Municipal Corporation
Created for mega-cities and major urban agglomerations (such as Mumbai, Delhi, Kolkata, Chennai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Ahmedabad, Pune) by Acts of **State Legislatures** (and by an Act of **Parliament** for Union Territories like the Municipal Corporation of Delhi). Every Municipal Corporation functions through **three distinct authorities**:
- **The Council:** The deliberative and legislative wing consisting of directly elected ward councillors (plus a few non-voting nominated experts), headed by a **Mayor** assisted by a Deputy Mayor. In most Indian cities, the Mayor is elected indirectly for a **one-year renewable term** and serves largely as a **ceremonial "First Citizen"** presiding over council debates!
- **The Standing Committees:** Created to facilitate detailed sectoral work in public works, education, health, taxation, and finance, with authority to make operational decisions within their remit.
- **The Municipal Commissioner:** The **chief executive authority** responsible for implementing decisions of the Council and Standing Committees—almost invariably an **IAS officer appointed by the State Government**, creating the iconic structural friction between an accountable ceremonial Mayor and a powerful State-controlled bureaucrat.

#### 2. Municipality (Municipal Council / Municipal Committee / Nagar Palika / City Municipality)
Established for smaller cities and towns by State Acts (or Parliamentary Acts for UTs). Like a Corporation, it has **three authorities**: (a) the **Council** (deliberative wing headed by an elected **President / Chairman**, who plays a significantly stronger executive-supervisory role than a ceremonial big-city Mayor); (b) **Standing Committees**; and (c) the **Chief Executive Officer / Chief Municipal Officer**, appointed by the State Government to manage day-to-day administration.

#### 3. Notified Area Committee (NAC)
Constituted for two specific categories of settlements: (a) a **fast-developing town due to industrialisation**; or (b) a town that does not yet satisfy all statutory criteria for a full Municipality yet is considered important by the State Government. Crucially, an NAC is **NOT created by a separate statute**—it is established solely by a **notification in the State Government Gazette** (applying select provisions of the State Municipal Act). Its defining Prelims distinction: a Notified Area Committee is an **ENTIRELY NOMINATED BODY**—every member, including the Chairman, is **nominated by the State Government**, with **NO elected members**!

#### 4. Town Area Committee (TAC)
Set up by a **separate Act of the State Legislature** for the administration of a **small town**. It operates as a **semi-municipal authority** entrusted with a limited set of basic civic duties—drainage, roads, street lighting, and conservancy. Depending on the specific State Act, a TAC may be **wholly elected, wholly nominated by the State Government, or partly elected and partly nominated**.

#### 5. Cantonment Board
Established for municipal administration of the **civilian population residing within a military cantonment area** (delimited military stations). Unlike all other local bodies under Entry 5 of the State List, Cantonment Boards are governed by **Entry 3 of the Union List** under a central statute—the **`Cantonments Act, 2006`** (which repealed the colonial Cantonments Act, 1924)—and function under the **administrative control of the Union Ministry of Defence (Directorate General Defence Estates)**, **NOT the State Government**!

Cantonment Boards are classified into **four categories** based on civilian population: **Category I** (above 50,000), **Category II** (10,000 to 50,000), **Category III** (2,500 to 10,000), and **Category IV** (below 2,500). A Cantonment Board is a **partly elected and partly nominated body**: elected members serve a **five-year term** (with **Vice-President** elected by and from amongst the elected members), while nominated/ex-officio members hold office as long as they retain their official station post. Crucially, the **military officer commanding the station serves as the ex-officio PRESIDENT** of the Board and presides over its meetings, while the **Executive Officer**—a civilian officer of the **Indian Defence Estates Service (IDES)**—is **appointed by the President of India** to implement Board resolutions.

> [!news]
> **Dismantling a Colonial Enclave — Cantonment Civil Area Excision (2023–2026):**
> Responding to decades of demands from civilian cantonment residents citing restricted building norms, lack of state-scheme convergence, and military-headed municipal governance, the Union Ministry of Defence began unwinding India's **62 colonial-era cantonments**. On **27 April 2023**, the Centre formally denotified **Khas Yol Cantonment** in Himachal Pradesh—converting the military area into a dedicated military station and merging civilian zones with state local bodies (reducing India's cantonments from **62 to 61**). In **July 2023**, the MoD shared modalities with States for excising civil areas across up to **58 cantonments**, and on **5 March 2024**, issued formal draft gazette notifications inviting objections to excise civil areas of **10 cantonments** (Dehradun, Clement Town, Fatehgarh, Babina, Shahjahanpur, Mathura, Deolali, Ramgarh, Ajmer, and Nasirabad) for merger into adjacent state municipalities—though implementation across states like Himachal Pradesh slowed in 2025–26 over asset-liability valuation and municipal transition negotiations.

#### 6. Township
Established directly by large **Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs)**—such as SAIL (Bhilai, Rourkela, Bokaro), BHEL, NTPC, ONGC, and HMT—to provide residential housing and civic amenities to employees and workers settled in dedicated colonies near industrial plants. Managed entirely by a **Town Administrator** appointed by the enterprise (assisted by company engineers and staff), a Township is an administrative extension of the PSU itself and has **NO elected members**.

#### 7. Port Trust (Now Major Port Authority)
Historically created in major port cities (such as Mumbai, Kolkata, and Chennai) under the Major Port Trusts Act, 1963 to manage port operations alongside civic amenities inside port limits, with both official and non-official representatives chaired by a Central Government official. Following the enactment of the **`Major Port Authorities Act, 2021`**, India's twelve Major Port Trusts transitioned into autonomous **Major Port Authority Boards** with modern corporate governance and commercial autonomy while retaining port-estate municipal oversight.

#### 8. Special Purpose Agency (SPA)
Unlike the seven **area-based** bodies above, **Special Purpose Agencies** are **FUNCTION-BASED (single-purpose)** bodies created by Acts of State Legislatures or State executive resolutions to perform designated specialised urban functions outside municipal jurisdiction—examples include **Town Improvement Trusts**, **Urban Development Authorities** (DDA in Delhi, BDA in Bengaluru, MMRDA in Mumbai, HMDA in Hyderabad), **Water Supply and Sewerage Boards** (BWSSB, DJB, CMWSSB), **Housing Boards**, **Pollution Control Boards**, and **Electricity Supply Boards**. They operate as autonomous statutory corporations answerable directly to State Ministries rather than to elected city councils—a major source of urban governance fragmentation analysed in Section 5.

### Central Council of Local Government (Article 263, 1954)

To promote cooperative policy dialogue between the Union and State Governments on local-body governance, the **Central Council of Local Government** was established in **1954** by an **Order of the President of India under `Article 263`** (Inter-State Council provisions of the Constitution). Aspirants must master three classic Prelims facts embedded in this institution:

1. **Shift in Mandate (1958) and Name (1980s):** Originally established as the *"Central Council of Local Self-Government,"* it initially dealt with **BOTH urban and rural local governments**. However, **since 1958**, its mandate was narrowed to deal **EXCLUSIVELY with URBAN local government**! In the **1980s**, the word *"self-government"* was replaced with *"government,"* giving it its present name.
2. **Composition:** It comprises the **Union Minister for Housing and Urban Affairs** (formerly Urban Development) as **Chairman**, along with the **Ministers for Local Self-Government of all States** as members.
3. **Purely Advisory Functions:** It operates strictly as an **advisory body**—considering and recommending broad lines of urban policy, proposing model municipal legislation, examining patterns of cooperation between the Centre and States, drawing up common programmes of urban action, recommending central financial assistance, and reviewing work accomplished by local bodies with Union grants.

> [!remember]
> **Two High-Frequency Elimination Anchors for Urban Local Government:**
> - **NAC vs TAC vs Cantonment Board vs SPA:** **NAC** = Gazette notification & **100% nominated**; **TAC** = separate State Act & semi-municipal; **Cantonment Board** = **Union Defence Ministry** (`2006 Act`) & military commander President; **SPA** = **function-based** (NOT area-based)!
> - **Central Council of Local Government (1954):** Created under **`Article 263`** (Presidential Order) → dealt with **both rural & urban** until 1958 → **ONLY urban local government since 1958** → **purely advisory**, chaired by **Union Minister for Housing & Urban Affairs**!

## 5. The 3Fs (Functions, Funds, Functionaries), Local Finances and Governance Reforms

Over three decades after the `73rd` and `74th Constitutional Amendments` came into force in 1993, India's grassroots democracy displays a sharp structural paradox. On the **electoral and representational axis**, decentralisation has been a triumph: regular polls operate across over **2.5 lakh Gram Panchayats** and nearly **5,000 urban local bodies**, bringing more than **31 lakh elected representatives**—including **over 15 lakh women (~46.6%)** and lakhs of SC/ST leaders—into constitutional governance. Yet on the **functional, fiscal, and administrative axis**, India's third tier remains severely stunted. Across UPSC Mains GS-2, this persistent gulf between constitutional promise and ground reality is systematically diagnosed through the **3Fs Framework**: **Functions**, **Funds**, and **Functionaries**.

### Functions: Discretionary Devolution, Activity Mapping Gaps and Parallel Parastatals

The root constitutional flaw crippling local autonomy sits in a single permissive auxiliary verb: both **`Article 243G`** (29 Eleventh Schedule rural subjects) and **`Article 243W`** (18 Twelfth Schedule urban subjects) declare that a State Legislature ***"may, by law, endow"*** local bodies with powers and responsibilities. Because devolution was left optional to win State consent in 1992, most State Governments enacted legislative transfers on paper while retaining actual executive control, budgets, and staff inside State line departments.

#### Incomplete Activity Mapping
Genuine devolution requires granular **Activity Mapping**—systematically unbundling every functional item (such as primary health, drinking water, or primary education) into six distinct operational sub-tasks: *(1) policy formulation and standard-setting, (2) planning, (3) asset creation, (4) operations and maintenance (O&M), (5) beneficiary selection, and (6) monitoring/social audit*—and assigning each sub-task unambiguously to the Gram Panchayat, Block Samiti, Zila Parishad, Municipality, or State Government so no tier overlaps or ducks accountability. Aside from frontrunner states like **Kerala, Karnataka, and Tamil Nadu**, most states have either skipped activity mapping or left it unbacked by corresponding budgetary heads, trapping PRIs and ULBs in **unfunded mandates** where they function merely as low-cost implementing agents of top-down Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) and State flagship programmes.

#### Parallel Parastatals, Smart City SPVs and the Census Towns Paradox
In urban India, functional hollowing is compounded by institutional fragmentation. High-value Twelfth Schedule functions—**urban planning, land-use regulation, water supply, public transport, and slum redevelopment**—are systematically siphoned away from elected municipal corporations into State-controlled **Special Purpose Agencies / parastatals** (such as the Delhi Development Authority [DDA], Bengaluru Development Authority [BDA], Mumbai Metropolitan Region Development Authority [MMRDA], and State Water and Housing Boards). Even flagship Union schemes such as the **Smart Cities Mission (2015)** routed investments through **Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs)** incorporated under the Companies Act, 2013—corporatised boards chaired by state bureaucrats with negligible municipal councillor oversight—directly subverting the spirit of the 74th Amendment.

Simultaneously, India faces a stark spatial-governance anomaly flagged by **NITI Aayog's *Reforms in Urban Planning Capacity in India* (September 2021)** and **PRS Legislative Research (2025)**:
- Nearly **half of India's 7,933 urban settlements** (Census 2011) are **Census Towns**—places meeting all three demographic/economic criteria of urbanity (population `≥ 5,000`, `≥ 75%` male main workforce in non-agriculture, density `≥ 400 persons/sq. km`)—yet State Governments refrain from notifying them as statutory Municipalities to preserve rural CSS subsidies and avoid municipal taxation, leaving fast-urbanising peripheries governed as ill-equipped rural Gram Panchayats!
- Only about **28 per cent (2,216 towns)** possessed approved statutory **Master Plans**, exposing cities to unplanned sprawl, wetland encroachment, urban flooding, and heatwave vulnerability while **District Planning Committees (`Art. 243ZD`)** and **Metropolitan Planning Committees (`Art. 243ZE`)** lie largely dormant or purely ceremonial across most metros.

### Funds and Local Finances: Own Revenue, SFCs, Union FC Grants and Municipal Bonds

Fiscal autonomy is the lifeblood of self-government; without buoyant local revenues and predictable statutory transfers, elected councils remain reduced to petitioning State secretariats. Recent primary empirical audits by the **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** and the **16th Finance Commission (2026)** reveal the full depth of India's third-tier fiscal crisis—and the historic reforms now underway.

#### The Own-Source Revenue Crisis: RBI Empirical Audits (2022–2024)

|---|---|---|---|
| **Total Revenue as % of GDP** | All ULBs **~1.0% GDP** (2022); **232 MCs = 0.6% GDP** (2023–24 BE) | Total PRI revenue **₹35,354 cr** (~0.1% GDP) | Centre **9.2%**, States **14.6%**; Brazil **7.4%**, South Africa **6.0%** |

The **RBI's second *Report on Municipal Finances* (13 November 2024)**, examining **232 Municipal Corporations** from 2019–20 to 2023–24 (BE), highlighted four structural roots of urban fiscal starvation:
1. **Property Tax Under-Collection (`0.15% of GDP`):** Though property tax forms over 60 per cent of municipal own-tax receipts, vast swaths of urban properties remain unmapped; outdated Annual Rental Value assessments lag far behind market capital values; exemptions abound; and local politicians fear electoral backlash from periodic rate revisions.
2. **Post-Octroi and Post-GST Erosion:** Abolition of **octroi** (historically Mumbai's and Gujarat's most buoyant local levy) and the subsumption of local entry taxes, advertisement levies, and entertainment taxes into the **Goods and Services Tax (GST, 2017)** permanently hollowed out municipal tax bases—with **NO constitutional guarantee of a dedicated municipal share of GST**, leaving ULBs dependent on erratic State compensation grants.
3. **Under-Priced User Charges:** Municipal water supply, sewerage, parking, and solid-waste fees recover only a fraction of operational costs, forcing most corporations to spend own revenues entirely on staff salaries and pensions with negligible surplus for capital expenditure.
4. **Rural Own-Revenue Vacuum:** In rural India, the **RBI's *Report on Finances of Panchayati Raj Institutions* (January 2024)** revealed that 95 per cent of Gram Panchayat receipts consist of tied/untied Union and State grants, with own taxes averaging barely ₹21,000 per GP—though property mapping under **SVAMITVA** and digital revenue portals like **SAMARTH Panchayat** are beginning to unlock local property-tax rolls.

#### State Finance Commissions (`Articles 243I` and `243Y`) and the 16th FC Constitutional Debate

Constitutionalised to inject objective, rule-based fiscal predictability at the sub-state level, **State Finance Commissions (SFCs)** have emerged as the single weakest link in India's fiscal federalism. Across states, six recurring pathologies plague SFCs: (1) **chronic delays** in constituting SFCs every five years; (2) **non-synchronisation** with the Union Finance Commission's five-year cycle; (3) appointment of retired bureaucrats lacking public-finance expertise and **absence of permanent SFC secretariats** or institutional memory; (4) severe **local fiscal data gaps** (unaudited, cash-based local accounts); (5) years-long delays in tabling **Action Taken Reports (ATRs)** in State Legislatures; and (6) **selective rejection or non-release** of accepted devolution grants by State Finance Departments.

This chronic failure triggered a landmark constitutional proposal from the **16th Finance Commission (2025–26)**! Because **`Articles 280(3)(bb)` and `280(3)(c)`** direct the Union Finance Commission to recommend local-body augmentation *"on the basis of the recommendations made by the Finance Commission of the State,"* successive Union FCs found themselves unable to aggregate uneven, delayed, or missing SFC reports across 28 states. Breaking decades of cautious diplomacy, the **16th FC** observed bluntly:

> "In Articles 280(3)(bb) and 280(3)(c), the Constitution directs the FC to make its recommendations on [rural and urban local bodies] on the basis of the recommendations made by the [SFC]. However, as successive commissions have noted… serious obstacles remain in the way of meaningfully basing our recommendations on those provided by SFCs... we recommend that the above-quoted expression be dropped from the relevant articles through a Constitutional amendment." — Report of the Sixteenth Finance Commission, Volume I (tabled 1 February 2026)

Simultaneously, the 16th FC retained **timely constitution of SFCs** as one of its three mandatory entry-level criteria for claiming Union local grants and urged **NITI Aayog** to publish a national compendium of SFC good practices. Public-finance scholars have sparked a healthy Mains debate on this proposal: while pragmatists endorse aligning constitutional text with operational reality, fiscal federalists warn that formally delinking Articles 280 and 243I/243Y could remove the last constitutional pressure compelling States to strengthen SFCs—arguing instead for legally enforcing synchronisation and permanent SFC cells!

#### Union Finance Commission Local Body Grants: 15th FC (2021–26) vs 16th FC (2026–31)

Under **`Articles 280(3)(bb)` and `280(3)(c)`**, Union Finance Commission grants have evolved into the single largest predictable lifeline for India's Panchayats and Municipalities. Chaired by **Dr. Arvind Panagariya**, the **16th Finance Commission** (constituted 31 December 2023; submitted report on **17 November 2025**; tabled and accepted by Parliament on **`1 February 2026`**) delivered India's largest-ever local-body fiscal package—**₹7,91,493 crore** for **2026–31** (out of ₹9,47,409 crore total grants-in-aid, after discontinuing Revenue Deficit, Sector-Specific, and State-Specific grants)—marking an **~81 per cent jump** over the 15th FC's ₹4,36,361 crore!

#### Municipal Bonds and Capital Market Financing

Because tax transfers alone cannot bridge India's multi-trillion-rupee urban infrastructure deficit, tapping domestic debt markets through **Municipal Bonds** has become a vital pillar of urban finance. After early pioneers (**Bengaluru 1997** with state guarantee; **Ahmedabad 1998**—India's first municipal bond *without* state guarantee!), the market stagnated until **SEBI's 2015 Issue and Listing of Municipal Debt Securities (ILMDS) Regulations** (revised 2019) and **AMRUT / AMRUT 2.0 incentives** (offering ₹13 crore per ₹100 crore issued for first-time ULB issuers) revitalised issuances across **Pune (2017)**, **Hyderabad**, **Ahmedabad (2019)**, **Surat**, **Vadodara**, **Visakhapatnam**, **Lucknow**, **Ghaziabad** (India's first green municipal bond via private placement, 2021), and **Indore** (**India's first public retail green municipal bond** in February 2023, funding a 60 MW solar plant for water pumping!).

> [!news]
> **Record Municipal Bond Momentum & RBI Repo Collateral Boost (2025–2026):**
> Following the Central Government's **22 October 2025 Gazette notification** under Section 45U of the RBI Act, 1934, the **RBI issued its Master Direction on Repurchase Transactions (Repo) on 11 November 2025**, officially admitting **SEBI-regulated Municipal Debt Securities as eligible collateral for repo and reverse repo transactions**—a major liquidity breakthrough enabling banks and institutional investors to borrow against municipal bonds! Reinforced by Union Budget 2025–26's **₹1 lakh crore Urban Challenge Fund** (funding up to 25% of bankable "Cities as Growth Hubs," redevelopment, and water/sanitation projects conditioned on at least 50% financing from bonds, bank loans, or PPPs), municipal bond issuances surged to a record in **FY 2025–26**—with over a dozen ULBs (including **Greater Chennai, Nashik, Surat, Pimpri-Chinchwad**, and first-time issuers **Agra, Prayagraj, Varanasi, and Bhavnagar**) raising roughly **₹1,756–2,000 crore** (matching nearly two-thirds of the entire 2018–25 cumulative volume!). Yet perspective remains vital: per **RBI (Nov 2024)** and **CareEdge Ratings (Sept 2025)**, municipal bonds still represent just **0.06–0.09 per cent of India's corporate bond market** (against **~14% of GDP in the United States**), constrained by weak credit ratings, accrual-accounting gaps, and execution shortfalls (illustrated when Pune Municipal Corporation moved to return unutilised proceeds from its 2017 ₹200 crore water bond).

### Functionaries: Bureaucratic Dominance, Capacity Deficit and Proxy Leadership

Even well-funded local bodies collapse without accountable, technically skilled **Functionaries**—the missing third tier of India's civil service.

#### Missing Local Cadres and Acute Technical Shortage
Most Indian States lack dedicated, professional **State Municipal Cadres** and **Panchayat Cadres**. Key operational officials—the **Municipal Commissioner** (IAS), **Zila Parishad Chief Executive Officer**, **Block Development Officer (BDO)**, and **Panchayat Secretary**—serve on temporary deputation from State or All-India Services. Because their promotions, transfers, and Annual Performance Appraisal Reports (APARs) are controlled by State Ministers and Chief Secretaries rather than elected Mayors or Zila Parishad Presidents, their institutional loyalty runs vertically upward to State capitals instead of downward to local citizens. Meanwhile, technical capacity is alarmingly thin: **NITI Aayog (2021)** documented barely **~5,000 registered urban planners** across India—roughly **one urban planner per 75,000 urban citizens** (compared to 1 per 5,000 in the UK)—alongside chronic vacancies in municipal engineering, town planning, GIS mapping, and double-entry accrual accounting.

#### Bureaucratic Dominance: The Colonial "Commissioner System" vs Ceremonial Mayor
In most Indian Municipal Corporations (modelled on the 1888 Bombay Municipal Corporation Act), executive authority is structurally divorced from democratic legitimacy. The **Municipal Commissioner**—an unelected IAS officer appointed and transferable at will by the State Government—wields virtually all executive, administrative, financial, and contracting authority. By contrast, the **Mayor** is typically elected indirectly by councillors for an ephemeral **one-year term** (as in Delhi, Bengaluru, or Mumbai's 2.5-year rotational system), restricted to chairing council meetings and welcoming visiting dignitaries without authority to write the Commissioner's appraisal or execute city-wide projects. Citizens thus hold the Mayor politically responsible for flooded roads or broken drainage, while real power rests with a State-appointed bureaucrat insulated from voters!

> [!debate]
> **Directly Elected Empowered Mayor (5-Year Term) vs Municipal Commissioner Model**
> - **Case FOR Directly Elected Executive Mayor (2nd ARC, 6th Report; Shashi Tharoor Private Member's Bill; Global Best Practice — London, New York, Tokyo):** Creates a single, visible, democratically accountable city chief executive with a **fixed 5-year mandate**; empowers the Mayor to coordinate parastatals (metro, water, police-traffic, housing), negotiate municipal bonds, and hold the Commissioner (recast as a professional City Manager) accountable to citizens.
> - **Case for CAUTION / Institutional Safeguards:** Critics warn that a directly elected Mayor facing a hostile Municipal Council controlled by a rival party can trigger **executive–legislative gridlock** (the US presidential deadlock at city scale); without strict checks—such as a strong Council budget veto, statutory **Municipal Ombudsman**, transparent procurement, and an empowered Standing Committee—concentrating urban land-use power in one individual risks populist patronage or corruption. **Balanced Consensus:** Pair a directly elected 5-year Mayor (or Mayor-in-Council model as in Kolkata/West Bengal, where the Mayor + cabinet are accountable to the Council) with strong Council checks, Ward Committees, and a dedicated Municipal Cadre.

#### Women's Empowerment vs Proxy Leadership (*Sarpanch Pati / Pradhan Pati*)
Women's mandatory reservation under `Articles 243D` and `243T`—voluntarily expanded to **50 per cent by 21 States and 2 UTs**—has brought **over 15 lakh Women Elected Representatives (~46.6%)** into PRIs. Landmark empirical economics research by **Raghabendra Chattopadhyay and Esther Duflo (2004)** demonstrated across West Bengal and Rajasthan that Gram Panchayats headed by women Sarpanches invest significantly more in **clean drinking water, local roads, maternal health, and primary schools**—public goods directly prioritised by rural women. Over multiple election cycles, women's reservation also breaks gender stereotypes, inspiring higher educational aspirations among adolescent village girls.

Yet deep patriarchal structures persistently subvert substantive agency through **proxy politics**—popularly termed ***Sarpanch Pati*, *Pradhan Pati*, or *Mukhiya Pati***—where husbands, fathers-in-law, or male relatives attend official meetings, sign cheques, negotiate tenders, and issue administrative directives on behalf of formally elected women officeholders.

> [!news]
> **Sushil Kumar Advisory Committee Report on Curbing *Pradhan Pati* (5 February 2025):**
> Acting in compliance with Supreme Court observations on proxy control in PRIs, the Union Ministry of Panchayati Raj constituted an Advisory Committee (October 2023) chaired by former Mines Secretary **Sushil Kumar**, which submitted its landmark report on **5 February 2025** (accepted by MoPR). Diagnosing proxy rule across socio-cultural, legal, and administrative dimensions, the Committee recommended six decisive interventions:
> 1. **Enforce "Exemplary Penalties"** against verified cases of male relatives usurping elected women's authority, backed by clear statutory definitions of proxy interference;
> 2. **Public Swearing-In of Women Pradhans** directly in open **Gram Sabha** assemblies to cement community legitimacy;
> 3. **Women's Ombudspersons & Watchdog Committees** alongside **confidential helplines and whistleblower rewards** to report coercion safely;
> 4. **Federations of Women Elected Representatives** for peer mentoring, leadership pipelines, and legal-literacy clinics under **RGSA**;
> 5. **Digital Transparency via Panchayat NIRNAY** and video-recorded Gram Sabha proceedings verifying active physical participation; and
> 6. **Ending Rapid 5-Year Single-Term Seat Rotation** (which discourages male elites from letting women build independent political careers) in favour of **two consecutive terms (10-year rotation)** so women leaders cultivate durable grassroots constituencies!

#### Digital Transformation of Village Governance (2024–2026)
Over the past three years, digital public infrastructure has begun tackling chronic rural accounting opacity and asset-mapping deficits:
- **eGramSwaraj and AuditOnline:** Integrated planning, accounting, and PFMS payment platform facilitating **over ₹3 lakh crore in transparent digital payments** across **>2.5 lakh Gram Panchayats** by March 2026, paired with **AuditOnline** (meeting 16th FC online-audit entry criteria), **Meri Panchayat** (citizen app), and **SAMARTH Panchayat** (boosting local Own Source Revenue).
- **SVAMITVA Scheme:** Drone-mapping inhabited rural *abadi* lands across **~3.29–3.30 lakh villages** and preparing **over 3.10 crore Property Cards** by early 2026—providing clear property titles that unlock institutional rural credit, slash land disputes, and create accurate rural property-tax assessment registers!
- **SabhaSaar AI (Launched 14 August 2025):** An **AI-powered voice-to-text Gram Sabha minutes generator** integrated with **Bhashini** across **23 Indian languages**, adopted by **over 1.1 lakh Gram Panchayats** by early 2026—curbing forged Gram Sabha resolutions and automating transparent minute-keeping.

> [!example]
> **Three Gold-Standard Grassroots Models for UPSC Mains Enrichment:**
> 1. **Kerala's People's Plan Campaign (1996) & Kudumbashree:** Earmarked **35–40 per cent of State plan funds** directly as untied grants to local bodies, backed by participatory *Gram Sabhas*, citizen task forces, transparent activity mapping, and convergence with the 45-lakh-strong women's self-help network **Kudumbashree**—proving real 3Fs devolution accelerates human development!
> 2. **Indore Municipal Corporation (Madhya Pradesh):** Combined 100 per cent door-to-door waste segregation, user-fee collection, bio-CNG monetisation, and strong fiscal transparency to top *Swachh Survekshan* repeatedly and launch **India's first oversubscribed retail Green Municipal Bond (Feb 2023)**.
> 3. **Tamil Nadu Urban Development Fund (TNUDF — Pooled Finance):** Enabled smaller Tier-II and Tier-III municipalities—too small to tap bond markets individually—to **pool creditworthiness and issue joint municipal bonds** for water and sanitation infrastructure, offering a replicable template for smaller towns nationwide.

### Second ARC (6th Report — Local Governance) and Balanced Way Forward

In its seminal **Sixth Report, *Local Governance: An Inspiring Journey into the Future* (October 2007, chaired by M. Veerappa Moily)**, the **Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2nd ARC)** articulated the definitive philosophical and operational compass for third-tier governance in India. Grounding all reforms in the **Principle of Subsidiarity**—*whatever can be done best at a lower tier of government must be vested at that tier and NOT centralised upward*—the 2nd ARC laid down ten foundational recommendations that remain the gold standard for GS-2 Mains answers:

1. **Clear Constitutional & Statutory Activity Mapping:** Replace vague overlapping transfers across all 29 rural and 18 urban items with legally binding activity mapping backed by corresponding untied funds and staff.
2. **Empowerment of the Mayor (5-Year Direct Election):** Abolish the colonial 1-year ceremonial Mayor model; institute a **directly elected Mayor** with a **fixed 5-year term** exercising executive authority over the city administration, converting the Municipal Commissioner into an accountable Chief Executive Officer under the Mayor.
3. **Institutionalising Area Sabhas (Model Nagar Raj Bill):** Create statutory **Area Sabhas** (comprising all registered voters in a polling-booth area, below the Wards Committee) across every urban ward so city residents enjoy direct participatory democracy equivalent to the rural Gram Sabha!
4. **Operationalising DPCs and MPCs & Dismantling Parallel Silos:** Empower **District Planning Committees (`Art. 243ZD`)** and **Metropolitan Planning Committees (`Art. 243ZE`)** as genuine spatial-budgetary authorities; gradually merge urban parastatals (Development Authorities, Water Boards, Smart City SPVs) into accountable elected ULBs.
5. **Strengthening and Synchronising State Finance Commissions:** Synchronise every SFC cycle cleanly with the Union Finance Commission; create permanent SFC technical cells; enforce mandatory legislature tabling of ATRs within 6 months; and link Union grants strictly to **16th FC's three entry criteria**.
6. **Municipal Tax Buoyancy, Property Reforms & Bond Deepening:** Roll out universal **GIS-based property tax** mapping with transparent unit-area/capital-value assessment; rationalise user charges; examine a **dedicated local-body share of GST** (mirroring 13th/15th FC debates); expand **TNUDF-style state pooled finance** alongside the **₹1 lakh crore Urban Challenge Fund** and **RBI's repo-eligible municipal bonds**.
7. **Dedicated Professional Local Cadres & CAG Audit Supervision:** Establish dedicated **State Municipal Cadres** and **Panchayat Technical Cadres** (planners, civil engineers, accrual accountants) accountable to local bodies, paired with **CAG Technical Guidance and Supervision** over State Local Fund Audits and **Social Audits** modelled on Meghalaya's Social Audit Act.
8. **Independent Local Body Ombudsman:** Establish an independent statutory **Ombudsman** at the district/state level (pioneered by Kerala) to investigate corruption, maladministration, and proxy governance in PRIs and ULBs without enabling arbitrary State executive supersession.
9. **Substantive Inclusion & Tribal Self-Rule:** Implement the **Sushil Kumar Committee (2025)** anti-proxy roadmap (exemplary penalties, 10-year two-term women's rotation, public oaths) to eliminate *Pradhan Pati*; notify Odisha's pending PESA Rules and **harmonise Forest, Excise, Mining, and Land statutes** across all 10 PESA states; and enact **MESA** for Scheduled Area towns.
10. **Climate-Resilient Local Governance:** Equip every Municipality and Gram Panchayat with statutory Master Plans / Gram Panchayat Development Plans (GPDPs) integrating urban drainage, heatwave action plans, wastewater circularity (leveraging the **16th FC's ₹56,100 cr window**), and community ecosystem resilience.

## 1. Evolution of Panchayati Raj and Reform Committees (1957–1992)

- **Ancient & Chola Roots:** Chola rural bodies: peasant villages = ***ur***, merchant towns = ***nagaram***, Brahmin *brahmadeyas* = ***sabha/mahasabha***. **Uttaramerur inscriptions** (**919 & 921 CE**, **Parantaka I**) record 30 wards, functional ***variyams*** (*eri*, *thotta*, *pon*, *samvatsara*), and ***kudavolai*** (**palm-leaf lottery**, *not* adult suffrage; 35–70 yrs, propertied; account defaulters & kin disqualified).
- **Colonial Milestones:** **Madras Municipal Corporation (1687–88)** (first in India); **Bombay & Calcutta (1726)**. **Mayo (1870)** = financial decentralisation. **Ripon (18 May 1882)** = **"Magna Carta of Local Self-Government"** (*"Father of Local Self-Government"* — non-official majorities, political education). **Hobhouse Royal Commission (1907–09)** = revive village panchayats. **GoI Act 1919** = **"Transferred" subject**; **GoI Act 1935** = Provincial subject → **Entry 5, State List**.
- **Gandhi vs Ambedkar & `Art. 40`:** **Gandhi** (*Harijan*, 1942/1946) championed ***Gram Swaraj*** (*"oceanic circle"*). **Ambedkar** (**4 Nov 1948**) warned villages were *"a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness and communalism."* **K. Santhanam's** compromise (**22 Nov 1948**) placed village panchayats in non-justiciable **`Art. 40` (DPSP)**.
- **CDP (1952) & NES (1953):** Top-down bureaucratic schemes lacked popular participation → **Balwantrai Mehta Study Team (1957)**.
- **Balwantrai Mehta (1957):** Coined **"democratic decentralisation"** (Prelims 2015); **3-tier** (**Gram Panchayat** direct; **Panchayat Samiti** block = **executive**; **Zila Parishad** = **advisory**, chaired by **Collector**). Launched at **Nagaur, Rajasthan (2 Oct 1959)**, then **AP (Nov 1959)**. *(Santhanam panels: 1963 Finances; 1965 Elections.)*
- **Ashok Mehta (1977–78):** **2-tier** (**Zila Parishad = executive** + **Mandal Panchayat**, 15k–20k pop.); **official political party participation**; compulsory taxes; social audits; **Nyaya Panchayats**; State PRI Minister; **FIRST call for constitutional recognition**.
- **1978–88 Committees:** **Dantwala (1978)** = Block planning; **Hanumantha Rao (1984)** = separate District Planning body (Minister/Collector); **G.V.K. Rao (1985)** = **"grass without roots"**, **DDC** as ZP CEO, regular polls; **L.M. Singhvi (1986)** = **new constitutional chapter**, **Gram Sabha** as direct democracy, **judicial tribunals**; **Thungon (1988)** = 3-tier, 5-yr term, SC/ST/women quota, **SFC**, Collector ZP CEO; **Gadgil (1988)** = **direct 73rd Amdt blueprint** (**direct polls at all 3 tiers**, 5-yr term, quotas, subject list, taxes, **SFC & SEC**).
- **1989–93 Legislative Arc:** Rajiv Gandhi's **64th/65th Bills (1989)** fell in RS; V.P. Singh's **74th Bill (1990)** lapsed; Narasimha Rao's Bills (**Mirdha** & **Singh Deo** JPCs) passed **22–23 Dec 1992**, ratified by **17 States**, assented **20 Apr 1993**: **73rd Act** effective **`24 April 1993`** (**National Panchayati Raj Day**; Karnataka first Act, MP first polls); **74th Act** effective **`1 June 1993`**.

## 2. The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 — Panchayats (Part IX and Eleventh Schedule)

- **Scope & Article Map (`243–243O`):** Part IX + **11th Schedule (29 items, `243G`)** + **`280(3)(bb)`**. `243` Definitions (*village* by **Governor**); `243A` **Gram Sabha**; `243B` 3-tier; `243C` Polls; `243D` Quotas; `243E` Term; `243F` Disqualifications; `243G` Powers; `243H` Taxes; `243I` **SFC**; `243J` Audit; `243K` **SEC**; `243L` UTs (**President**); `243M` Exemptions; `243N` Continuance; `243O` Court Bar.
- **Gram Sabha (`243(b), 243A`):** **All registered voters** in village electoral roll (*not* all adults); powers defined by **State Legislature**.
- **Tiers, Polls & Voting (`243B–C`):** Mandatory **3 tiers** (2-tier allowed ONLY if State pop. **`≤ 20 lakh`** — Goa, Sikkim, Manipur). **All seats directly elected**; Intermediate & District Chairpersons **indirectly elected**; **Village Chairperson** mode by State. **`243C(4)` grants ALL members (incl. ex-officio MPs/MLAs) RIGHT TO VOTE** in meetings!
- **Reservations & Triple Test (`243D`):** Proportional **SC/ST** & **`≥ 1/3rd` Women** (incl. SC/ST women) in seats & Chairpersons; **21 States & 2 UTs** at **50%** (Bihar first 2006; **>15L women, ~46.6%**). **Sunset (`243D(5)`):** SC/ST tied to **`Art. 334` (25 Jan 2030)**; **women's quota permanent!** **`83rd Amend 2000` (`243M(3A)`)** exempts **Arunachal Pradesh** from SC quota. **OBC (`243D(6)`)** voluntary — bound by SC **Triple Test** (*Krishna Murthy* 2010, *Vikas Gawali* 2021, *Suresh Mahajan* 2022): (1) empirical commission on political backwardness, (2) body-wise quota, (3) **SC+ST+OBC `≤ 50%`**; polls cannot await compliance.
- **Duration & Age (`243E–F`):** **5-yr term** (*"and no longer"*); re-poll within **6 months** of dissolution; reconstituted body serves **ONLY remainder** (no poll if **`< 6 months`** left — Prelims 2016!). Min age = **21 years** (NOT 25!).
- **SFC, SEC & Courts (`243I, 243K, 243O`):** **SFC** every 5 yrs by **Governor** (+ ATR in Legislature). **SEC** (ECI has NO role) appointed by **Governor**, **removed ONLY like a High Court Judge** (by **President**); ***Fouziya Imtiaz Sheikh* (2021)** barred serving bureaucrats as SECs. **`243O`** bars courts in delimitation; disputes via **election petition** only.
- **Compulsory vs Voluntary:** **Compulsory** = Gram Sabha, 3-tier (`≤20L` exception), direct seats/indirect upper chairs, SC/ST & `≥1/3rd` women, 21 yrs, 5-yr term/6-mo re-poll, SEC, SFC. **Voluntary** = Gram Sabha powers, Village Chair election, MP/MLA seats, OBC/50% women quota, **29 11th Schedule items (`243G` *"may"*)**, local taxes (`243H`), audit (`243J`).
- **Exemptions (`243M`):** **Nagaland, Meghalaya, Mizoram** (opt-in via State Assembly special majority `243M(4)(a)`), **5th/6th Schedules**, **Manipur Hill Areas**, Darjeeling Gorkha Hill Council district tier, Arunachal SC quota. Parliament extends via **ordinary law (`243M(4)(b)`, NOT Art. 368)**.

## 3. The PESA Act, 1996 — Tribal Self-Rule in Fifth Schedule Areas

- **Scope:** **Dileep Singh Bhuria Committee (1995)**; enacted **`24 Dec 1996`** under **`243M(4)(b)`**. Applies **ONLY to Fifth Schedule Areas in 10 States** (AP, CG, GJ, HP, JH, MP, MH, OD, RJ, TS — 45 full & 63 partial districts, 663 blocks, 22,141 GPs, 77,564 villages); NOT Sixth Schedule.
- **Gram Sabha Powers (`Sec. 4`):** Hamlet/habitation Gram Sabha (`4(b)`) preserves customs, cultural identity, resources & customary justice (`4(d)`); approves plans & beneficiaries (`4(e)`); issues **utilisation certificates** (`4(f)`); **mandatory consultation** on land acquisition & rehab (`4(i)` — **prior CONSENT** under **LARR 2013 `Sec. 41`**); minor water bodies (`4(j)`); **prior recommendation** for **MINOR MINERALS ONLY** (`4(k)–(l)` — excludes major minerals!). **7 Endowments (`4(m)`):** intoxicants prohibition/regulation, **ownership of Minor Forest Produce (MFP)**, prevent/restore ST land alienation, manage village markets, control ST moneylending, oversee social-sector staff, control local/Tribal Sub-Plans.
- **ST Quotas (`4(g)–(h), (n)–(o)`):** ST seats **`≥ 50%`**; **100% Chairpersons at ALL levels** reserved for **STs** (*Rakesh Kumar* 2010); up to **`1/10th`** unrepresented ST nominations at upper tiers; anti-usurpation (`4(n)`); **Sixth Schedule** district pattern (`4(o)`).
- **Rulings & 2026 Status:** ***Samatha* (1997)** barred private mining leases on tribal land; ***Orissa Mining Corp. / Niyamgiri* (2013)** let **12 Dongria Kondh Gram Sabhas** (PESA + FRA) reject bauxite mining; **Mendha-Lekha** pioneered bamboo CFR. **2026 Rules:** **Jharkhand** notified rules **`2 Jan 2026`** → **9 of 10 states** notified; **Odisha sole pending state** (Lok Sabha reply, 4 Aug 2026). Gaps: non-harmonised Forest/Excise/Mining/Land laws, major-mineral gap, missing urban **MESA**.

## 4. The 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 — Municipalities (Part IXA and Twelfth Schedule)

- **Core Architecture:** Effective **`1 June 1993`**; **Part IXA (`243P–243ZG`)**, **12th Schedule (18 items, `243W`)**, **`280(3)(c)`**. Three Central Ministries: **MoHUA**, **Defence** (Cantonments), **MHA** (UTs).
- **3 Constitutional Types (`243Q`):** **Nagar Panchayat** (transitional), **Municipal Council** (smaller urban), **Municipal Corporation** (larger urban) — notified by **Governor** on 5 criteria; **Industrial Township** exemption (Jamshedpur).
- **Polls, Non-Voting Trap & Wards Committees (`243R–S`):** Direct ward polls; **Mayor/Chair mode** by State Legislature. **Nominated municipal experts (`243R(2)(a)(i)`) have NO voting right** (contrast `243C(4)`!). **Wards Committees (`243S`)** mandatory at **`≥ 3 lakh`** pop.
- **Quotas, Term, Shared Bodies & Exemptions (`243T–ZC, ZG`):** Proportional SC/ST & **`≥ 1/3rd` women** (SC/ST sunset **Art. 334 [2030]**, **women permanent**; OBC voluntary + **Triple Test**); **5-yr term** (*Kishansing Tomar* 2006, 5-Judge Bench), 6-mo re-poll for remainder, **21 yrs** min age (`243V`). Shares the **SAME SFC (`243Y/243I`)** & **SEC (`243ZA/243K`)** as PRIs! **`243ZC`** exempts 5th/6th Schedules & Darjeeling — **NO urban MESA enacted**. **`243ZG`** bars delimitation suits.
- **DPC (`243ZD`) vs MPC (`243ZE`):** **DPC** (every district; merges PRI + ULB plans; **`≥ 4/5ths [80%]`** elected by District Panchayat + Municipal members). **MPC** (every **Metro Area `≥ 10 lakh`**, `1+` districts, `2+` ULBs/PRIs [`243P(c)`]; **`≥ 2/3rds [66.7%]`** elected by Municipal members + **Panchayat Chairpersons**).
- **Twelfth Schedule (18 Items):** Urban/town planning, land-use, socio-economic planning, roads/bridges, water supply, sanitation/SWM, **fire services**, **urban forestry/ecology**, weaker sections, slums, urban poverty, parks, culture, burials/crematoriums, cattle pounds, **vital statistics**, street lighting/parking/bus stops, **slaughter houses/tanneries** (NO Police!).
- **8 Urban Local Bodies:** (1) **Municipal Corporation** (ceremonial Mayor; State-appointed **IAS Commissioner** = real executive); (2) **Municipality**; (3) **Notified Area Committee** (Gazette notification, **100% nominated**); (4) **Town Area Committee** (State Act, semi-municipal); (5) **Cantonment Board** (**`Cantonments Act, 2006`**, **Union List Entry 3**, **Ministry of Defence**, 4 categories, **station commander = ex-officio President**, **IDES EO appointed by President**; **Khas Yol** denotified **27 Apr 2023** [62→**61**]; 10 of 58 draft notifications Mar 2024); (6) **Township** (PSU, 0 elected); (7) **Major Port Authority (`2021 Act`)**; (8) **Special Purpose Agency** (**function-based** — DDA, BDA, MMRDA, Water/Housing Boards).
- **Central Council of Local Government (`Art. 263`, 1954):** Presidential Order; both rural & urban until **1958**; **URBAN ONLY since 1958**; advisory; chaired by **Union Housing & Urban Affairs Minister**.

## 5. The 3Fs (Functions, Funds, Functionaries), Local Finances and Governance Reforms

- **Functions:** Discretionary **"may"** (`243G/243W`), weak **Activity Mapping**, parallel parastatals/Smart City SPVs, dormant DPCs/MPCs, & **Census Towns** (nearly half of 7,933 urban settlements run as GPs; only **28% [2,216 towns]** have Master Plans — **NITI Aayog 2021**).
- **Own-Revenue Crisis (RBI 2022–24):** All ULBs ~**1% GDP** (vs **7.4% Brazil**, **6% South Africa**). Across **232 MCs (RBI Nov 2024)**: revenue **0.6% GDP** (vs 9.2% Centre, 14.6% States); top 10 take **>58%**; own tax ~**30%**; **property tax** **0.15% GDP** (vs **1.7% OECD**); post-octroi/GST erosion. **RBI PRI Report (Jan 2024):** own tax **~1–2%** (₹737 cr / ₹35,354 cr), **~95% grant reliance** (80% Union, 15% State).
- **SFCs & 16th FC Call:** Chronic SFC delays, non-synchronisation & ignored ATRs → **16th FC (2026)** recommended amending **`Arts. 280(3)(bb)/(c)`** to drop *"on the basis of [SFC] recommendations"* while keeping timely SFCs an entry condition & asking NITI Aayog to publish SFC best practices.
- **15th FC (2021–26) vs 16th FC (2026–31, 1 Feb 2026):** **15th FC:** **₹4,36,361 cr** (RLB ₹2.37L cr, ULB ₹1.21L cr, Health ₹70k cr, New Cities ₹8k cr). **16th FC (Panagariya):** **₹7,91,493 cr (+81%)**: **RLB ₹4,35,236 cr** (Basic ₹3,48,188 cr + Perf. ₹87,048 cr; 90% 2026 rural pop. + 10% area) + **ULB ₹3,56,257 cr** (Basic ₹2,32,125 cr + Perf. ₹58,032 cr + **₹56,100 cr Wastewater** [10–40L cities] + **₹10,000 cr Urbanisation Premium** [@ ₹2,000/person for village mergers into `≥1L` ULBs]; 90% 2026 urban pop. + **10% ULB OSR**). **60:40** Basic+Perf. RLB:ULB (overall urban ~45%); **80% Basic** (50% untied / 50% tied to sanitation/SWM/water) + **20% Perf.**; **3 entry conditions** (elected bodies, online accounts, timely SFCs) + GIS property tax, CAG TGS & Appendix III disclosure.
- **Municipal Bonds & Capital:** SEBI 2015 + AMRUT 2.0; **Ahmedabad (1998)** first non-guaranteed, **Indore (Feb 2023)** first retail green muni bond; **RBI (Oct–Nov 2025)** permitted municipal debt as **repo/reverse-repo collateral**; **₹1L cr Urban Challenge Fund**; record **FY 2025–26** (~₹1,756–2,000 cr), yet only **0.06–0.09%** of corporate bonds (vs 14% GDP US); **TNUDF** pooled finance model.
- **Functionaries, *Pradhan Pati*, PDI & Tech:** Missing local cadres; **1 planner/75,000 urban citizens**; IAS Commissioner vs ceremonial 1-yr Mayor. **Sushil Kumar panel (5 Feb 2025):** **"exemplary penalties"**, Gram Sabha public oath, women's ombudspersons, **Panchayat NIRNAY**, **10-yr two-term rotation**. **PDI 2024 (13 Feb 2025):** **39.9% → 43.9%**; Top 5: **Karnataka (72.23), Kerala (70.59), Tamil Nadu (68.38), Maharashtra (61.44), UP (60.07)**. Tech: **PAI 2.0**, **eGramSwaraj** (>₹3L cr), **SVAMITVA** (>3.10 cr cards), **SabhaSaar AI** (14 Aug 2025, 23 languages).
- **2nd ARC (6th Report, Oct 2007) & Way Forward:** **Principle of Subsidiarity**; statutory **Activity Mapping**; **directly elected 5-yr Executive Mayor**; **Area Sabhas** (Model Nagar Raj Bill); active **DPCs/MPCs** & parastatal merger; synchronised **SFCs** + 16th FC rules; **GIS property tax**, pooled finance & local GST share; dedicated local cadres + **CAG TGS** + **Ombudsman**; PESA harmonisation & **MESA**.
