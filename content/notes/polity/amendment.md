## 1. Solid enough to govern, flexible enough to grow

Every written constitution faces a dilemma: make it too easy to alter and a passing majority will rewrite the rules of the Republic; make it too rigid and a changing society will outgrow its fundamental law. The framers consciously chose a middle path. Speaking in the Constituent Assembly, Jawaharlal Nehru explained why permanence must leave room for change:

> "While we want this Constitution to be as solid and as permanent a structure as we can make it, nevertheless there is no permanence in Constitutions. There should be a certain flexibility. If you make anything rigid and permanent, you stop a nation's growth, the growth of a living, vital, organic people." — Jawaharlal Nehru, Constituent Assembly, 8 November 1948

India therefore adopted a **blend of rigidity and flexibility**—neither as rigid as the United States Constitution nor as flexible as the unwritten British one. **K.C. Wheare** admired the range of procedures India provides: "this variety in the amending process is wise but is rarely found." The formal amending power sits in **Part XX, Article 368**, borrowed from the **Constitution of South Africa** (with India adding state ratification for federal provisions). Up to **November 2026**, Parliament has enacted **106 Constitutional Amendment Acts**—the latest being the 106th Amendment Act, 2023 on women's reservation, brought into force in April 2026.

## 2. How Article 368 works: procedure step by step

Formal amendments under Article 368 depart from ordinary lawmaking at five crucial stages:

1. **Initiation:** A bill may be introduced in **either House of Parliament**—Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha—and **never in a state legislature**. It may be moved by a **minister or a private member**, and needs **no prior permission of the President**.
2. **Special majority in each House separately:** Each House must pass it by a **majority of its total membership** (sanctioned strength, irrespective of vacancies or absentees) **and** a majority of **not less than two-thirds of members present and voting**.
3. **No joint sitting:** Each House must pass the bill independently. Article 108 does **not** apply, so disagreement between the two Houses defeats the bill.
4. **Ratification by states (federal provisions only):** Amendments touching the federal clauses in the proviso to Article 368(2) must be ratified by resolutions of **not less than one-half of the state legislatures** by a **simple majority** (majority of members present and voting) before reaching the President. The Constitution sets **no time limit** for ratification.
5. **Mandatory presidential assent:** The **24th Amendment (1971)** amended Article 368(2) to stipulate that the President "shall give his assent"—he can neither withhold assent nor return the bill for reconsideration.

> **Prelims pointer:** Five classic traps: a **private member** may introduce an amendment bill; **no prior presidential permission** is needed; **no joint sitting** is permitted; presidential assent is **mandatory** since 1971; and **Bill numbers differ from Act numbers** because lapsed or defeated bills consume numbers (the 122nd Bill became the 101st Act; the 128th Bill became the 106th Act).

## 3. Three tracks to amend the Constitution

Although Article 368 prescribes **two** formal methods, the Constitution can be modified along **three** tracks, because several provisions are changeable by ordinary parliamentary legislation outside Article 368.

```text
 ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ Track 1: SIMPLE MAJORITY (outside Art. 368)            │
 │ >50% present & voting · e.g. Arts. 2-4, 169, 5th/6th S │
 └──────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────┘
                            │
 ┌──────────────────────────▼─────────────────────────────┐
 │ Track 2: SPECIAL MAJORITY (Art. 368)                   │
 │ >50% total membership + 2/3 present & voting, EACH     │
 │ House separately · e.g. FRs, DPSPs, most provisions    │
 └──────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────┘
                            │
 ┌──────────────────────────▼─────────────────────────────┐
 │ Track 3: SPECIAL MAJORITY + STATE RATIFICATION         │
 │ Track 2 + >= half of state legislatures (simple maj.)  │
 │ e.g. Arts. 54, 55, 73, 162, 241, 279A, SC/HC, 7th Sch. │
 └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Track 1 (simple majority outside Article 368)** covers ordinary laws expressly declared not to be Article 368 amendments: admission, reorganisation or renaming of states (**Articles 2–4**, illustrated by the **Kerala (Alteration of Name) Act, 2026**, renaming Kerala as **Keralam** from 25 August 2026); state legislative councils (**Article 169**); **Second, Fifth and Sixth Schedules**; **citizenship** (Article 11); parliamentary **quorum** (Article 100(3)), privileges (Article 105(3)), salaries (Article 106), rules of procedure (Article 118) and use of English (Article 120); official language (Article 343); number of puisne Supreme Court judges (Article 124(1)) and additional SC jurisdiction (Article 138); and delimitation, elections and Union territories.

**Track 2 (special majority of Parliament)** governs the bulk of the Constitution—including **Fundamental Rights**, **Directive Principles**, and all provisions outside Tracks 1 and 3—requiring both special-majority thresholds in each House separately.

**Track 3 (special majority plus ratification by at least half the states)** protects the federal provisions in the **proviso to Article 368(2)**: Presidential election (**Articles 54 and 55**); Union (**Article 73**) and state (**Article 162**) executive power; UT High Courts (**Article 241**); the **GST Council** (**Article 279A**, added to the proviso by the **101st Amendment, 2016**); the Supreme Court and High Courts (Chapter IV, Part V; Chapter V, Part VI); legislative distribution (Chapter I, Part XI) and **any Seventh Schedule List**; state representation in Parliament (**Fourth Schedule**); and **Article 368 itself**.

| Feature | Track 1: Simple majority | Track 2: Special majority | Track 3: Special + states |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Art. 368?** | No (ordinary law) | Yes | Yes (proviso to Art. 368(2)) |
| **Vote** | >50% present & voting | >50% total + 2/3 present & voting (each House) | Same as Track 2 + ≥ half states (simple maj., no deadline) |
| **Joint sitting** | Possible (Art. 108) | Not permitted | Not permitted |
| **Assent** | Ordinary veto (Art. 111) | Mandatory (24th Amdt) | Mandatory (24th Amdt) |
| **Examples** | Arts. 2–4, 11, 169; 2nd/5th/6th Sch. | FRs, DPSPs, 86th, 103rd, 106th | Arts. 54–55, 73, 162, 241, 279A, SC/HC, 7th Sch., Art. 368 |

## 4. Criticism and constitutional limits

Scholars highlight six defects in Article 368's procedure. India creates **no special constitutional convention**—ordinary Parliament acts as constituent body. **States cannot initiate** amendments (save resolutions for legislative councils under Article 169), and **most of the Constitution is amendable by Parliament alone**. There is **no time frame** for state ratification and silence on whether a state may rescind consent. The **absence of a joint sitting** lets one House block widely supported changes, while **sketchy wording** leaves courts and conventions to fill procedural gaps.

Beyond arithmetic, Parliament's constituent power meets **two binding locks**:

- **Substantive limit (basic structure):** In ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)**, the Supreme Court held that Article 368 cannot alter or destroy the Constitution's **basic structure** (explored in full in the dedicated Basic Structure note). That lock struck down the **99th Amendment (2014)** creating the NJAC on 16 October 2015 (4:1).
- **Procedural limit (federal ratification):** In ***Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah* (20 July 2021)**, a three-judge bench struck down **Part IXB** of the **97th Amendment (2011)** insofar as it governed **state co-operative societies** (Entry 32, List II) for want of ratification by half the states under Article 368(2)—holding that even a substantial "change in effect" on a State List entry requires ratification. By **2:1** (Justices R.F. Nariman and B.R. Gavai; Justice K.M. Joseph dissenting on severability), the Court preserved Part IXB for **multi-State co-operative societies** while leaving Article 19(1)(c) and Article 43B untouched.

## 5. Major constitutional amendments: 1951 to 2015

India's amendment history is easiest to master in two layers: the foundational 1951–1978 arc and the thematic amendment families from 1967 to 2015.

### Foundation, integration and the Emergency arc (1951–1978)

| Amendment | Core changes |
|---|---|
| **1st (1951)** | Art. 15(4) (backward classes); **Arts. 31A, 31B & Ninth Schedule** (land reforms); three grounds added to Art. 19(2) |
| **7th (1956)** | States Reorganisation: **14 states & 6 UTs**; common High Courts; Art. 350A |
| **9th (1960)** | Transferred Berubari Union to Pakistan after *Berubari* opinion (1960) |
| **10th, 12th, 14th (1961–62)** | Integrated **Dadra & Nagar Haveli** (10th), **Goa, Daman & Diu** (12th), **Puducherry** (14th, UT legislatures) |
| **24th (1971)** | Reversed *Golaknath*: FRs amendable (Art. 13(4)); mandatory presidential assent |
| **25th (1971)** | **Art. 31C** (39(b)–(c) shielded from Arts. 14 & 19); "amount" replaced "compensation" in Art. 31(2) |
| **26th (1971)** | Abolished **privy purses** and princely privileges |
| **35th & 36th (1974–75)** | Sikkim associate state (35th, Art. 2A), then **22nd full state** (36th) |
| **38th & 39th (1975)** | Barred judicial review of emergencies/ordinances (38th) and PM/President/VP/Speaker polls (39th, Art. 329A—struck down in *Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain*, 1975) |
| **42nd (1976)** | **"Mini-Constitution"**: *socialist*, *secular*, *integrity* in Preamble; **Part IVA** (Duties), **Part XIVA** (Tribunals); Art. 39(f) recast & 39A, 43A, 48A added; Art. 31C widened to all DPSPs; **Art. 368(4)–(5)**; 6-year terms; President bound by Cabinet (Art. 74); **five subjects** shifted to Concurrent List (education, forests, weights & measures, wild animals/birds, administration of justice) |
| **43rd (1977) & 44th (1978)** | Janata rollback: 43rd restored SC/HC review (repealing Arts. 31D, 32A, 131A, 144A, 226A, 228A); 44th restored 5-year terms, **armed rebellion** (Art. 352), written Cabinet advice, **Arts. 20–21** unsuspendable, property → **Art. 300A**, President allowed one reconsideration return, Art. 38(2) |

> **Remember:** The **44th Amendment did NOT repeal Articles 368(4)–(5)** or roll back the widened Article 31C. The Janata government's Bill tried to, but those clauses failed in the Rajya Sabha, and it was left to the Supreme Court to strike them down in ***Minerva Mills* (1980)**.

### Thematic amendment families (1967–2015)

**Eighth Schedule languages.** Three amendments expanded the Eighth Schedule from 14 to **22 languages**: the **21st (1967)** added Sindhi; the **71st (1992)** added Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali; and the **92nd (2003)** added Bodo, Dogri, Maithili and Santhali (while the **96th, 2011** changed "Oriya" to "Odia").

**Political integrity, franchise and local democracy.** The **52nd Amendment (1985)** inserted the **Tenth Schedule** (anti-defection law), and the **91st Amendment (2003)** later deleted its "split" exemption, barred defectors from ministerial office and capped Union and state councils of ministers at **15 per cent** of lower-House strength (minimum 12 in states). The **61st Amendment (1988)** lowered the voting age from 21 to **18** (Article 326). The **69th Amendment (1991)** gave the **National Capital Territory of Delhi** an elected legislature (Articles 239AA–239AB). Grassroots democracy entered the Constitution via the **73rd and 74th Amendments (1992)** for **Panchayats** (Part IX, Eleventh Schedule) and **Municipalities** (Part IXA, Twelfth Schedule). The **86th Amendment (2002)** added **Article 21A** (education for ages 6–14), recast Article 45 and inserted Duty 51A(k), while the **97th Amendment (2011)** constitutionalised co-operatives (Article 19(1)(c), Article 43B, Part IXB—partially invalidated in *Rajendra N. Shah*, 2021).

**Constitutional commissions.** The **65th Amendment (1990)** replaced the single special officer with a multi-member **NCSC-ST** (Article 338); the **89th Amendment (2003)** split it into **NCSC** (Article 338) and **NCST** (Article 338A); and the **102nd Amendment (2018)** added **NCBC** (Article 338B).

**Reservation and delimitation.** Four amendments shaped SC/ST reservation in promotions: the **77th (1995)** inserted Article 16(4A) for reservation in promotion; the **81st (2000)** inserted Article 16(4B) excluding **carry-forward backlog vacancies** from the 50 per cent ceiling; the **82nd (2000)** added a proviso to Article 335 permitting relaxed qualifying standards; and the **85th (2001)** conferred **consequential seniority** retrospectively from 17 June 1995. On representation, the **84th Amendment (2001)** froze state-wise Lok Sabha and Assembly seat totals (fixed on the **1971 census**) until the **first census after 2026**, permitting intra-state constituency readjustment on the **1991 census**; the **87th Amendment (2003)** shifted that intra-state readjustment to the **2001 census** while preserving frozen seat totals.

**Others Prelims picks up.** The **13th (1962)** made Nagaland a state with the special protection of Article 371A. The **15th (1963)** raised the retirement age of High Court judges from 60 to 62. The **58th (1987)** provided an authoritative text of the Constitution in Hindi. The **70th (1992)** brought the elected MLAs of Delhi and Puducherry into the electoral college for the President. The **80th (2000)** put all central taxes, other than cesses and surcharges, into one divisible pool shared with the states. The **93rd (2005)** added Article 15(5), allowing reservation in private educational institutions other than minority ones. The **94th (2006)** moved the requirement of a tribal-welfare minister from Bihar to Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh, and the **98th (2012)** added Article 371J for the Hyderabad–Karnataka region.

**Judiciary and external border.** The **99th Amendment (2014)** replaced the collegium with the **NJAC** (Articles 124A–124C), struck down **4:1** on **16 October 2015** in *SCAORA v. Union of India* as violating judicial independence. The **100th Amendment (2015)** operationalised the 1974 **India–Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement** (and 2011 Protocol), exchanging enclaves and adverse possessions.

## 6. Recent amendments (2016–2026) and pending bills

The six amendments enacted since 2016 have reshaped fiscal federalism, affirmative action and women's representation.

**101st Amendment (2016, 122nd Bill) — GST.** Ratified by over half the states, it launched GST on **1 July 2017**—India's most ambitious cooperative-federal reform. It inserted **Article 246A** (concurrent GST power; exclusive Parliament power over inter-state supply), **Article 269A** (IGST levy and apportionment) and **Article 279A** (**GST Council**, entrenched in Article 368(2)'s proviso), and revised Seventh Schedule tax entries.

**102nd Amendment (2018, 123rd Bill) and 105th Amendment (2021, 127th Bill) — NCBC and state OBC lists.** The 102nd constitutionalised the **NCBC** (**Article 338B**) and added **Article 342A** (presidential SEBC notification) and Article 366(26C). On **5 May 2021**, in ***Dr. Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v. Chief Minister***, a five-judge bench ruled **3:2** that Article 342A gave the President and Parliament exclusive authority to identify SEBCs, stripping states of separate OBC lists. Parliament promptly passed the **105th Amendment (2021)**, amending Articles **338B(9)**, **342A** (confining 342A(1)–(2) to the Central List and adding **Article 342A(3)**) and **366(26C)** to restore state and UT OBC lists without mandatory NCBC consultation.

**103rd Amendment (2019, 124th Bill) — EWS quota.** Inserted **Articles 15(6) and 16(6)** enabling up to **10 per cent reservation** in education (including private unaided non-minority institutions) and public jobs for **Economically Weaker Sections** outside SCs, STs and non-creamy-layer OBCs. On **7 November 2022**, in ***Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India***, five judges upheld it **3:2** (Justices Maheshwari, Trivedi, Pardiwala; CJI U.U. Lalit and Justice S. Ravindra Bhat dissenting), ruling economic criteria do not violate basic structure and the 50 per cent ceiling is not inflexible.

**104th Amendment (Act of 2019, 126th Bill).** In force from 25 January 2020, it extended SC/ST legislative seat reservation to **80 years** (until **25 January 2030**, amending Article 334) and **discontinued nominated Anglo-Indian seats** (Articles 331 and 333).

**106th Amendment (2023, 128th Bill) — *Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam*.** Assented on 28 September 2023, it reserves **one-third of seats for women** (including within SC/ST quotas) in the Lok Sabha (**Article 330A**), state assemblies (**Article 332A**) and Delhi assembly (**Article 239AA**), with a **15-year sunset** (**Article 334A**) and rotation after each delimitation. Brought into force on **16 April 2026**, operational rollout awaits delimitation following the first census after commencement. No 107th Amendment Act has been enacted as of November 2026.

> **In the news:** Three major amendment bills shaped Parliament between December 2024 and November 2026. The **129th Amendment Bill, 2024** (*One Nation, One Election*, introduced **17 December 2024** after the Kovind committee report) proposes **Article 82A** and amends Articles **83, 172 and 327** (with a companion bill for Delhi, Puducherry and J&K); it remains before a JPC chaired by **P.P. Chaudhary** (extended to the Winter Session of 2026). On 1 October 2026 the Supreme Court cancelled an informal discussion the panel had planned with its judges, because the Bill may one day come before the Court in judicial review. The **130th Amendment Bill, 2025** (introduced **20 August 2025**, amending Articles **75, 164 and 239AA** on removing ministers detained 30 consecutive days on serious charges) remains before a JPC chaired by **Aparajita Sarangi**, which has also been given until the Winter Session of 2026 to report. Most strikingly, the **131st Amendment Bill, 2026** (introduced **16 April 2026** to raise Lok Sabha seats to 850, lift the post-2026 freeze using 2011 census data and fast-track women's quota for 2029) was **defeated in the Lok Sabha on 17 April 2026**—298 for, 230 against out of 528 voting, short of the 352 two-thirds special majority under Article 368.

## 7. Evaluating the amending power

**106 amendments in seventy-six years** looks frequent beside the United States' twenty-seven, yet most reflect administrative housekeeping, territorial integration and decennial SC/ST quota extensions needed in a detailed constitution governing both Union and states. The real tension pits **parliamentary sovereignty** against **constitutionalism**: Article 368's special majority blocks unilateral rewrites—vividly shown when the 131st Bill fell short in April 2026—while **basic structure** stops even supermajorities from dismantling judicial independence or democracy (*Minerva Mills*, *NJAC*), and ***Rajendra N. Shah* (2021)** proved that state ratification under Article 368(2) is an enforceable federal shield.

> **Mains angle:** Frame GS-2 answers on three axes: **constitutionalism vs. parliamentary sovereignty** (Article 368's arithmetic + basic structure); **living document vs. overuse** (nation-building 73rd/74th, 86th, 101st, 106th vs. Emergency-era 39th, 42nd); and **federal balance** (*Rajendra N. Shah*, GST Council entrenchment in Art. 279A, and federal stakes in simultaneous polls and post-2026 delimitation).
