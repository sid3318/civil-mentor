## 1. A constitution made by Indians

The idea that Indians should frame their own constitution, through an assembly of their own choosing, grew with the national movement. M.N. Roy, a pioneer of the communist movement in India, first put the idea forward in 1934. The Indian National Congress made it an official demand in 1935. In 1938 Jawaharlal Nehru declared, on the Congress's behalf, that the constitution of free India must be framed by a Constituent Assembly elected on the basis of adult franchise, without outside interference.

The British accepted the demand in principle in the **August Offer** of 1940. The **Cripps Mission** of 1942 brought a draft proposal for an elected constitution-making body after the Second World War, but the Muslim League rejected it, because it wanted India divided into two states with a constituent assembly for each. It was the **Cabinet Mission** of 1946 that finally laid down how the Constituent Assembly would be formed.

```text
1934  M.N. Roy proposes a Constituent Assembly
1935  Congress makes it an official demand
1938  Nehru: elected on adult franchise
1940  August Offer accepts it in principle
1942  Cripps Mission proposal, rejected
1946  Cabinet Mission plan; Assembly elected
```

## 2. How the Assembly was formed

The Cabinet Mission scheme was a compromise between speed and representativeness. Elections on universal adult franchise would have taken too long. The Assembly was instead elected indirectly, by the provincial legislative assemblies chosen in the elections of early 1946.

- **Total strength: 389.** Of these, 296 seats went to British India and 93 to the princely states. The 296 were split between 292 for the eleven Governors' provinces and 4 for the Chief Commissioners' provinces (Delhi, Ajmer-Merwara, Coorg and British Baluchistan).
- Seats were allotted to each province and princely state roughly in proportion to population, about one seat for every ten lakh people.
- Each province's seats were divided among three communities, **Muslims, Sikhs and General** (everyone else), in proportion to their population.
- The members of each community in the provincial legislative assembly elected that community's representatives, using **proportional representation by single transferable vote**.
- The heads of the princely states **nominated** their representatives.

The Assembly was therefore partly elected and partly nominated, and its elected members were chosen indirectly by legislatures that had themselves been elected on a limited franchise. In the elections of July–August 1946, the Congress won 208 of the 296 seats for British India, the Muslim League 73, and small groups and independents the remaining 15. The princely states stayed out at first.

Even so, the Assembly brought together nearly every section of Indian society: Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, Parsis, Anglo-Indians, Indian Christians, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and about 15 women, among them Sarojini Naidu, Hansa Mehta, Durgabai Deshmukh, Rajkumari Amrit Kaur and Dakshayani Velayudhan.

> **Prelims pointer:** The members of the Constituent Assembly were not directly elected by the people. They were elected indirectly by the provincial assemblies, by proportional representation (single transferable vote), and the princely states' members were nominated. Mahatma Gandhi was not a member.

## 3. The Assembly at work

The Assembly met for the first time on **9 December 1946**. The Muslim League boycotted it, and 211 members attended. Following French practice, the oldest member, **Dr Sachchidananda Sinha**, was elected temporary President. On 11 December **Dr Rajendra Prasad** was elected President and **H.C. Mukherjee** Vice-President; V.T. Krishnamachari later became a second Vice-President. **Sir B.N. Rau** was appointed the Assembly's constitutional adviser.

### The Objectives Resolution

On **13 December 1946** Nehru moved the historic **Objectives Resolution**, which set out the philosophy of the constitution to come. The Assembly adopted it unanimously on **22 January 1947**. The resolution declared that India would be an "independent sovereign republic". It said all power and authority of sovereign India would derive from the people. It promised all people justice (social, economic and political), equality of status and opportunity, and the freedoms of thought, expression, belief, faith, worship, vocation, association and action. And it pledged adequate safeguards for minorities, backward and tribal areas, and the depressed and other backward classes. Its modified version became the **Preamble**.

### What independence changed

The Indian Independence Act, 1947, changed the Assembly's position in three ways.

1. It became a fully **sovereign body**, free to frame any constitution and to repeal or alter any law made by the British Parliament for India.
2. It also became a **legislature**. When it met as the Dominion Legislature it was chaired by G.V. Mavalankar; when it met as the constitution-making body, by Rajendra Prasad. It continued in both roles until 26 November 1949.
3. The members from the areas that went to Pakistan withdrew, and the Assembly's strength fell from the planned 389 to **299**.

The Assembly did more than write the Constitution. It ratified India's membership of the Commonwealth (May 1949). It adopted the national flag (22 July 1947), and the national anthem and the national song (24 January 1950). And it elected Dr Rajendra Prasad as the first President of India (24 January 1950).

## 4. The committees

Most of the work was done in committees, which then reported to the full Assembly.

| Committee | Chairman |
|---|---|
| Union Powers Committee | Jawaharlal Nehru |
| Union Constitution Committee | Jawaharlal Nehru |
| States Committee (for negotiating with the princely states) | Jawaharlal Nehru |
| Provincial Constitution Committee | Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel |
| Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal and Excluded Areas | Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel |
| Drafting Committee | Dr B.R. Ambedkar |
| Rules of Procedure Committee | Dr Rajendra Prasad |
| Steering Committee | Dr Rajendra Prasad |

The Advisory Committee worked through sub-committees, which examiners often ask about:

| Sub-committee of the Advisory Committee | Chairman |
|---|---|
| Fundamental Rights | J.B. Kripalani |
| Minorities | H.C. Mukherjee |
| North-East Frontier Tribal Areas and Assam Excluded and Partially Excluded Areas | Gopinath Bardoloi |
| Excluded and Partially Excluded Areas (other than those in Assam) | A.V. Thakkar |

Among the smaller committees, the Order of Business Committee was chaired by K.M. Munshi, the House Committee by B. Pattabhi Sitaramayya, the Credentials Committee by Alladi Krishnaswamy Ayyar, and the ad hoc Committee on the National Flag by Rajendra Prasad.

### The Drafting Committee

The most important committee, the **Drafting Committee**, was set up on 29 August 1947. It had seven members:

1. Dr B.R. Ambedkar (Chairman)
2. N. Gopalaswamy Ayyangar
3. Alladi Krishnaswamy Ayyar
4. Dr K.M. Munshi
5. Syed Mohammad Saadullah
6. N. Madhava Rau, who replaced B.L. Mitter after he resigned on grounds of ill health
7. T.T. Krishnamachari, who replaced D.P. Khaitan after his death in 1948

The Committee prepared the first draft of the Constitution, taking into account the proposals of the various committees, and published it in February 1948. The people of India were given eight months to discuss it, and in the light of their comments a second draft was published in October 1948.

> **Remember:** "Father of the Indian Constitution" refers to Dr B.R. Ambedkar, for his role as chairman of the Drafting Committee and his piloting of the draft through the Assembly. He is also sometimes called a "modern Manu".

## 5. Enactment and enforcement

Ambedkar introduced the final draft on **4 November 1948**, its first reading. The Assembly then went through it clause by clause, in a second reading that lasted from 15 November 1948 to 17 October 1949. The third reading began on 14 November 1949, and on **26 November 1949** the motion that "the Constitution as settled by the Assembly be passed" was declared carried. 284 members signed it on 24 January 1950.

As adopted, the Constitution contained a Preamble, **395 Articles and 8 Schedules**. The making of it took 2 years, 11 months and 18 days, over 11 sessions. The framers studied the constitutions of about 60 countries, considered the draft for 114 days, and spent about ₹64 lakh.

### Two dates of commencement

Some provisions came into force on **26 November 1949** itself, because they were needed before the rest could work. These covered citizenship, elections, the provisional Parliament, and temporary and transitional matters. Among them were Articles 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 60, 324, 366, 367, 379, 380, 388, 391, 392 and 393. The remaining provisions, the major part of the Constitution, came into force on **26 January 1950**, the date of its commencement.

The date was chosen with care. On 26 January 1930 the Congress had celebrated the first "Purna Swaraj Day", following its Lahore session resolution of December 1929. With the Constitution's commencement, the Indian Independence Act, 1947, and the Government of India Act, 1935, were repealed, although the Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act, 1949, continued. The Constituent Assembly itself continued as the **provisional Parliament** until the first Lok Sabha was formed after the general elections of 1951–52.

> **Example:** The original Constitution was handwritten in a flowing italic style by Prem Behari Narain Raizada, and illuminated by artists of Santiniketan led by Nandalal Bose. The Hindi version was calligraphed by Vasant Krishan Vaidya. The elephant was adopted as the Assembly's symbol, or seal.

## 6. Was the Assembly truly representative?

The Assembly has been criticised on several grounds, and a Mains answer should weigh each against the response to it.

| Criticism | Response |
|---|---|
| **Not representative.** Its members were not elected directly on universal adult franchise | Direct election would have delayed independence. The Assembly drew in every major community and region, and its draft was opened to public comment for eight months |
| **Not sovereign.** It was created by British proposals and, at first, worked within British limits | The Independence Act made it fully sovereign, and its authority came from the people it claimed to speak for, as the Preamble says |
| **Dominated by the Congress.** Granville Austin observed that the Assembly was a one-party body in an essentially one-party country | The Congress itself was a broad coalition, and it deliberately brought in outsiders and critics, Ambedkar among them |
| **Dominated by lawyers and politicians** | Their expertise helped produce a precise legal document |
| **Too slow.** Nearly three years | The framers were building institutions for a vast and divided country; deliberation was the point |

Granville Austin also described the Assembly's method as "consensus and accommodation": most decisions were reached through discussion and compromise rather than by majority vote. This is one of the strongest arguments for the Constitution's legitimacy.

Ambedkar's last speech to the Assembly, on 25 November 1949, is one of the most quoted texts in Mains answers. He warned that political democracy could not last unless it rested on social democracy:

> "On the 26th of January 1950, we are going to enter into a life of contradictions. In politics we will have equality and in social and economic life we will have inequality." — B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 25 November 1949

In the same speech he cautioned against hero-worship in politics and against "the grammar of anarchy", by which he meant unconstitutional methods of protest once constitutional ones were available.

> **Mains angle:** Use the making of the Constitution to discuss legitimacy, deliberation and nation-building (GS-1, post-independence India). The Assembly turned the values of the freedom struggle into institutions, accommodated minorities through discussion, and left a document that still draws its authority from "We, the people". Balance the criticisms against the method of consensus, and end with Ambedkar's warning: political equality needs social and economic equality to survive.

> **In the news:** On 26 November 2024, Constitution Day (Samvidhan Divas), the 75th anniversary of the Constitution's adoption was marked by a joint sitting of both Houses of Parliament in Samvidhan Sadan, addressed by President Droupadi Murmu. Versions of the Constitution in Sanskrit and Maithili were released, along with a commemorative coin and a postage stamp. Constitution Day has been observed on 26 November since 2015.
