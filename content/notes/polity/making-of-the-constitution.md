## 1. Evolution of the Demand for a Constituent Assembly

The making of the Indian Constitution was neither an abrupt post-independence improvisation nor a constitutional gift bestowed by Westminster. It was the culmination of a half-century struggle for **constitutional self-determination**—the insistence that the fundamental law governing hundreds of millions of Indians could derive its legitimacy only from an assembly of Indians themselves, not from statutes drafted in Whitehall and enacted by the British Parliament. Every major colonial enactment from the `Indian Councils Act of 1861` through the `Government of India Act of 1935` had rested on the premise of **British parliamentary supremacy**: Britain decided when, how far, and under what safeguards Indians might participate in governing their own country.

The demand for an indigenous **Constituent Assembly** directly overturned that colonial premise. By asserting that Indians must write their own constitution without external interference, the national movement bridged **constitutional politics** and **mass sovereignty**—transforming the freedom struggle from a plea for administrative concessions into the founding act of a modern republic. For UPSC aspirants, this evolutionary arc connects **GS Paper I (Modern Indian History & Post-Independence Consolidation)** with **GS Paper II (Indian Polity)**, while providing some of the most frequently tested chronological and conceptual distinctions in the Prelims examination.

### M.N. Roy's Pioneering Proposal (1934) & INC Official Demand (1935)

#### Early Nationalist Precedents (1895–1934)

Long before the formal term "Constituent Assembly" entered Indian political vocabulary, nationalist leaders articulated the core principle of Indian constitutional authorship. As early as **`1895`**, the **Constitution of India Bill**—widely known as the **Swaraj Bill** and inspired by **Bal Gangadhar Tilak**—sketched an indigenous vision of home rule, fundamental liberties, and elected legislatures. Two decades later, the First World War and the principle of national self-determination gave the demand sharper political urgency.

On **`5 January 1922`**, writing in *Young India*, **Mahatma Gandhi** laid down the moral foundation of Indian constitutionalism: *"Swaraj will not be a free gift of the British Parliament; it will be a declaration of India's full self-expression. That it will be expressed through an Act of Parliament is true, but it will be merely a courteous ratification of the declared wish of the people of India."* In **`1924`**, **Motilal Nehru** moved a resolution in the Central Legislative Assembly on behalf of the Swaraj Party demanding a representative Round Table Conference to frame a scheme of full responsible government, while the **`Commonwealth of India Bill (1925)`** (drafted under Annie Besant and Tej Bahadur Sapru) and the landmark **`Nehru Report (August 1928)`** demonstrated that Indians possessed both the legal sophistication and political vision to author their own constitutional blueprint.

> [!example]
> **Why the Nehru Report (1928) Was Not Yet a Constituent Assembly:** Although Motilal Nehru's 1928 Report was India's first indigenous constitutional draft—anticipating universal adult suffrage, nineteen Fundamental Rights, and joint electorates—it was produced by an **All-Parties Conference committee**, not an elected Constituent Assembly. Its deadlock over communal amendments convinced younger nationalists like Jawaharlal Nehru that only an assembly elected directly by the people could command unchallengeable national legitimacy.

#### M.N. Roy (1934) and Official Congress Endorsement (1935–1936)

The explicit political proposal for a **Constituent Assembly** to frame India's Constitution was put forward for the **first time in `1934`** by **`M.N. Roy`** (Manabendra Nath Roy), a pioneering theorist of the communist movement in India and later the founder of Radical Humanism. Roy recognised that British-sponsored Round Table Conferences were designed to pit Indian communities and princely rulers against one another under imperial arbitration; only a sovereign Constituent Assembly rooted in the Indian masses could break the colonial stalemate.

In **`1935`**, the **Indian National Congress (INC)** formally adopted the idea and made the demand for a Constituent Assembly an **official party demand**—rejecting the newly enacted `Government of India Act of 1935` precisely because it had been imposed from London without Indian consent. At the **Swaraj Party conference (`May 1934`)** and the **Congress Working Committee resolution (`June 1934`)**, Congress declared that the only satisfactory alternative to the British White Paper was a constitution drawn up by a Constituent Assembly elected on the basis of adult suffrage or as near it as possible. The demand was forcefully reiterated at the **Lucknow Session (`April 1936`)** and the **Faizpur Session (`December 1936`)**, both presided over by Jawaharlal Nehru, and later at the **Haripura Session (`1938`)** and **Tripuri Session (`1939`)**.

### Jawaharlal Nehru's 1938 Declaration on Adult Franchise & Freedom from Outside Interference

In **`1938`**, **Jawaharlal Nehru**, speaking authoritatively on behalf of the Indian National Congress, gave the demand its sharpest democratic formulation. He declared that **"the Constitution of free India must be framed, without outside interference, by a Constituent Assembly elected on the basis of adult franchise."**

Two principles embedded in Nehru's 1938 declaration carried profound constitutional weight:

- **Freedom from Outside Interference (Sovereignty):** The British Parliament could neither dictate the composition of the constitution-making body nor reserve a veto over its final document. Any treaty between Britain and free India had to be negotiated on equal terms *after* Indians had settled their own polity.
- **Universal Adult Franchise (Popular Legitimacy):** By insisting on election through adult suffrage rather than the property- and tax-restricted electorates of `1919` and `1935`, Nehru sought to bypass sectarian elites and anchor the future republic in direct popular consent. Ironically, as Partition loomed in `1946`, Congress had to compromise temporarily on direct adult-franchise elections for the Constituent Assembly in order to expedite the transfer of power—yet it redeemed Nehru's `1938` pledge in full by embedding **universal adult suffrage** directly into `Article 326` of the Constitution itself.

### August Offer (1940), Cripps Proposals (1942) & Acceptance via Cabinet Mission Plan (May 1946)

For years, Britain dismissed the Constituent Assembly demand as an unrealistic Congress slogan. Only the existential crisis of the **Second World War (`1939–1945`)**, the resignation of Congress provincial ministries in October–November `1939`, and the rapid Japanese advance across Southeast Asia compelled London to move—in three reluctant, escalating stages—from conditional concession to an operational blueprint.

#### The August Offer (`8 August 1940`)

Desperate to secure Indian cooperation in the war effort after the fall of France, Viceroy **Lord Linlithgow** issued the **August Offer** on **`8 August 1940`**. For the **first time**, the British Government accepted the demand for a Constituent Assembly **in principle**. It promised that after the war, a representative body would be set up to frame a new constitution, conceding that constitution-making should be **"primarily the responsibility of Indians themselves."**

However, the Offer fell far short of nationalist expectations on three counts: it spoke only of **Dominion Status** rather than complete independence; it used the qualifying word **"primarily"** (implying continued British participation and oversight); and it explicitly handed a **communal veto** to minorities by promising that Britain would not transfer authority to any system of government whose authority was *"directly denied by large and powerful elements in India's national life."* Congress rejected the offer—Nehru remarking that the Dominion Status concept was *"as dead as a doornail"*—and Mahatma Gandhi launched **Individual Satyagraha (`October 1940`)**.

#### The Cripps Proposals (`March 1942`)

Following the collapse of British Singapore (`February 1942`) and growing American and Chinese pressure on Prime Minister Winston Churchill, **Sir Stafford Cripps**—a member of the British War Cabinet—arrived in India in **`March 1942`** with a draft declaration proposing an independent Constitution to be framed **after the Second World War**.

The Cripps Proposals marked a decisive advance over the August Offer: constitution-making was now to be placed **solely** (not merely "primarily") in Indian hands through a **Constitution-Making Body** whose British Indian members would be elected indirectly by provincial legislatures via proportional representation, alongside nominees of princely states. Crucially, however, Cripps inserted a fatal **provincial opt-out clause**: any province not prepared to accept the new Union Constitution could retain its existing constitutional position and frame a separate constitution of its own with the same full Dominion status.

Both major Indian parties rejected the Cripps Mission, though for diametrically opposite reasons:

- **Indian National Congress** rejected the plan because the opt-out clause planted the seeds of national Balkanisation, princely states' representatives were to be nominated by autocratic rulers rather than elected by their peoples, and Britain refused to share real control over wartime **Defence**. Gandhi famously dismissed the post-war Dominion pledge as *"a post-dated cheque."*
- **The Muslim League** rejected the Cripps Proposals because it refused to accept a single Indian Union and demanded that India be divided into **two autonomous states with two separate Constituent Assemblies**.

#### Acceptance via the Cabinet Mission Plan (`16 May 1946`)

After the Second World War ended, Britain's newly elected Labour Government under **Clement Attlee**—facing economic exhaustion at home, the **INA Trials**, and the **Royal Indian Navy Mutiny (`February 1946`)**—dispatched a three-member high-powered **Cabinet Mission** to India in **`March 1946`**. Its members were **Lord Pethick-Lawrence** (Secretary of State for India and chairman), **Sir Stafford Cripps** (President of the Board of Trade), and **A.V. Alexander** (First Lord of the Admiralty).

Announced on **`16 May 1946`**, the **Cabinet Mission Plan** explicitly **rejected the demand for two Constituent Assemblies** and a sovereign Pakistan on geographical, economic, and military grounds. Instead, it devised a complex **three-tier federal scheme** (a minimalist Union handling only Defence, Foreign Affairs, and Communications; provincial groupings into Sections A, B, and C; and autonomous provinces holding residuary powers) paired with a detailed, immediately workable blueprint for electing a single **389-member Constituent Assembly**. Because the grouping formula appeared to offer the Muslim League the substance of Pakistan within a loose Union while preserving Indian territorial unity for Congress, the Cabinet Mission's Constituent Assembly scheme was **more or less accepted** by both parties in June `1946`—clearing the way for the historic elections of July–August `1946`.

## 2. Composition & Election of the Constituent Assembly

Constituted in **`November 1946`** under the scheme formulated by the **Cabinet Mission Plan**, the Constituent Assembly had to reconcile two competing imperatives: **democratic legitimacy** and **administrative speed**. Ideally, the Cabinet Mission noted, an assembly elected directly through universal adult suffrage would have been most representative. Yet preparing electoral rolls for nearly 400 million people—over 85% of them illiterate and scattered across British provinces and more than 550 princely states—would have delayed constitution-making by at least two to three years at a moment of acute communal volatility.

The Mission therefore adopted a pragmatic compromise: utilise the **newly elected Provincial Legislative Assemblies** (chosen in the provincial elections of `January–April 1946` under the `Government of India Act of 1935`) as **electoral colleges** to elect British India's representatives indirectly, while inviting the princely states to join through a parallel consultative mechanism.

### Total Strength (389): British India vs Princely States

The Cabinet Mission fixed the **total strength** of the Constituent Assembly at **`389` seats**, distributed strictly in proportion to population between the two political halves of undivided India:

#### Governors' Provinces (`292`) and Chief Commissioners' Provinces (`4`)

**British India** was allotted **`296` seats** in total, sub-divided into two administrative categories:

- **Eleven Governors' Provinces (`292` seats):** Madras (`49`), Bombay (`21`), **Bengal (`60`)**, **United Provinces (`55`)**, **Punjab (`28`)**, **Bihar (`36`)**, **Central Provinces & Berar (`17`)**, **Assam (`10`)**, **Orissa (`9`)**, **Sind (`4`)**, and **North-West Frontier Province (`3`)**. *(Check: 49+21+60+55+28+36+17+10+9+4+3 = 292.)*
- **Four Chief Commissioners' Provinces (`4` seats — one each):** **Ajmer-Merwara**, **British Baluchistan**, **Coorg**, and **Delhi**. While Delhi, Ajmer-Merwara, and Coorg chose their representatives through local electoral bodies/legislative assemblies (Coorg Legislative Council; Delhi and Ajmer-Merwara through their Central Assembly representatives/advisory councils), British Baluchistan's representative was nominated via its Shahi Jirga and Quetta municipal body.

#### Princely States' Allocation (`93` Seats)

The **Indian Princely States**—numbering over 550 principalities ranging from vast Hyderabad, Mysore, and Travancore to tiny Kathiawar estates—were collectively allotted **`93` seats** based on the same one-seat-per-million population formula. Larger states received individual seats (e.g., Hyderabad 16, Mysore 7, Travancore 6, Kashmir 4, Baroda 3, Gwalior 4), while smaller states were grouped into composite clusters. Under the Cabinet Mission Plan, the **heads of the princely states** were to **nominate** their representatives, though the exact mode of selection was left to consultation between the Assembly's Negotiating Committee (States Committee) and the Chamber of Princes—which ultimately agreed that at least half of the princely states' representatives would be elected by state legislative bodies or popular assemblies rather than simply handpicked by rulers.

> **Mnemonic for the Four Chief Commissioners' Provinces (`4` Seats):** Remember **`A-B-C-D`**!
> - **A** — **A**jmer-Merwara
> - **B** — **B**ritish Baluchistan *(went to Pakistan in August 1947, reducing India's Chief Commissioners' seats from 4 to 3)*
> - **C** — **C**oorg
> - **D** — **D**elhi

### Population Ratio, Community Allocation & Indirect PR-STV Election

#### One Seat per Million and Tripartite Communal Division

To prevent any single province or community from being disproportionately weighted—a chronic grievance under earlier British reforms—the Cabinet Mission instituted an objective demographic yardstick:

- **Population Ratio:** Each British Indian province and princely state (or group of smaller states) was allotted seats roughly in proportion to its respective population as recorded in the **1941 Census**—specifically, **one seat for every million (ten lakh) population**.
- **Three Principal Communities:** Seats allocated to each British Indian province were further divided among **three principal communities**—**Muslims**, **Sikhs**, and **General** (comprising all persons other than Muslims and Sikhs: Hindus, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Parsis)—strictly in proportion to their respective provincial populations. Crucially, the separate **Sikh** category applied only in **Punjab** (which received 4 Sikh seats out of its 28), while all other provinces divided seats strictly between Muslims and General.

#### Proportional Representation by Single Transferable Vote (PR-STV)

Within each provincial legislative assembly, the representatives of each community were elected exclusively by **members of that own community** in the provincial legislature (so Muslim MLAs elected the province's Muslim CA members; General MLAs elected the General CA members; Sikh MLAs in Punjab elected Sikh CA members). Voting followed the method of **Proportional Representation by means of the Single Transferable Vote (PR-STV)**.

Why did the Cabinet Mission choose PR-STV rather than a simple first-past-the-post vote by provincial assemblies? Under a simple majority vote, a party holding 55% of General seats in a province could have swept 100% of that province's Constituent Assembly seats, shutting out smaller parties, regional voices, and independent legal minds. PR-STV guaranteed **multi-preference minority representation**—allowing smaller factions (such as the Scheduled Castes Federation, Communists, Krishak Praja Party, and independent jurists) to secure representation whenever they crossed the Hare quota.

```flow
# How the Constituent Assembly Was Elected (Cabinet Mission Scheme, 1946)

Demographic Seat Allotment (389 Total) | 1 seat per 10 lakh population across British India (296) and Princely States (93)

Constituent Assembly Constituted (Nov 1946) | Partly elected, partly nominated, indirectly elected body—yet socially encompassing all major Indian communities
```

The **93 seats allotted to the Princely States** remained unfilled throughout late `1946` and early `1947` because the Chamber of Princes—uncertain about the lapse of British paramountcy and alarmed by the Congress–League standoff—initially stayed away. The breakthrough arrived on **`28 April 1947`**, when representatives of **six princely states** (**Baroda, Bikaner, Jaipur, Patiala, Rewa, and Udaipur**) took their seats in the Assembly. Following the **Mountbatten Plan (`3 June 1947`)**, representatives of most remaining states within Indian territory steadily joined.

Notably, **Dr B.R. Ambedkar's own path into the Constituent Assembly** illustrates both the mechanics of PR-STV and the Congress leadership's deliberate statesmanship. Defeated in Bombay in the 1946 provincial elections, Ambedkar secured election to the Constituent Assembly in July `1946` from **Bengal** (representing the Scheduled Castes Federation with support from Jogendra Nath Mandal and Scheduled Caste/independent MLAs in the Bengal Assembly). When Partition divided Bengal in `1947`—ending his Bengal membership as the eastern constituencies supporting him went to Pakistan—**Dr Rajendra Prasad** and **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** urged Bombay Premier **B.G. Kher** to secure Ambedkar's immediate return to the Assembly. **M.R. Jayakar** vacated his Bombay seat, and Ambedkar was re-elected from **Bombay Presidency** on a Congress-backed ticket—enabling him to chair the Drafting Committee weeks later.

### Character of the Assembly: Social Breadth & the Fifteen Women Members

#### Partly Elected, Partly Nominated — Yet Socially Inclusive

Structurally, the Constituent Assembly was a **partly elected and partly nominated body**; moreover, its elected members were **indirectly elected** by provincial legislators who had themselves been chosen on a **limited franchise** (roughly 10–14% of British India's total population, restricted by tax, property, and educational qualifications under the `1935 Act`). **Mahatma Gandhi** was **not a member** of the Constituent Assembly—preferring to work outside formal constitutional chambers to heal communal violence in Noakhali, Bihar, and Delhi—and **Muhammad Ali Jinnah**, though elected, boycotted the Assembly entirely to lead Pakistan's Constituent Assembly in Karachi. Congress Socialists led by **Jayaprakash Narayan** likewise chose to abstain.

Despite these structural limitations, the Assembly attained remarkable **social, regional, and ideological breadth**. The Congress leadership consciously issued party tickets beyond its own ranks so that India's finest jurists, economists, minority voices, and social reformers were seated inside the hall. Consequently, the Assembly included representatives of every significant section of Indian society: **Hindus, Muslims** (Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, Rafi Ahmed Kidwai, Syed Mohammad Saadullah, Begum Aizaz Rasul), **Sikhs** (Sardar Hukam Singh, Baldev Singh, Ujjal Singh), **Parsis** (H.P. Modi, R.K. Sidhwa), **Anglo-Indians** (Frank Anthony), **Indian Christians** (H.C. Mukherjee, Jerome D'Souza, Rajkumari Amrit Kaur), **Scheduled Castes** (B.R. Ambedkar, Jagjivan Ram, H.J. Khandekar, Dakshayani Velayudhan), and **Scheduled Tribes** (Jaipal Singh Munda, Rev. J.J.M. Nichols-Roy, Boniface Lakra).

## 3. Working of the Assembly & the Objectives Resolution

The Constituent Assembly convened amidst one of the most turbulent chapters in subcontinental history—marked by the communal carnage following the Muslim League's *Direct Action Day* (`16 August 1946`), an uneasy coalition inside the Interim Government, and mounting uncertainty over whether India would emerge united or partitioned. Yet from its very first sitting, the Assembly conducted itself with the solemn parliamentary dignity of an independent sovereign legislature.

### First Meeting (9 December 1946) & Dr Sachchidananda Sinha as Temporary President

The Constituent Assembly held its **first meeting** on **`9 December 1946`** at 11:00 a.m. in the Constitution Hall (today the Central Hall of **Samvidhan Sadan**, Old Parliament House) in New Delhi. **The Muslim League boycotted the meeting**, insisting upon its demand for a separate sovereign state of Pakistan, and the princely states had not yet nominated their representatives. Consequently, **`211` members** attended the inaugural sitting *(207 signed the official attendance register on Day 1, with four additional members joining immediately during the opening proceedings)*.

In strict adherence to the **French constitutional convention** (*doyen d'âge*), **Dr Sachchidananda Sinha**—the **oldest member** of the Assembly (a distinguished jurist and legislator from Bihar, whose name was proposed by Congress President J.B. Kripalani)—was elected as the **temporary President** of the Assembly. On `10 December 1946`, **Frank Anthony** (leader of the Anglo-Indian community) was appointed temporary Deputy Chairman. In his poignant inaugural address, Dr Sinha urged the framers to rise above partisan passion and look to enduring constitutional ideals, quoting the poet Iqbal's tribute to India's civilisational permanence.

### Election of Dr Rajendra Prasad, Two Vice-Presidents & Sir B.N. Rau

#### Permanent President and the Twin Vice-Presidents

Two days later, on **`11 December 1946`**, the Assembly unanimously elected **`Dr Rajendra Prasad`** as its permanent **President**. Widely respected across party and ideological lines for his calm temperament, deep legal training, and unswerving fairness, Rajendra Prasad guided the Assembly's deliberations for nearly three years without once losing the trust of minority or dissenting members.

A subtle institutional detail frequently tested in the UPSC Prelims is that the Constituent Assembly had **two Vice-Presidents**—not one:

- **`H.C. Mukherjee`** (Harendra Coomar Mookerjee, an eminent academic and Indian Christian leader from Bengal), elected on **`25 January 1947`** (representing British India / Indian Provinces).
- **`V.T. Krishnamachari`** (former Diwan of Baroda and Jaipur), elected on **`16 July 1948`** (representing the Indian Princely States, ensuring parity of institutional leadership once the states entered the Assembly).

#### Sir B.N. Rau, H.V.R. Iyengar, and the Assembly Secretariat

Equally decisive to the Assembly's institutional success was its non-political technical secretariat—a team of towering civil servants and legal draftsmen whose silent work underpinned every floor debate:

- **Constitutional Advisor (Legal Advisor):** **`Sir B.N. Rau`** (Benegal Narsing Rau, retired ICS officer, former judge of the Calcutta High Court, and subsequently the first Indian judge at the International Court of Justice at The Hague). **Crucially, Sir B.N. Rau was NOT a member of the Constituent Assembly.** He travelled to the United States, Canada, Ireland, and the United Kingdom to consult eminent jurists (including US Supreme Court Justice Felix Frankfurter, who famously advised him against copying the vague American "due process of law" clause—influencing India's adoption of *"procedure established by law"* in `Article 21`!). In **`October 1947`**, B.N. Rau produced the **initial Draft Constitution** (comprising **243 Articles and 13 Schedules**), which served as the working foundation for the Drafting Committee.
- **Secretary to the Constituent Assembly:** **`H.V.R. Iyengar`** (ICS), who managed the entire administrative and documentary machinery of the Assembly.
- **Chief Draftsman:** **`S.N. Mukerjee`** (Surendra Nath Mukherjee), whom Dr B.R. Ambedkar publicly credited on `25 November 1949` for his rare ability to translate the most intricate constitutional proposals into precise, crystal-clear statutory language.

### The Objectives Resolution (13 December 1946 / 22 January 1947)

On **`13 December 1946`**, **Jawaharlal Nehru** moved the historic **`Objectives Resolution`** in the Constituent Assembly. More than a mere procedural motion, it laid down the **fundamentals and philosophy of India's constitutional structure**, articulating the moral creed of the freedom struggle in solemn juridical prose. Although the Assembly overwhelmingly supported the resolution during five days of impassioned debate in December `1946`, Dr M.R. Jayakar and Dr B.R. Ambedkar urged a brief postponement in the hope that the Muslim League and Princely States might join the chamber before its passage. When it became evident that the League would persist in its boycott, the Assembly **unanimously adopted** the Objectives Resolution on **`22 January 1947`**, with every member rising to their feet in solemn affirmation.

> "Words are magic things often enough, but even the magic of words sometimes cannot convey the magic of the human spirit and of a Nation's passion... It is a Resolution and yet, it is something much more than a resolution. It is a Declaration. It is a firm resolve. It is a pledge and an undertaking and it is for all of us, I hope, a dedication." — Jawaharlal Nehru, Constituent Assembly, 13 December 1946

#### The Eight Foundational Principles

Every aspirant must master all **eight clauses** of the Objectives Resolution—not just its headline promises—because they reveal both the enduring ideals of `1950` and the pre-Partition federal assumptions that changed after `1947`:

1. **Independent Sovereign Republic:** The Constituent Assembly declared its firm and solemn resolve to proclaim India as an **Independent Sovereign Republic** and to draw up a Constitution for her future governance. *(Note: The words "Socialist" and "Secular" were NOT in the Objectives Resolution; they were added to the Preamble only by the `42nd Amendment Act of 1976`.)*
2. **Union of India:** The territories then comprising **British India**, the territories then forming the **Indian States**, such other parts of India outside British India and the States, and such other territories as were willing to be constituted into Independent Sovereign India shall be a **Union of them all**.
3. **Autonomous Constituent Units with Residuary Powers:** The said territories—whether with their present boundaries or with such others as might be determined by the Constituent Assembly and thereafter according to the law of the Constitution—shall possess and retain the status of **autonomous units**, together with **residuary powers**, and exercise all powers and functions of government and administration save and except those vested in or assigned to the Union. *(Crucial historical note: Vesting residuary powers in the provinces reflected the **Cabinet Mission Plan's** pre-Partition framework; once Partition occurred in August `1947`, the Assembly abandoned weak federalism and vested residuary powers in the **Union Parliament** under `Article 248`!)*
4. **Popular Sovereignty:** All power and authority of Sovereign Independent India, its constituent parts, and organs of government **are derived from the people**—a definitive repudiation of both British parliamentary supremacy and princely divine right.
5. **Justice, Equality, and Fundamental Freedoms:** Wherein shall be guaranteed and secured to **all the people of India**:
   - **Justice** — social, economic, and political;
   - **Equality** — of status, of opportunity, and before the law;
   - **Freedom** — of thought, expression, belief, faith, worship, vocation, association, and action, subject to law and public morality.
6. **Safeguards for Minorities and Marginalised Sections:** Adequate safeguards shall be provided for **minorities**, **backward and tribal areas**, and **depressed and other backward classes**—providing the philosophical anchor for `Articles 15(4)`, `16(4)`, `29–30`, `46`, `330–342`, and the **Fifth and Sixth Schedules**.
7. **Territorial Integrity and Sovereign Rights:** Whereby shall be maintained the **integrity of the territory of the Republic** and its **sovereign rights on land, sea, and air** according to justice and the law of civilised nations.
8. **International Peace and Human Welfare:** This ancient land attains its rightful and honoured place in the world and makes its full and willing contribution to the **promotion of world peace and the welfare of mankind**—directly foreshadowing `Article 51` (Directive Principles on international peace and security).

#### Constitutional Legacy: From Objectives Resolution to the Preamble

The **Objectives Resolution** shaped every subsequent stage of constitution-making—guiding the Union Constitution, Provincial Constitution, and Fundamental Rights committees throughout `1947–1949`. Its **modified version forms the present Preamble** of the Indian Constitution, translating its eight pledges into the concise opening declaration *"We, the People of India..."* (with one celebrated addition by the Drafting Committee: **`Fraternity`**—assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity of the Nation—inspired by Dr B.R. Ambedkar's insistence on social solidarity). In **`Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)`**, the Supreme Court repeatedly invoked the Objectives Resolution alongside the Preamble to establish that the Constitution's foundational commitments constitute an inviolable **Basic Structure**.

> [!mains]
> **Mains Value-Addition — Why the Objectives Resolution Matters Today:** In GS-2 answers on the Preamble, constitutional morality, or transformative constitutionalism, highlight that the **Objectives Resolution (`13 Dec 1946`)** was India's **pre-Independence moral contract**. Unlike constitutions forged exclusively after winning power, India's framers committed themselves to **republican popular sovereignty, socio-economic justice, and minority safeguards** *eight months before Independence*—binding the future postcolonial state to an inclusive civic vision even as communal partition loomed outside the Assembly doors.

### Structural Transformation under the Indian Independence Act, 1947

Between December `1946` and June `1947`, the Constituent Assembly operated under the shadow of the Cabinet Mission's constraints and the Muslim League's boycott. Everything changed with the **Mountbatten Plan (`3 June 1947`)** for the partition of British India, followed by the **Indian Independence Act, 1947** (which received Royal Assent on `18 July 1947` and came into force on `15 August 1947`). Immediately after `3 June 1947`, representatives of most princely states joined the Assembly, and **Muslim League members representing areas remaining in the Indian Dominion** also took their seats inside the chamber.

The **Indian Independence Act of 1947** transformed the Constituent Assembly's legal status, functions, and membership in **three fundamental respects**:

#### Full Sovereignty and Abrogation of British Parliamentary Statutes

First, the Assembly was declared a **fully sovereign body**. Under the Cabinet Mission Plan, the Assembly had been hedged in by restrictions on Union powers, provincial groupings, and communal majorities; its draft was still presumed to require British parliamentary ratification. The `1947 Act` swept away every limitation: the Assembly was now completely free to **frame any Constitution it pleased** and was explicitly empowered to **abrogate, repeal, or alter any law made by the British Parliament in relation to India**—including the `Government of India Act of 1935` and the `Indian Independence Act of 1947` itself. With British paramountcy lapsing, the Assembly became the supreme constituent authority of free India.

#### Dual Role: Constituent Assembly vs Dominion Legislature (G.V. Mavalankar)

Second, the Assembly was converted simultaneously into a **legislative body**—making it the **first Parliament of free India (Dominion Legislature)**, replacing the old Central Legislative Assembly and Council of State created under the `1919 Act`. Crucially, the Assembly was assigned **two separate functions**—(1) making a Constitution for free India, and (2) enacting ordinary laws for the governance of the country—and these two roles were scrupulously **performed on separate days** under **different presiding officers**:

- Whenever the Assembly met as the **Constituent Body** (constitution-making), it was chaired by **`Dr Rajendra Prasad`**.
- Whenever the Assembly met as the **Legislative Body** (ordinary law-making, passing budgets, and holding Nehru's Cabinet accountable through Question Hour), it was chaired by **`G.V. Mavalankar`** (Ganesh Vasudev Mavalankar, formally elected Speaker when the Dominion Legislature held its first sitting on **`17 November 1947`**—earning him the historic honour of being both the **Speaker of the Dominion Legislature/Provisional Parliament** and subsequently the **first Speaker of the Lok Sabha** in `1952`).

These **dual functions continued until `26 November 1949`**, when the task of making the Constitution was completed. From `26 January 1950` until the constitution of the first elected Parliament in `May 1952` (following the `1951–52` general elections), the Assembly continued solely as the **Provisional Parliament of India**—during which it even enacted the landmark **First Constitutional Amendment Act (`1951`)**.

#### Post-Partition Reconstitution: Strength Reduced from 389 to 299

Third, the **Muslim League members hailing from areas included in Pakistan** (West Punjab, East Bengal, Sind, NWFP, British Baluchistan, and Sylhet district of Assam) withdrew from India's Constituent Assembly to form Pakistan's Constituent Assembly. Consequently, the total strength of the Indian Constituent Assembly fell from **`389`** (fixed in `1946`) to **`299`**:

- **Indian Provinces (formerly British India):** Reduced from **`296` to `229`** (`226` from nine Governors' Provinces + `3` from Chief Commissioners' Provinces—Delhi, Ajmer-Merwara, and Coorg—since British Baluchistan went to Pakistan). Among Indian provinces, the **United Provinces (`55`)**, **Madras (`49`)**, and **Bihar (`36`)** held the largest delegations.
- **Indian Princely States:** Reduced from **`93` to `70`** (with **Mysore (`7`)** and **Travancore (`6`)** holding the largest active delegations, alongside Hyderabad's 16-seat quota until its integration in September `1948`).

```compare
# Constituent Assembly: Pre-Independence (1946) vs Post-Independence (1947–1949)
Before Indian Independence Act, 1947 | After Indian Independence Act, 1947
Legal Status :: Semi-sovereign; constrained by Cabinet Mission Plan's 3-tier Union and grouping limits | Fully sovereign body; empowered to frame any Constitution and repeal any British Act (including IIA 1947)
Institutional Role :: Single role exclusively as a constitution-making body | Dual role on separate days: Constituent Body (President: Dr Rajendra Prasad) & Dominion Legislature (Speaker: G.V. Mavalankar)
Total Membership :: 389 seats (296 British India: 292 Governors' + 4 Chief Commissioners' Provinces; 93 Princely States) | 299 seats (229 Indian Provinces + 70 Indian Princely States) after Partition withdrawals
Federal Orientation :: Weak Centre (Defence, Foreign Affairs, Communications) with residuary powers in provinces (Objectives Resolution Cl. 3) | Strong Union Federation with residuary powers vested in Union Parliament (Article 248)
```

## 4. Committees of the Constituent Assembly

A 299-member chamber could never draft a comprehensive constitutional text directly on the assembly floor. To combine specialist expertise with democratic deliberation, the Constituent Assembly organised its workload through an intricate **committee system** comprising **eight Major Committees** and over a dozen **Minor Committees** (procedural and substantive). Each committee investigated a dedicated constitutional domain—Union powers, provincial autonomy, fundamental rights, minority safeguards, tribal governance, judiciary, or finance—consulted experts and provincial governments, and submitted reasoned reports to the full Assembly. Only after the Assembly debated and adopted the broad principles of these committee reports did the **Drafting Committee** weave them into coherent legal clauses.

### The Eight Major Committees & the Five Advisory Sub-Committees

The **eight Major Committees** formed the institutional engine of constitution-making. Leadership of these bodies was intentionally shared across the Assembly's core pillars—Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, Dr Rajendra Prasad, and Dr B.R. Ambedkar—to ensure that every draft carried broad political authority from the outset.

| # | Major Committee | Chairman | Core Mandate & Constitutional Imprint |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Union Powers Committee** | **Jawaharlal Nehru** | Demarcated Union vs State legislative spheres; initially worked within the Cabinet Mission's 3-subject Centre, then pivoted after Partition to a strong Centre with the **Seventh Schedule** lists and Union residuary powers (`Art. 248`) |
| 2 | **Union Constitution Committee** | **Jawaharlal Nehru** | Designed the architecture of the Union Executive (indirectly elected President + parliamentary Cabinet), bicameral Parliament, and federal judiciary |
| 3 | **Provincial Constitution Committee** | **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** | Drafted the model State Constitution—choosing parliamentary government in states with an appointed **Governor** (abandoning its initial proposal for a directly elected Governor to avoid executive friction with Chief Ministers) |
| 4 | **Drafting Committee** | **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** | Synthesised all committee reports and Sir B.N. Rau's initial draft into the clause-by-clause text of the Draft Constitution (`Feb 1948` and `Oct 1948`) |
| 5 | **Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal and Excluded Areas** | **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** | Oversaw the most sensitive social compact of the republic: enforceable Fundamental Rights, abolition of religious separate electorates, time-bound SC/ST political reservations, and tribal autonomy frameworks |
| 6 | **Rules of Procedure Committee** | **Dr Rajendra Prasad** | Framed the Assembly's standing rules of debate, quorum, amendments, voting, and committee procedures |
| 7 | **States Committee** *(Committee for Negotiating with States)* | **Jawaharlal Nehru** | Negotiated the entry, representation, and constitutional integration of Indian Princely States into the Constituent Assembly |
| 8 | **Steering Committee** | **Dr Rajendra Prasad** | Coordinated timelines, inter-committee schedules, and the overall agenda of the Constituent Assembly |

#### The Five Sub-Committees of the Advisory Committee

Because the **Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal and Excluded Areas** (chaired by **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel**) carried an extraordinary social and territorial mandate, it operated through **five specialised Sub-Committees**—a perennial favourite for UPSC Prelims match-the-following questions:

1. **Fundamental Rights Sub-Committee — Chairman: `J.B. Kripalani`:** Drafted the distinction between **justiciable Fundamental Rights** (`Part III`)—enforceable through Supreme Court and High Court writs—and **non-justiciable Directive Principles of State Policy** (`Part IV`), drawing on the Irish Constitution and Indian nationalist pledges. Prominent members included Dr B.R. Ambedkar, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, K.M. Munshi, Minoo Masani, Hansa Mehta, and Rajkumari Amrit Kaur.
2. **Minorities Sub-Committee — Chairman: `H.C. Mukherjee`:** Addressed the thorniest political legacy of British rule. Initially, it recommended retaining seat reservations for religious minorities in joint electorates; however, following the trauma of Partition and courageous appeals from minority leaders themselves (including H.C. Mukherjee, Begum Aizaz Rasul, and Tajamul Husain), the Advisory Committee famously recommended **abolishing all religion-based political reservations and separate electorates**, confining reserved legislative seats strictly to **Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes** (initially for ten years under `Article 334`) alongside cultural and educational rights (`Articles 29–30`).
3. **North-East Frontier Tribal Areas and Assam Excluded & Partially Excluded Areas Sub-Committee — Chairman: `Gopinath Bardoloi`:** Toured the hill districts of undivided Assam (with key input from Khasi leader Rev. J.J.M. Nichols-Roy) and recommended **Autonomous District Councils** to protect tribal land, customary laws, and forest livelihoods—the direct architectural basis of the **Sixth Schedule** (`Articles 244(2)` and `275(1)`).
4. **Excluded and Partially Excluded Areas (Other than Those in Assam) Sub-Committee — Chairman: `A.V. Thakkar` (Thakkar Bapa):** Studied tribal regions across central, western, and southern India (Bihar, Orissa, Central Provinces, Bombay, Madras, East Punjab) and recommended **Tribes Advisory Councils** and gubernatorial safeguards over land alienation and moneylending—forming the blueprint for the **Fifth Schedule** (`Article 244(1)`).
5. **North-West Frontier Tribal Areas Sub-Committee:** Constituted to examine the tribal belt along the North-West Frontier Province (with Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan, Khan Abdul Samad Khan, and Mehr Chand Khanna nominated to it). Following the `3 June 1947` Mountbatten Plan and the referendum transferring NWFP to Pakistan, this fifth sub-committee could not visit the region and became **infructuous/inoperative**.

Provincial Constitution Committee | Chaired by Sardar Patel | Rejected directly elected Governors in favour of Presidentially appointed Governors to preserve unified parliamentary accountability in States.
Advisory Committee & 5 Sub-Committees | Patel · Kripalani · Mukherjee · Bardoloi · Thakkar | Crafted Parts III & IV, dismantled communal separate electorates, and architected the Fifth Schedule (Thakkar) and Sixth Schedule (Bardoloi).

### The Drafting Committee (29 August 1947): All Seven Members & Working Method

Among all bodies created by the Constituent Assembly, the most pivotal was the **seven-member Drafting Committee**, constituted on **`29 August 1947`**—just two weeks after Independence—and tasked with scrutinising Sir B.N. Rau's preliminary text and drafting a complete, legally airtight Constitution reflecting the Assembly's committee decisions. At its very first meeting on **`30 August 1947`**, the Committee unanimously elected **`Dr B.R. Ambedkar`** as its Chairman.

| # | Member | Role / Replacement Note | Constitutional & Legal Profile |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** | **Chairman** | Brilliant jurist, economist, and India's first Law Minister; piloted the Draft Constitution clause by clause through the Assembly |
| 2 | **N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar** | Member | Former Diwan of Jammu & Kashmir and Cabinet Minister; played a key role in federal provisions and `Article 370` |
| 3 | **Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar** | Member | Former Advocate-General of Madras; constitutional jurist who defended strong central authority, judicial independence, and adult franchise |
| 4 | **Dr K.M. Munshi** | Member | Eminent Bombay lawyer, statesman, and writer; key voice on Fundamental Rights, civil liberties, and national unity |
| 5 | **Syed Mohammad Saadullah** | Member | Former Prime Minister (Premier) of Assam and Muslim League leader who stayed in India; brought provincial and minority perspectives |
| 6 | **N. Madhava Rau** | Replaced **B.L. Mitter** *(resigned due to ill health)* | Former Diwan of Mysore; contributed administrative expertise on princely states and federal finance |
| 7 | **T.T. Krishnamachari** | Replaced **D.P. Khaitan** *(died in 1948)* | Madras parliamentarian and financial expert; later Union Finance Minister; instrumental in financial and federal drafting |

The Drafting Committee worked with astonishing speed and rigour. Taking **less than six months** to produce its initial text—sitting for a total of **`141` days** across its entire tenure—it published the **First Draft of the Constitution in `February 1948`** (containing 315 Articles and 8 Schedules). Demonstrating an extraordinary commitment to public participation, the Assembly gave the people of India, provincial legislatures, courts, and newspapers **eight full months** to critique the draft and submit amendments. Carefully sifting through thousands of public comments and institutional suggestions, the Drafting Committee published its revised **Second Draft in `October 1948`**.

On `5 November 1948`, **T.T. Krishnamachari** candidly placed on record in the Constituent Assembly how disproportionately heavy the drafting burden had fallen upon **Dr B.R. Ambedkar**: one member had resigned (B.L. Mitter), one had passed away (D.P. Khaitan), another was away in America, others were preoccupied with state duties or troubled by ill health—leaving Ambedkar to carry the bulk of the drafting and floor defence on his shoulders. For his unmatched intellectual mastery, lucid replies to hundreds of amendments, and tireless piloting of the text, Dr B.R. Ambedkar is universally revered as the **"Father of the Constitution of India,"** its **"Chief Architect,"** and—in an evocative tribute to India's ancient lawgiver—a **"Modern Manu."**

## 5. Enactment, Enforcement & Key Milestones

Once the Drafting Committee circulated its revised **Second Draft in `October 1948`**, the Constitution moved from technical committee rooms onto the full floor of the Constituent Assembly for exhaustive democratic scrutiny. Every article, exception, and punctuation mark was tested against opposing ideological viewpoints—Gandhian decentralisers, socialist planners, classical liberals, federalist provinces, and minority spokespersons—through a formal three-reading parliamentary procedure.

### From First Draft to Three Readings: Sessions, Amendments & Expenditure

The legislative passage of the Constitution unfolded across **three structured Readings** spanning just over twelve months (`November 1948 – November 1949`):

- **First Reading (`4 November – 9 November 1948`):** On **`4 November 1948`**, Dr B.R. Ambedkar formally introduced the final Draft Constitution in the Assembly and delivered a masterly introductory address explaining its foundational choices—parliamentary responsibility over American presidential stability, a Union of States without right of secession, single citizenship, village panchayats vs individual rights, and why administrative provisions were drawn from the `1935 Act`. The Assembly held a **general discussion** on the draft's broad principles for **five days** until `9 November 1948`.
- **Second Reading (`15 November 1948 – 17 October 1949`):** This was the longest and most demanding phase—an exhaustive **clause-by-clause consideration** of every single article and schedule. Across eleven intense months of debate, members proposed as many as **`7,653` amendments**, of which **`2,473` amendments** were actually moved, debated, and disposed of on the Assembly floor.
- **Third Reading (`14 November – 26 November 1949`):** Commenced on **`14 November 1949`**. Dr B.R. Ambedkar moved the formal motion—*"the Constitution as settled by the Assembly be passed."* After twelve days of valedictory reflection—capped by Ambedkar's unforgettable closing warning on `25 November 1949` and Dr Rajendra Prasad's concluding address on `26 November 1949`—the motion was **declared passed on `26 November 1949`**.

In total, the Constituent Assembly held **`11` formal Sessions** between `9 December 1946` and `26 November 1949` (followed by a special final sitting on **`24 January 1950`** to sign the illuminated manuscripts, adopt the National Anthem and Song, and elect India's first President). The entire constitution-making enterprise spanned **`2 years, 11 months, and 18 days`**, covering **`165` sitting days**—of which **`114` days** were devoted exclusively to examining the Draft Constitution. The framers systematically studied the constitutional texts and practices of roughly **`60` countries**, at a remarkably modest total public expenditure of approximately **`₹64 lakh`** (`₹63,96,729`).

```flow
# From Preliminary Draft to Republic Day: The Enactment Pipeline (1947–1950)
Oct 1947: Sir B.N. Rau's Initial Draft | 243 Articles & 13 Schedules prepared by the Constitutional Advisor from early committee reports
Feb 1948: First Draft Published | Drafting Committee (7 members) circulated 315 Articles & 8 Schedules for 8 months of open public & provincial comment
Oct 1948: Second Draft Published | Revised text incorporating thousands of citizen, ministerial, and legislative suggestions
Nov 1948 – Nov 1949: Three Readings | First Reading (4–9 Nov 1948) -> Second Reading clause-by-clause (15 Nov 1948 – 17 Oct 1949; 7,653 proposed / 2,473 discussed) -> Third Reading (14–26 Nov 1949)
26 Nov 1949: Adoption & Partial Enforcement | Preamble, 395 Articles, 8 Schedules adopted; 16 immediate Articles enforced via Art. 394 || 24 Jan 1950: Final Signing Sitting | 284 members signed handwritten manuscripts; Anthem, Song & First President chosen
26 Jan 1950: Full Commencement (Republic Day) | Remaining provisions enforced on Purna Swaraj anniversary; GoI Act 1935 & IIA 1947 repealed (Art. 395); SC inaugurated 28 Jan 1950
```

### Adoption (26 November 1949), Immediate Commencement of 16 Articles & Full Commencement (26 January 1950)

#### Adoption Day (`26 November 1949`) & the Original Constitutional Architecture

On **`26 November 1949`**, the Assembly adopted the Constitution, and Dr Rajendra Prasad authenticated it as President of the Assembly. That date—**`26 November 1949`** (*Miti Margashirsha Shukla Saptami, Samvat 2006 Vikram*)—is expressly enshrined in the **Preamble** as the date on which *"We, the People of India"* adopted, enacted, and gave to themselves the Constitution. Out of the Assembly's post-Partition membership of **`299`**, **`284` members** appended their signatures to the authenticated Constitution (with the ceremonial signing of the three official manuscripts—calligraphed English, printed English, and calligraphed Hindi—taking place on **`24 January 1950`**, Jawaharlal Nehru signing first and Dr Rajendra Prasad signing last).

As originally adopted on `26 November 1949`, the Constitution contained a **Preamble**, **`395` Articles** (organised into **22 Parts**), and **`8` Schedules**. A vital procedural nuance often tested in UPSC Prelims is that **the Preamble was enacted *after* the entire body of the Constitution had already been enacted**, ensuring that the opening statement of philosophy faithfully reflected every substantive provision settled by the Assembly.

#### Immediate Commencement of `16` Articles (`Article 394`)

Rather than waiting until `26 January 1950` for every clause to activate, **`Article 394`** brought **`16` specific Articles** (fifteen enumerated articles plus Article 394 itself) into force **immediately on `26 November 1949`**. Each of these sixteen provisions was operationally indispensable during the two-month transition before Republic Day:

- **Citizenship (`Articles 5, 6, 7, 8, 9`):** Required immediately to settle citizenship status amid cross-border Partition migration and to prepare universal voter rolls.
- **Oath of the President (`Article 60`):** Required so that the newly elected President of India could validly take office and administer oaths on `26 January 1950`.
- **Election Commission (`Article 324`):** Activated immediately so an independent Election Commission could begin the monumental logistical exercise of registering ~173 million adult voters for India's first general elections.
- **Definitions & Interpretation (`Articles 366, 367`):** Necessary legal dictionary to construe the newly active constitutional articles.
- **Provisional Parliament, Provisional President & Transitional Powers (`Articles 379, 380, 388, 391, 392`):** Provided legal continuity for the Constituent Assembly to function as the Provisional Parliament, for filling casual vacancies, for electing the provisional President, and for empowering the Governor-General/President to remove transitional difficulties (`Art. 392`).
- **Short Title & Commencement (`Articles 393, 394`):** Formally named *"The Constitution of India"* (`Art. 393`) and supplied the legal trigger (`Art. 394`) for immediate vs deferred commencement.

#### Full Commencement (`26 January 1950`) & Repeals (`Article 395`)

The **remaining major provisions** of the Constitution came into force exactly two months later, on **`26 January 1950`**—explicitly termed the **"date of its commencement"** in `Article 394` and celebrated nationwide as **Republic Day**. The choice of `26 January` was deeply symbolic: exactly twenty years earlier, on **`26 January 1930`**, Indians across the subcontinent had observed the first **`Purna Swaraj` (Complete Independence) Day**, pursuant to the historic resolution passed at the **Lahore Session (`December 1929`)** of the Indian National Congress under Jawaharlal Nehru's presidency. By inaugurating the sovereign republic on `26 January 1950`, the framers fulfilled the exact pledge taken on the banks of the Ravi two decades prior.

Simultaneously, **`Article 395`** severed the final legal umbilical cord to the British Parliament by **repealing** both the **`Indian Independence Act of 1947`** and the **`Government of India Act of 1935`** (together with all enactments amending or supplementing the `1935 Act`). **One crucial statute was expressly exempted from repeal under `Article 395`:** the **`Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act, 1949`** (effective `10 October 1949`), which was **continued** in force so that appellate jurisdiction of the British Privy Council in London could never revive. Two days later, on **`28 January 1950`**, the **Supreme Court of India** was formally inaugurated (with **Justice Harilal J. Kania** as first Chief Justice of India), succeeding the colonial Federal Court (`1937`).

### Other Historic Functions Performed by the Constituent Assembly

Beyond framing the Constitution and enacting ordinary Dominion legislation, the Constituent Assembly performed **five landmark nation-building functions** that laid the symbolic and diplomatic foundations of the Indian Republic:

- **Adoption of the National Flag (`22 July 1947`):** Moving the resolution weeks before Independence, Jawaharlal Nehru presented the horizontal tricolour (deep saffron, white with the 24-spoke navy-blue Ashoka Chakra from Sarnath's Lion Capital replacing the *charkha*, and dark green—adapted from Pingali Venkayya's design).
- **Ratification of India's Commonwealth Membership (`May 1949`):** Following the historic **London Declaration (`April 1949`)**—which transformed the "British Commonwealth" into the modern **Commonwealth of Nations** so that a fully sovereign republic could remain a member accepting the British monarch purely as a symbolic *Head of the Commonwealth* without owing allegiance to the Crown—the Assembly ratified India's membership in `May 1949`.
- **Adoption of the National Anthem (`24 January 1950`):** Formally adopted Rabindranath Tagore's ***Jana Gana Mana*** as India's National Anthem at its final sitting.
- **Adoption of the National Song (`24 January 1950`):** Simultaneously declared Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay's ***Vande Mataram*** to hold equal honour as the National Song.
- **Election of the First President of India (`24 January 1950`):** Unanimously elected **`Dr Rajendra Prasad`** as the first President of the Republic of India. Thereafter, from `26 January 1950` until `May 1952`, the Assembly served as India's **Provisional Parliament** until the first bicameral Parliament assembled after the `1951–52` general elections.

### Interim Government (1946) vs First Cabinet of Free India (1947)

A recurring UPSC Prelims favourite is distinguishing between portfolios held in the **Interim Government of `1946`** and the **First Cabinet of Free India (`1947`)**. Constitutionally, the two executives differed fundamentally: members of the **Interim Government** (sworn in on **`2 September 1946`**, expanded on **`26 October 1946`** when five Muslim League nominees joined) were legally members of the **Viceroy's Executive Council** under the `1919/1935` transitional framework—meaning the **Viceroy (`Lord Wavell`, later `Lord Mountbatten`) remained President** of the Council while **Jawaharlal Nehru** was designated **Vice-President** of the Council. In contrast, on **`15 August 1947`**, Nehru became **Prime Minister** heading a **sovereign, collectively responsible Cabinet** under a purely constitutional Governor-General.

### Expert Committee of the Congress (July 1946), Calligraphy & Artwork, and Authoritative Hindi Text (Article 394A)

#### Expert Committee of the Congress (`8 July 1946`)

Even while elections to the Constituent Assembly were still underway, the Indian National Congress appointed an **eight-member Expert Committee** on **`8 July 1946`** to prepare background research, procedural rules, and draft constitutional proposals so the Assembly would not lose momentum once convened. Its members were **`Jawaharlal Nehru` (Chairman)**, **M. Asaf Ali**, **K.M. Munshi**, **N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar**, **K.T. Shah**, **D.R. Gadgil**, **Humayun Kabir**, and **K. Santhanam**; subsequently, on the Chairman's proposal, **Krishna Kripalani** was co-opted as a member and convener.

The Expert Committee held **two sittings**—the first in **New Delhi (`20–22 July 1946`)** and the second in **Bombay (`15–17 August 1946`)**. Beyond preparing notes on the procedure for appointing key committees and framing the declaration of objectives (which Nehru refined into the **Objectives Resolution**), the Committee laid down the institutional road map for Indian constitutionalism. As constitutional historian **Granville Austin** observed, *"It was the Congress Experts Committee that set India on the road to her present Constitution."*

#### Symbol, Calligraphy & Santiniketan Illumination

The framers wanted India's founding charter to be not merely a printed legal statute, but an enduring work of Indian craftsmanship reflecting the nation's civilisational continuity:

- **Symbol (Seal) of the Constituent Assembly:** The **Elephant** was adopted as the official seal of the Constituent Assembly—symbolising wisdom, dignity, endurance, and constitutional weight.
- **English Calligraphy:** The entire original English Constitution was handwritten—not typeset or printed—by master calligrapher **`Prem Behari Narain Raizada (Saxena)`** in a graceful flowing **italic** style over six months on parchment sheets. When Jawaharlal Nehru asked about his fee, Raizada famously declined payment, requesting only that he be allowed to write his own name on every page and his grandfather's name (*Master Ramprasadji Saxena*) alongside his on the final page.
- **Beautification & Illumination:** Every page border and Part opening was illuminated by artists from **Kala Bhavana, Santiniketan**, led by **`Nandalal Bose`**. Specifically, **`Beohar Rammanohar Sinha`**—Bose's disciple—illuminated, beautified, and ornamented the **original Preamble** (delicately signing his short name "Ram" at its lower corner).
- **Hindi Calligraphy:** The original **Hindi version** of the Constitution was calligraphed by **`Vasant Krishan Vaidya`** and elegantly decorated and illuminated by **Nandalal Bose**. Today, the original handwritten English and Hindi manuscripts are preserved in specially engineered inert-gas (helium/nitrogen) climate-controlled cases in the **Library of the Parliament of India**.

> [!example]
> **The 22 Visual Panels of the Original Constitution:** Nandalal Bose's Santiniketan team illustrated each of the Constitution's **22 Parts** with sequential scenes tracing 5,000 years of Indian civilisation—from the **Mohenjodaro bull seal** (Part I) and a **Vedic *gurukul*** (Part II) through **Rama, Sita & Lakshmana returning from Lanka** (Part III: Fundamental Rights), **Krishna counselling Arjuna on the Gita** (Part IV: DPSPs), **Gautama Buddha** (Part V), **Mahavira** (Part VI), **Emperor Ashoka**, **Gupta art & Vikramaditya's court**, **Nalanda University**, **Nataraja**, **Akbar's court**, **Chhatrapati Shivaji & Guru Gobind Singh**, **Rani Lakshmibai & Tipu Sultan**, **Mahatma Gandhi's Dandi March & Noakhali peace walk**, and **Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose**, concluding with the Himalayas, desert, and oceans—visually grounding a modern liberal republic in India's plural cultural heritage.

#### Authoritative Text in the Hindi Language (`Article 394A` — `58th Amendment Act, 1987`)

Originally, the Constitution of `1950` contained **no provision** conferring formal legal status upon an authoritative Hindi text (even though members signed a Hindi manuscript on `24 January 1950`). To rectify this gap and satisfy longstanding parliamentary demand, Parliament enacted the **`58th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1987`**, which inserted **`Article 394A`** into **Part XXII** of the Constitution.

Under `Article 394A`, the **President of India** is mandated to cause to be published under his authority:
1. The **translation of the Constitution in the Hindi language**, signed by the members of the Constituent Assembly, with such modifications as necessary to align it with the language, style, and terminology adopted in the authoritative texts of Central Acts in Hindi, incorporating all amendments made prior to publication; and
2. The **translation in the Hindi language of every subsequent Constitutional Amendment** made in English.

The translation published under `Article 394A` is deemed to be the **authoritative text in Hindi for all purposes**, carrying the exact same legal meaning as the original English text; if any difficulty arises in construing any provision, the **President** shall cause the Hindi text to be suitably revised.

## 6. Criticism, Defence & Constitutional Philosophy

Few founding assemblies in modern history have operated under more daunting historical pressures—simultaneous decolonisation, violent Partition, massive refugee displacement, the integration of 552 princely states, acute poverty, and deep linguistic and social diversity—yet produced a constitutional charter that has sustained unbroken electoral democracy for over seven and a half decades. Even so, both colonial observers and Indian scholars subjected the Constituent Assembly to six classical lines of criticism. For UPSC Mains (GS-1 Post-Independence Consolidation and GS-2 Indian Polity), mastering **both the criticisms and their nuanced constitutional rebuttals**—anchored in **Granville Austin's** scholarship and **Dr B.R. Ambedkar's `25 November 1949` closing address**—separates an average descriptive script from a top-scoring analytical answer.

### Six Classical Criticisms of the Constituent Assembly & Their Rebuttal

Critics from diverse ideological standpoints—British conservatives, Marxist commentators, constitutional jurists, and dissenting Assembly members themselves—challenged the Assembly's legitimacy, composition, speed, and autonomy along **six principal grounds**:

1. **Not a Representative Body:** Critics argued that the Constituent Assembly was not directly elected by the people of India on the basis of universal adult franchise; British Indian members were indirectly chosen by provincial legislatures elected on a narrow ~10–14% property-and-education franchise, while princely states' representatives were largely nominated by rulers.
   - *Rebuttal:* Conducting direct universal suffrage elections across an undivided subcontinent of ~400 million people in `1946` would have delayed both constitution-making and Independence by years amid escalating communal strife. Even without direct election, the Assembly drew in every major community, region, and ideological stream, invited **eight months of open public scrutiny (`Feb–Oct 1948`)** on its Draft Constitution, and immediately subjected the resulting republic to the world's largest **universal adult franchise election (`1951–52`)**—where the electorate overwhelmingly ratified the constitutional order and its principal architects.
2. **Not a Sovereign Body:** Some critics contended that because the Constituent Assembly was created under the **Cabinet Mission Plan** formulated by the British Government and initially held sessions with British permission, it lacked original revolutionary sovereignty.
   - *Rebuttal:* Whatever formal constraints existed prior to August `1947` were obliterated both politically and legally. Politically, the Assembly asserted popular sovereignty in its very opening months via the **Objectives Resolution (`13 Dec 1946`)** and refused to let the British Crown dissolve or dictate its proceedings. Legally, the **`Indian Independence Act, 1947`** recognised its full sovereignty; when it adopted the Constitution on `26 November 1949`, it neither sought British Royal Assent nor Westminster's ratification—and boldly **repealed the Indian Independence Act itself** via `Article 395`, breaking the chain of colonial legal continuity (an act of *autochthonous* constitutional creation).
3. **Time-Consuming ("Drifting Committee"):** Critics faulted the Assembly for taking **`2 years, 11 months, and 18 days`**, contrasting it unfavorably with the **American Constitutional Convention at Philadelphia (`1787`)**, which drafted the US Constitution in barely **four months**. Inside the Assembly itself, **`Naziruddin Ahmed`** coined the famous sarcastic pun calling the Drafting Committee the **"Drifting Committee"** to mock its perceived slowness and frequent revisions.
   - *Rebuttal:* The comparison with `1787` Philadelphia is structurally flawed. The original US Constitution was a terse **7-Article** compact for thirteen small, relatively homogeneous coastal states—leaving state constitutions separate and sidestepping slavery (which triggered a bloody Civil War seventy years later). India's Assembly simultaneously framed the constitutions of both the **Union and all States**, integrated **552 princely states**, safeguarded diverse tribal belts (`5th & 6th Schedules`), designed affirmative action and fundamental rights, and deliberated across **2,473 floor amendments** while also functioning as the Dominion Legislature governing a partitioned country. Deliberative patience was the very foundation of the Constitution's durability.
4. **Dominated by the Congress:** Critics alleged that the Assembly was effectively a single-party chamber where Congress imposed its partisan will. Celebrated American constitutional scholar **`Granville Austin`** famously summarised this structural reality: *"The Constituent Assembly was a one-party body in an essentially one-party country. The Assembly was the Congress and the Congress was India."*
   - *Rebuttal:* Crucially, **Granville Austin himself supplied the most powerful refutation** of the charge of partisan authoritarianism. Far from being a monolithic ideological sect, the Indian National Congress of `1946–1949` was an expansive national umbrella housing Gandhian decentralisers, Fabian socialists, conservative industrialists, secular liberals, and orthodox traditionalists—meaning fierce ideological battles played out *inside* Congress ranks. Moreover, the Congress High Command deliberately used its electoral strength to bring **non-Congress legal and intellectual luminaries** into the Assembly and its key committees—including **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** (Drafting Committee Chairman), **Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar**, **N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar**, **H.N. Kunzru**, **Dr S. Radhakrishnan**, **Dr Shyama Prasad Mukherji**, **H.C. Mukherjee**, and **Frank Anthony**.
5. **Lawyer–Politician Domination:** Critics observed that legal practitioners and career politicians overwhelmingly dominated the Assembly, leaving peasants, industrial workers, artisans, and scientists thinly represented—and argued that this professional tilt accounted for the Constitution's elephantine size, dense legal prose, and technical complexity (later prompting British jurist **Sir Ivor Jennings** to dub it a *"lawyer's paradise"*).
   - *Rebuttal:* Translating political ideals into an enforceable federal division of powers, writ jurisdictions, emergency safeguards, and minority protections demanded rigorous legal craftsmanship; without jurists like Ambedkar, Ayyar, Munshi, and B.N. Rau, constitutional ambiguities could have paralysed the fledgling republic. At the same time, social reformers, Gandhian field workers, women activists, and tribal representatives ensured that legal precision never eclipsed transformative goals like **abolition of untouchability (`Art. 17`)**, **village panchayats (`Art. 40`)**, and **socio-economic Directive Principles (`Part IV`)**.
6. **Dominated by Hindus:** Imperial detractors attacked the Assembly as a sectarian body. **`Lord Viscount Simon`** dismissed the Constituent Assembly as **"a body of Hindus,"** while former British Prime Minister **`Winston Churchill`** contemptuously remarked that the Assembly represented **"only one major community in India."**
   - *Rebuttal:* This colonial charge confused demographic majority with majoritarianism—and ignored that the Muslim League's boycott was self-chosen while nationalist Muslims (Maulana Azad, Rafi Ahmed Kidwai, Syed Mohammad Saadullah, Begum Aizaz Rasul, Tajamul Husain), Sikhs, Indian Christians, Parsis, Anglo-Indians, Dalits, and Adivasis actively shaped every major committee. Most decisively, the proof lay in the document itself: despite the trauma of religious Partition, a Hindu-majority Assembly rejected theocratic majoritarianism and crafted a **secular republic** guaranteeing equal citizenship, freedom of conscience and religion (`Articles 25–28`), and robust cultural and educational rights for religious and linguistic minorities (`Articles 29–30`).

| Criticism | Key Critic / Catchphrase | Balanced Constitutional Defence |
|---|---|---|
| **Not Representative** | Indirect election; limited ~10–14% franchise; princely nominees | Direct adult polls would have delayed freedom; socially broad membership; 8-month public consultation; validated by `1951–52` universal franchise election |
| **Not Sovereign** | Created via British Cabinet Mission Plan | Objectives Resolution asserted popular sovereignty; `IIA 1947` confirmed full sovereignty; enacted without British assent and repealed `IIA 1947` (`Art. 395`) |
| **Time-Consuming** | `2y 11m 18d` vs 4 months for USA; **Naziruddin Ahmed's** *"Drifting Committee"* | Framed Union + State charters (395 Arts), integrated 552 princely states, ran Dominion Parliament simultaneously, debated 2,473 amendments |
| **Congress-Dominated** | **Granville Austin:** *"The Assembly was the Congress and the Congress was India"* | Congress was a plural umbrella; deliberately inducted non-Congress titans (Ambedkar, Ayyar, Kunzru, Mukherjee, Mukherji); worked via consensus |
| **Lawyer–Politician Heavy** | Complex legalism; **Sir Ivor Jennings's** *"lawyer's paradise"* | Legal mastery prevented federal & judicial collapse; balanced by Gandhian, women, Dalit, and tribal social reformers |

> [!debate]
> **Was the Constituent Assembly an Elite Conclave or a Deliberative Miracle?**
> - **Critical View:** Historians and political theorists note that indirect election on a restricted franchise excluded the direct voice of landless labourers and poor peasants, while strong centralisation and preventive detention clauses (`Art. 22`) reflected anxieties of an educated governing elite worried about post-Partition disorder.
> - **Institutional Defence:** Comparative constitutional scholarship (Granville Austin, Rajeev Bhargava, Rohit De) highlights that almost no other postcolonial assembly voluntarily enfranchised hundreds of millions of poor, illiterate citizens on Day One (`Art. 326`), abolished hereditary privilege and untouchability (`Arts. 17–18`), and subjected state power to independent judicial review. Its legitimacy rested less on procedural perfection in `1946` than on its **inclusive deliberation** and **transformative outcomes** in `1950`.

### Granville Austin's 'Seamless Web' & the Method of Consensus and Accommodation

In his seminal work ***The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation (`1966`)***, American historian **Granville Austin** distilled the animating philosophy of the Constituent Assembly into two celebrated analytical concepts that remain gold-standard citations in UPSC Mains answers:

#### The "Seamless Web" of Three Interdependent Strands

Austin observed that India's framers did not view the Constitution merely as a neutral mechanical rulebook separating powers. Instead, they wove a **"seamless web"** consisting of **three inseparable, mutually reinforcing strands**:

1. **Protecting and Enhancing National Unity and Integrity:** Forged in the crucible of Partition and princely fragmentation, this strand explains India's strong federal Centre, single citizenship, unified judicial hierarchy, All-India Services, and emergency provisions—designed to hold a subcontinental civilisation together without crushing regional languages or cultures.
2. **Establishing the Institutions and Spirit of Political Democracy:** Embodied in universal adult suffrage (`Art. 326`), an independent Election Commission (`Art. 324`), collective parliamentary responsibility (`Art. 75(3)`), and enforceable civil liberties (`Part III`)—ensuring that governance remained continuously accountable to ordinary citizens.
3. **Fostering a Social Revolution:** Perhaps the Assembly's boldest ambition—using law and democratic statecraft to dismantle centuries of caste oppression, gender subordination, feudal land concentration, illiteracy, and mass poverty through **Fundamental Rights** (especially `Articles 15, 17, 23`), **Directive Principles of State Policy (`Part IV`)**, and affirmative action.

Austin emphasised why these three strands form a **seamless web**: undue stress on any single strand risks unravelling the other two. Pursuing national unity at the expense of democracy degenerates into authoritarianism; operating formal political democracy while neglecting social revolution deepens inequality until citizens lose faith in constitutional institutions; pursuing social transformation through undemocratic coercion destroys liberty itself.

#### Decision-Making by Consensus, Principle of Accommodation & the "Oligarchy"

How did a fractious assembly of nearly 300 strong-minded personalities avoid splitting over explosive questions like official language, federalism, property rights, or minority safeguards? Austin identified **two distinctively Indian contributions to the art of constitution-making**:

- **Decision-Making by Consensus:** Rather than ramming contentious articles through by a brute Congress majority vote, the leadership consistently postponed votes, referred disputes to informal sub-committees and Congress Assembly Party caucuses, and kept negotiating until near-unanimous consent emerged (as seen in the **Munshi–Ayyangar Formula** on Hindi and English as official languages under `Article 343`, and the voluntary relinquishment of religious quotas in the Minorities Sub-Committee).
- **The Principle of Accommodation:** The framers rejected dogmatic ideological purity in favour of creative synthesis—accommodating seemingly opposing concepts within one framework: **federalism** with strong **unitary centralisation**; **parliamentary supremacy** with **judicial review**; **republican sovereignty** with **Commonwealth membership**; and **modern individual rights** alongside **group/cultural protections** and **Directive Principles**.

Austin also noted the stabilising role of an informal four-member leadership circle—which he termed the **"Oligarchy"**—comprising **Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, and Dr Rajendra Prasad**. Crucially, this leadership exercised *moral persuasion rather than authoritarian control*, routinely bowing to forceful interventions by backbenchers and independent voices such as **H.V. Kamath**, **K.T. Shah**, **Shibban Lal Saxena**, **Mahavir Tyagi**, **Jaipal Singh Munda**, and **Begum Aizaz Rasul**.

### Dr B.R. Ambedkar's Historic Closing Address (25 November 1949)

On **`25 November 1949`**, the eve of the Constitution's adoption, **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** rose to deliver his reply to the Third Reading debate. Widely regarded as the single greatest speech in India's constitutional history, it moved beyond legal technicalities to offer a sober, prophetic meditation on what it takes for a young democracy to survive.

Ambedkar began by reminding citizens that no written parchment can guarantee liberty on its own—its success depends entirely on the **constitutional morality** of the people and the political parties who operate it:

> "I feel, however good a Constitution may be, it is sure to turn out bad because those who are called to work it, happen to be a bad lot. However bad a Constitution may be, it may turn out to be good if those who are called to work it, happen to be a good lot." — Dr B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 25 November 1949

He then issued **three enduring warnings**—the core prerequisites for preserving Indian democracy not merely in form, but in fact:

1. **Hold Fast to Constitutional Methods — Abandon the "Grammar of Anarchy":** Ambedkar warned that while revolutionary uprisings, civil disobedience, non-cooperation, and *satyagraha* had been justified against an unaccountable colonial regime where no constitutional remedies existed, persisting with unconstitutional agitation inside a democratic republic would undermine the rule of law itself. Where constitutional methods are open, such methods are *"nothing but the Grammar of Anarchy and the sooner they are abandoned, the better for us."*
2. **Beware of *Bhakti* (Hero-Worship) in Politics:** Invoking **John Stuart Mill's** caution to all democratic peoples not *"to lay their liberties at the feet of even a great man, or to trust him with powers which enable him to subvert their institutions,"* Ambedkar noted that while gratitude to great leaders has legitimate limits, blind devotion in public life is lethal to republican freedom:

> "Bhakti in religion may be a road to the salvation of the soul. But in politics, Bhakti or hero-worship is a sure road to degradation and to eventual dictatorship." — Dr B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 25 November 1949

3. **Make Political Democracy a Social Democracy — Resolving the "Life of Contradictions":** Ambedkar's most incisive warning targeted India's entrenched caste hierarchy and stark economic disparity. Political equality alone (`one man, one vote and one vote, one value`) could not survive indefinitely alongside social and economic exclusion (`denying one man, one value`):

> "On the 26th of January 1950, we are going to enter into a life of contradictions. In politics we will have equality and in social and economic life we will have inequality... How long shall we continue to live this life of contradictions? How long shall we continue to deny equality in our social and economic life? If we continue to deny it for long, we will do so only by putting our political democracy in peril. We must remove this contradiction at the earliest possible moment or else those who suffer from inequality will blow up the structure of political democracy which this Assembly has so laboriously built up." — Dr B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 25 November 1949

Ambedkar defined **social democracy** as a way of life recognising **liberty, equality, and fraternity** as an indivisible **"union of trinity"**: to divorce one from the other is to defeat the very purpose of democracy—without equality, liberty produces the supremacy of the few over the many; without liberty, equality kills individual initiative; and without **fraternity** (a sense of common brotherhood across castes and communities), liberty and equality can never become a natural course of social life, requiring a constable to enforce them.

## 1. Evolution of the Demand for a Constituent Assembly

- **Early Precedents:** Tilak's Swaraj Bill (`1895`), Gandhi in *Young India* (`5 Jan 1922`: Swaraj as India's own declared wish), Swaraj Party resolution (`1924`), Motilal **Nehru Report (`Aug 1928`)**.
- **Explicit Demand:** **`M.N. Roy` (`1934`)** first proposed a Constituent Assembly; **INC (`1935`)** made it an official demand (reaffirmed at Lucknow & Faizpur `1936`).
- **Nehru (`1938`):** Declared free India's Constitution must be framed **without outside interference** by a Constituent Assembly elected on **adult franchise**.
- **British Stages:** **August Offer (`8 Aug 1940`)** accepted in principle (*"primarily"* Indian; minority veto) → **Cripps Mission (`March 1942`)** proposed post-war *"solely"* Indian framing with provincial opt-out (rejected by INC & League; League demanded **two** Constituent Assemblies) → **Cabinet Mission Plan (`16 May 1946` — Pethick-Lawrence, Cripps, A.V. Alexander)** rejected two assemblies and laid down the operational **389-member** scheme.

## 2. Composition & Election of the Constituent Assembly

- **Constituted:** **`November 1946`** under the Cabinet Mission Plan; **total strength `389`**:
  - **British India (`296`):** **`292`** from 11 Governors' Provinces + **`4`** from Chief Commissioners' Provinces (**`A-B-C-D`**: **Ajmer-Merwara, British Baluchistan, Coorg, Delhi**).
  - **Princely States (`93`):** Nominated by heads of states (stayed away initially; 6 states—Baroda, Bikaner, Jaipur, Patiala, Rewa, Udaipur—joined `28 April 1947`).
- **Allocation & Mode:** Roughly **1 seat per million (`10 lakh`)** population; provincial seats divided among **Muslims, Sikhs** (Punjab only), and **General**; British Indian members **indirectly elected** by community MLAs in provincial assemblies (elected early `1946` on ~10–14% limited franchise) via **PR-STV** → **partly elected, partly nominated**.
- **July–August `1946` Results (`296` British India seats):** **INC `208`**, **Muslim League `73`**, **Small Groups & Independents `15`** (Unionist 1, Unionist Muslims 1, Unionist SCs 1, Krishak Praja 1, SCF 1 [Ambedkar originally from Bengal, later re-elected from Bombay after Partition], Non-Congress Sikhs 1, CPI 1 [Somnath Lahiri], Independents 8).
- **Notable Absences & Inclusion:** **Mahatma Gandhi** was **NOT** a member (nor Jinnah participating). Included **15 Women Members**: Ammu Swaminathan, Annie Mascarene, **Begum Aizaz Rasul** (only Muslim woman), **Dakshayani Velayudhan** (only Dalit woman), Durgabai Deshmukh, Hansa Mehta, Kamla Chaudhry, Leela Roy, Malati Choudhury, Purnima Banerjee, **Rajkumari Amrit Kaur** (only woman in 1st Cabinet), Renuka Ray, Sarojini Naidu, Sucheta Kripalani, Vijaya Lakshmi Pandit.

## 3. Working of the Assembly & the Objectives Resolution

- **First Sitting (`9 Dec 1946`):** Boycotted by Muslim League; **`211` members** attended (207 signed register Day 1). **`Dr Sachchidananda Sinha`** (oldest member, **French practice**) elected **temporary President** (Frank Anthony temporary Deputy Chairman).
- **Office Bearers:** **`Dr Rajendra Prasad`** elected permanent President (`11 Dec 1946`); **two Vice-Presidents** — **`H.C. Mukherjee`** (`25 Jan 1947`) and **`V.T. Krishnamachari`** (`16 July 1948`); **`Sir B.N. Rau`** = Constitutional Advisor (**NOT** a CA member; prepared initial Oct `1947` draft of 243 Arts/13 Schedules); **H.V.R. Iyengar** = Secretary; **S.N. Mukerjee** = Chief Draftsman.
- **Objectives Resolution:** Moved by **Nehru (`13 Dec 1946`)**; adopted unanimously **`22 Jan 1947`**. Eight pledges: Independent Sovereign Republic; Union of British India & States; autonomous units with residuary powers *(reversed to Union Parliament `Art. 248` after Partition)*; popular sovereignty; social/economic/political justice, equality & freedoms; safeguards for minorities, tribals & depressed classes; territorial integrity; world peace. Modified version became the **Preamble** (with *"Fraternity"* added).
- **Changes by Indian Independence Act `1947`:** (1) **Fully sovereign** (could repeal any British Act including `IIA 1947`); (2) **Dual Role on separate days** — Constituent Body chaired by **`Dr Rajendra Prasad`** & Dominion Legislature chaired by **`G.V. Mavalankar`** (first met as legislature `17 Nov 1947`; dual role till `26 Nov 1949`; Provisional Parliament `26 Jan 1950 – May 1952`); (3) **Strength cut `389 → 299`** (**`229`** Indian Provinces + **`70`** Princely States).

## 4. Committees of the Constituent Assembly

- **8 Major Committees:** **Union Powers**, **Union Constitution**, **States Committee** *(Nehru)*; **Provincial Constitution**, **Advisory Committee on FRs, Minorities & Tribal/Excluded Areas** *(Patel)*; **Drafting Committee** *(Ambedkar)*; **Rules of Procedure**, **Steering** *(Rajendra Prasad)*.
- **5 Advisory Sub-Committees:** **Fundamental Rights** (`J.B. Kripalani`), **Minorities** (`H.C. Mukherjee`), **NE Frontier & Assam Excluded/Partially Excluded Areas** (`Gopinath Bardoloi` → **6th Schedule**), **Excluded/Partially Excluded Areas other than Assam** (`A.V. Thakkar` → **5th Schedule**), **NW Frontier Tribal Areas** *(infructuous after Partition)*.
- **Key Minor Committees:** Finance & Staff (`Rajendra Prasad`), Credentials (`Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar`), House (`B. Pattabhi Sitaramayya`), Order of Business (`K.M. Munshi`), **Ad-hoc National Flag (`Dr Rajendra Prasad` — NOT Kripalani!)**, Functions of CA (`G.V. Mavalankar`), Chief Commissioners' Provinces (`Pattabhi Sitaramayya`), Special Committee on Draft (`Nehru`), Press Gallery (`Usha Nath Sen`). **3 Non-Member Chairmen:** **`S. Varadachariar`** (Ad-hoc Supreme Court & Ad-hoc Citizenship), **`Nalini Ranjan Sarkar`** (Financial Provisions), **`S.K. Dar`** (Linguistic Provinces Commission).
- **Drafting Committee (`29 Aug 1947`, 7 members):** **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** (Chairman — *"Father of Constitution" / "Modern Manu"*), **N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar**, **Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar**, **Dr K.M. Munshi**, **Syed Mohammad Saadullah**, **N. Madhava Rau** *(replaced **B.L. Mitter**, ill health)*, **T.T. Krishnamachari** *(replaced **D.P. Khaitan**, died `1948`)*. Sat **`141` days**; First Draft `Feb 1948` → 8 months public discussion → Second Draft `Oct 1948`. *(Nehru, Patel, Prasad, B.N. Rau were NOT on the Drafting Committee.)*

## 5. Enactment, Enforcement & Key Milestones

- **Three Readings:** **1st** (`4–9 Nov 1948`, Ambedkar introduced final draft); **2nd** (`15 Nov 1948 – 17 Oct 1949`, clause-by-clause, **`7,653` amendments proposed / `2,473` discussed**); **3rd** (`14–26 Nov 1949`, passed **`26 Nov 1949`**).
- **Stats:** **`11` Sessions** (+ final sitting `24 Jan 1950`), **`2 years, 11 months, 18 days`**, **`165` sitting days** (`114` on Draft), ~**`60` countries** studied, cost ~**`₹64 lakh`**. Originally **Preamble** *(enacted AFTER the Constitution)* + **`395` Articles (22 Parts)** + **`8` Schedules**; signed by **`284` of `299` members** (`24 Jan 1950`).
- **Two Commencement Dates:** **`26 Nov 1949`** (`Art. 394`) enforced **`16` immediate Articles**: **`5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 60, 324, 366, 367, 379, 380, 388, 391, 392, 393 & 394`**. **`26 Jan 1950`** (full commencement — **Purna Swaraj Day `26 Jan 1930`** from Lahore Session `Dec 1929`): `Art. 395` repealed **`IIA 1947`** & **`GoI Act 1935`** **EXCEPT `Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act, 1949`**; SC inaugurated `28 Jan 1950`.
- **Other Functions:** National Flag (`22 Jul 1947`); Commonwealth membership (`May 1949`); National Anthem, National Song & First President Dr Rajendra Prasad (`24 Jan 1950`).
- **Interim Govt (`2 Sept 1946`; Viceroy President, Nehru Vice-President) vs 1st Cabinet (`15 Aug 1947`, Nehru PM) Key Swaps:** Finance (*Liaquat Ali Khan* → *R.K. Shanmukham Chetty*), Law (*Jogendra Nath Mandal* → *B.R. Ambedkar*), Education (*Rajagopalachari* → *Maulana Azad*), Health (*Ghaznafar Ali Khan* → *Rajkumari Amrit Kaur*), Railways (*Asaf Ali* → *John Mathai*), Industries (*John Mathai* → *Shyama Prasad Mukherji*), Commerce (*I.I. Chundrigar* → *C.H. Bhabha*), Works/Mines/Power (*C.H. Bhabha* → *V.N. Gadgil*), Posts/Comm (*Abdur Rab Nishtar* → *Rafi Ahmed Kidwai*). Retained: Nehru (EA), Patel (Home/I&B + States), Rajendra Prasad (Food/Agri), Baldev Singh (Defence), Jagjivan Ram (Labour).
- **Expert Committee of Congress (`8 July 1946`):** 8 members (**Nehru** Chairman, Asaf Ali, Munshi, Gopalaswami Ayyangar, K.T. Shah, D.R. Gadgil, Humayun Kabir, K. Santhanam + convener Krishna Kripalani); sittings in Delhi (`July 1946`) & Bombay (`Aug 1946`).
- **Craftsmanship & Hindi Text:** Symbol = **Elephant**; English calligrapher = **Prem Behari Narain Raizada** (italic); illumination = **Nandalal Bose** (Santiniketan) & **Beohar Rammanohar Sinha** (Preamble); Hindi calligrapher = **Vasant Krishan Vaidya**. **`Article 394A`** (**`58th Amendment Act, 1987`**) empowers **President** to publish authoritative Hindi text. **Samvidhan Divas (`26 Nov`)** since `2015`; 75th anniversary (`26 Nov 2024`, Samvidhan Sadan) released **Sanskrit & Maithili** editions.

## 6. Criticism, Defence & Constitutional Philosophy

- **6 Criticisms:** (1) Not representative (indirect/limited franchise), (2) Not sovereign (Cabinet Mission origin), (3) Time-consuming (**Naziruddin Ahmed's** *"Drifting Committee"*), (4) Congress-dominated (**Granville Austin:** *"Assembly was the Congress..."*, yet Austin defended Congress's umbrella character & non-Congress induction), (5) Lawyer–politician dominated (**Ivor Jennings:** *"lawyer's paradise"*), (6) Hindu-dominated (**Lord Viscount Simon** *"body of Hindus"*; **Churchill** *"one major community"*).
- **Granville Austin's Philosophy:** **"Seamless Web"** = **National Unity + Political Democracy + Social Revolution**; methods = **Decision-Making by Consensus** & **Principle of Accommodation**; 4-leader **"Oligarchy"** (Nehru, Patel, Azad, Prasad).
- **Ambedkar's `25 Nov 1949` Closing Warnings:** (1) Abandon unconstitutional **"Grammar of Anarchy"**; (2) Avoid ***Bhakti* (hero-worship) in politics** (citing J.S. Mill — leads to degradation & dictatorship); (3) Resolve **"life of contradictions"** (political equality vs socio-economic inequality) by forging **social democracy** built on the **"union of trinity"** (**liberty, equality, fraternity**). Constitutional survival depends on the character of those who work it.
