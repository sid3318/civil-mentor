## 1. After 1857: the Crown takes over

The Revolt of 1857 convinced the British Parliament that India could no longer be governed through a trading company. The Government of India Act, 1858, transferred the government, territories and revenues of India from the East India Company to the British Crown. Over the next ninety years Parliament passed a series of Acts that slowly widened Indian participation, at first in debate, then in law-making, and finally in government. Each step came only after Indian pressure, and each was hedged with safeguards for British control. With the previous note, this completes the historical background of the Constitution, from the Regulating Act of 1773 to the Indian Independence Act of 1947.

```text
1858  Crown rule; Secretary of State, Viceroy
  │
1861  Indians nominated; provinces legislate again
  │
1892  Budget debate; indirect election begins
  │
1909  Elected members; separate electorates
  │
1919  Dyarchy in provinces; two Houses at Centre
  │
1935  Provincial autonomy; federal blueprint
  │
1947  Independence; two dominions
```

Two threads run through this period, and Mains answers in both GS-1 and GS-2 should pick them up. One is **representation**: who sat in the councils, and how they got there. The other is **responsibility**: whether the people who governed answered to the legislature. Representation came first and grew steadily. Real responsibility arrived only in the provinces, and only in 1937.

## 2. The Government of India Act, 1858

The Act of 1858 abolished the Company's rule and with it the system of double government.

- India was now to be governed by, and in the name of, Her Majesty.
- The Governor-General became the **Viceroy of India**, the Crown's direct representative. Lord Canning was the first.
- The Board of Control and the Court of Directors were abolished.
- A new office, the **Secretary of State for India**, took their powers. He was a member of the British Cabinet, answerable to Parliament, and was assisted by a 15-member advisory **Council of India**. He was also made a body corporate, able to sue and be sued in England and India.

The Act changed who controlled the government of India from London much more than it changed how India was governed. Queen Victoria's Proclamation, read out by Canning at Allahabad on 1 November 1858, set the tone: no further annexations, respect for the rights and dignity of the princes, non-interference in religion, and equal treatment under the law.

> **Prelims pointer:** The 1858 Act was "largely confined to the improvement of the administrative machinery" by which the government of India was supervised and controlled in England. It did not introduce any representative element in India; that began only in 1861.

## 3. The Indian Councils Acts of 1861 and 1892

The Indian Councils Act, 1861, marks the beginning of representative institutions in India. It allowed the Viceroy to nominate Indians as non-official members of his expanded council for law-making. In 1862 Lord Canning nominated three: the Raja of Benares, the Maharaja of Patiala and Sir Dinkar Rao.

The Act also reversed the centralisation of 1833. It restored legislative powers to the Bombay and Madras Presidencies, beginning a process of legislative devolution that ended in near-complete autonomy for the provinces in 1937. New legislative councils followed for Bengal (1862), the North-Western Provinces (1886) and Punjab (1897). The Act gave statutory recognition to the **portfolio system**, which Lord Canning had introduced in 1859, under which each member of the Viceroy's council took charge of particular departments. It also empowered the Viceroy to issue **ordinances** without the council's consent during an emergency, valid for six months.

The Indian Councils Act, 1892, came after the founding of the Indian National Congress in 1885 and its demands for reform. It increased the number of non-official members in the central and provincial councils, while keeping an official majority. Members could now **discuss the budget** and **ask questions** of the executive. Most interestingly, the Act provided for the nomination of some non-official members on the recommendation of bodies such as the provincial legislative councils, the Bengal Chamber of Commerce, district boards, municipalities, universities and zamindars. The word "election" was never used, but this was a limited and indirect use of the principle of election.

## 4. The Indian Councils Act, 1909: the Morley–Minto reforms

Named after John Morley, the Secretary of State, and Lord Minto, the Viceroy, the 1909 Act enlarged the councils and, for the first time, brought in direct election of some members.

- The Central Legislative Council grew from 16 to 60 members. The provincial councils were enlarged as well.
- The Central Council kept an official majority, but the provincial councils could have a non-official majority.
- Members could ask supplementary questions and move resolutions on the budget.
- Indians entered the executive councils. Satyendra Prasanna Sinha became the first Indian member of the Viceroy's Executive Council, as Law Member.
- Presidency corporations, chambers of commerce, universities and zamindars got separate representation.

The Act's most consequential provision was **communal representation**. Muslim members were to be elected only by Muslim voters, in **separate electorates**. The demand had been put to Lord Minto by the Simla Deputation led by the Aga Khan in October 1906, the year the Muslim League was founded. By accepting it, the Act "legalised communalism", and Lord Minto came to be called the **Father of the Communal Electorate**.

> **Mains angle:** Separate electorates did not make Indian politics communal by themselves, but they built religious division into the structure of representation. Each later reform widened them: to Sikhs, Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans in 1919, and to the depressed classes, women and labour in 1935. A GS-1 answer on the roots of partition should trace this line from 1909 to 1947. It should also note the reforms' context, the Swadeshi movement and the Moderate–Extremist split at Surat (1907): the reforms were meant to rally the Moderates while dividing communities.

Morley himself told the House of Lords that if the reforms were going to lead to a parliamentary system in India, he would have nothing to do with them. The councils grew, but responsibility stayed where it was.

## 5. The Government of India Act, 1919: the Montagu–Chelmsford reforms

On 20 August 1917 the Secretary of State, Edwin Montagu, declared in the House of Commons that British policy was "the progressive realisation of responsible government in India". The Government of India Act, 1919, which came into force in 1921, was the first attempt to act on that promise. Lord Chelmsford was Viceroy.

### Dyarchy in the provinces

The Act separated subjects into a central list and a provincial list, and let provincial legislatures make laws on theirs. The structure of government nonetheless stayed centralised and unitary. Provincial subjects were split again into two groups:

- **Transferred subjects** (such as education, health and local self-government) were administered by the Governor acting with ministers responsible to the legislative council.
- **Reserved subjects** (such as law and order, police, land revenue and irrigation) were administered by the Governor and his executive council, who were not responsible to the legislature.

This dual scheme was called **dyarchy**, from the Greek *diarche*, "double rule". It largely failed. Ministers controlled neither the purse, which was in a reserved department, nor the civil servants, who looked to the Governor.

```text
              Provincial subjects
                      │
        ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐
   Transferred                   Reserved
   Governor + ministers          Governor + executive council
   answerable to legislature     not answerable to legislature
```

### The other changes

- The Centre got a **bicameral legislature**: an Upper House (Council of State) and a Lower House (Legislative Assembly). A majority of the members of both Houses were directly elected.
- Three of the six members of the Viceroy's Executive Council, other than the Commander-in-Chief, had to be Indians.
- Separate electorates were extended to Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans.
- The franchise was given to a limited number of people on the basis of property, tax or education.
- A **High Commissioner for India** was appointed in London and took over some of the Secretary of State's functions.
- A **Public Service Commission** was provided for. The Central Public Service Commission was set up in 1926, on the Lee Commission's recommendation.
- Provincial budgets were separated from the central budget, and provincial legislatures could enact their own budgets.
- A **statutory commission** was to report on the working of the Act after ten years.

The reforms arrived with the Rowlatt Act and the Jallianwala Bagh massacre of 1919, and the Congress answered with Non-Cooperation rather than co-operation. That is the GS-1 context: the reforms were too little for nationalists who now had a mass movement behind them.

## 6. The road to 1935

The statutory commission came two years early. The **Simon Commission**, appointed in November 1927 under Sir John Simon, had seven British members and no Indian. It was boycotted across India with the slogan "Simon, go back". Its report of 1930 recommended abolishing dyarchy, extending responsible government in the provinces, creating a federation of British India and the princely states, and continuing communal electorates.

Three **Round Table Conferences** in London (1930 to 1932) followed. Gandhi attended only the second, after the Gandhi–Irwin Pact. The British Prime Minister, Ramsay MacDonald, then announced the **Communal Award** (August 1932), which extended separate electorates to the depressed classes. Gandhi began a fast unto death in Yerawada jail against separating the depressed classes from the Hindu electorate. The **Poona Pact** of September 1932 between Gandhi and B.R. Ambedkar replaced separate electorates for the depressed classes with seats reserved for them within the general Hindu electorate, and roughly doubled the number of seats the Award had offered. A White Paper (1933) and a Joint Select Committee of the British Parliament then produced the Act of 1935.

## 7. The Government of India Act, 1935

The Act of 1935 was a long and detailed document of 321 sections and 10 schedules. It was the most important constitutional enactment of British rule, and much of the structural framework of the Constitution of 1950 was drawn from it.

### What it provided

- **An All-India Federation**, made up of the provinces and the princely states as units. Powers were divided among three lists: a **Federal List** (59 items), a **Provincial List** (54 items) and a **Concurrent List** (36 items). Residuary powers went to the Viceroy. The Federation never came into being, because the princely states did not join it.
- **Provincial autonomy.** Dyarchy in the provinces was abolished. The provinces became autonomous units with responsible government: the Governor was to act on the advice of ministers responsible to the provincial legislature. This part came into force in 1937 and was discontinued in 1939.
- **Dyarchy at the Centre**, with federal subjects divided into reserved and transferred. It never came into operation.
- **Bicameralism** in six of the eleven provinces: Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Bihar, Assam and the United Provinces, each with a legislative council and a legislative assembly.
- **Wider communal representation**, with separate electorates now extended to the depressed classes (Scheduled Castes), women and labour.
- **The end of the Council of India.** The Secretary of State was given a team of advisers instead.
- **A wider franchise.** About 10 per cent of the total population got the vote.
- **A Reserve Bank of India** to control the country's currency and credit. It was set up in 1935.
- **Public service commissions:** a Federal Public Service Commission, a Provincial Public Service Commission for each province, and Joint Public Service Commissions for two or more provinces.
- **A Federal Court**, which was set up in 1937.

> **Example:** The Constitution borrowed heavily from the 1935 Act: the federal scheme with its three lists, the office of Governor, the judiciary, the public service commissions, the emergency provisions and many administrative details. That is why the Constitution's structural part is often said to derive largely from it, while its philosophy came from the national movement.

Nehru, who had no illusions about it, described the Act as "a machine with strong brakes but no engine". It gave the Governor-General and the Governors special responsibilities and discretionary powers that could override ministers. But it also gave Indians their first real experience of responsible government.

### The 1937 elections

Elections to the provincial assemblies were held early in 1937 under the new franchise. The Congress won a clear majority in most provinces, and its ministries took office in eight of the eleven: Madras, Bombay, the Central Provinces, the United Provinces, Bihar, Orissa, the North-West Frontier Province and Assam. The Muslim League did poorly even in Muslim-majority provinces, a result that pushed it to mobilise more aggressively in the years that followed. The Congress ministries resigned in October and November 1939, after Britain declared India a party to the Second World War without consulting Indians.

> **Mains angle:** The 1937 ministries are a good example for "the experience of self-government before 1947". They passed tenancy and debt-relief laws, expanded education, and gave Congress leaders administrative experience. They also deepened Congress–League distrust, as the League accused the ministries of ignoring Muslim interests.

## 8. The Indian Independence Act, 1947

After the Second World War, the British Prime Minister Clement Attlee announced on 20 February 1947 that British rule in India would end by June 1948. The last Viceroy, Lord Mountbatten, put forward a plan on 3 June 1947 to partition the country, and it was accepted by the Congress and the Muslim League. The British Parliament gave it legal form in the Indian Independence Act, 1947, which received royal assent on 18 July 1947.

- It ended British rule and declared India an independent and sovereign state from **15 August 1947**.
- It partitioned the country into two independent dominions, India and Pakistan, each free to leave the British Commonwealth.
- It abolished the office of Viceroy and provided a **Governor-General** for each dominion, appointed by the British King on the advice of the dominion's cabinet. The British government ceased to be responsible for either.
- It empowered the **Constituent Assemblies** of the two dominions to frame and adopt any constitution, and to repeal any Act of the British Parliament, including the Independence Act itself. Each Assembly would also act as its dominion's legislature until a new constitution took effect.
- It abolished the office of Secretary of State for India and transferred his functions to the Secretary of State for Commonwealth Affairs.
- It proclaimed the **lapse of British paramountcy** over the princely states and of treaty relations with the tribal areas. The princely states were free to join India or Pakistan, or to remain independent.
- Until the new constitutions were framed, each dominion was to be governed under the Government of India Act, 1935, as adapted.
- The British monarch lost the right to veto bills or to ask for bills to be reserved for his approval. That right now belonged to the Governor-General.
- The Governor-General of India and the provincial governors became constitutional heads, bound to act on the advice of their ministers.
- The title "Emperor of India" was dropped from the royal titles.
- Appointments to the civil services by the Secretary of State ended. Civil servants appointed before 15 August 1947 kept their benefits.

At midnight on 14–15 August 1947 power passed to the two dominions. Lord Mountbatten became the first Governor-General of the new Dominion of India and swore in Jawaharlal Nehru as the first Prime Minister. In June 1948 C. Rajagopalachari succeeded Mountbatten; he was the last Governor-General, and the only Indian to hold the office.

> **Remember:** The Constituent Assembly, which had been meeting since December 1946, became a fully sovereign body under the Independence Act. It also became the first Parliament of free India: G.V. Mavalankar presided when it sat as a legislature, and Rajendra Prasad when it sat to make the Constitution.

## 9. The arc, and why it matters

| Act | Representation | Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| 1858 | None in India | To Parliament, through the Secretary of State |
| 1861 | Indians nominated to the councils | None |
| 1892 | Indirect election, under another name | None, though members could question and debate the budget |
| 1909 | Direct election, with separate electorates | None |
| 1919 | Elected majorities, wider communal electorates | Partial, in transferred provincial subjects |
| 1935 | Wider franchise; separate electorates for more groups | Full, in the provinces (from 1937) |
| 1947 | Constituent Assembly sovereign | Full, to Indians |

> **Mains angle:** For "trace the evolution of responsible government in India", these two columns make a clean structure. Show that representation came early but responsibility late, that every step was a response to Indian agitation (the Congress, the Swadeshi movement, Non-Cooperation, Civil Disobedience), and that communal electorates grew with each reform. End with the continuity: the 1935 Act's federal design, governors and emergency powers live on in the Constitution, now joined to universal adult franchise and Fundamental Rights.
