## 1. Single integrated judiciary and origin

Unlike the United States—where federal and state courts operate on separate tracks—India has a **single integrated judiciary** modelled on the Government of India Act, 1935. One pyramid enforces both Union and State laws, with the **Supreme Court** at the apex, High Courts below it, and subordinate courts at the base.

> "The Indian Federation though a Dual Polity has no Dual Judiciary at all. The High Courts and the Supreme Court form one single integrated Judiciary having jurisdiction and providing remedies in all cases arising under the constitutional law, the civil law or the criminal law." — B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 4 November 1948

```text
          ┌───────────────────────────────┐
          │    Supreme Court of India     │
          │    (Part V, Arts 124–147)     │
          └───────────────┬───────────────┘
                          │ Binding precedent (Art 141)
                          ▼
          ┌───────────────────────────────┐
          │        25 High Courts         │
          │    (Part VI, Arts 214–231)    │
          └───────────────┬───────────────┘
                          │ Control (Arts 227, 235)
                          ▼
          ┌───────────────────────────────┐
          │ District & Subordinate Courts │
          │    (Part VI, Arts 233–237)    │
          └───────────────────────────────┘
```

**Part V, Chapter IV (Articles 124–147)** governs the Court. Inaugurated on **28 January 1950**, it succeeded the **Federal Court of India** (1937) and ended the British **Privy Council's** appellate jurisdiction (1949 Act). Serving simultaneously as constitutional interpreter, federal umpire, rights guardian and final appellate court, its remit is broader than the US Supreme Court's.

**Sanctioned strength (Article 124(1)):** Originally **eight judges** (1 CJI + 7), strength is raised by **Parliament by ordinary law**—**NOT** an Article 368 constitutional amendment. Under the **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956**, Parliament raised it to 14 (1960), 18 (1977), 26 (1986), 31 (2009), and **34 (1 CJI + 33)** in 2019.

> **In the news:** **Strength raised to 38 (August 2026) and 53rd CJI.** Following an **11 May 2026** letter by **53rd CJI Justice Surya Kant** (sworn in **24 November 2025**, succeeding CJI B.R. Gavai; retiring **9 February 2027**) citing heavy Constitution Bench workloads such as the nine-judge Sabarimala reference, Parliament enacted the **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2026** (Act No. 14 of 2026; assented **11 August 2026**, replacing the May 2026 Ordinance and effective retrospectively from **16 May 2026**), raising strength from **34 to 38 (1 CJI + 37)**. As of **1 November 2026** (Department of Justice), working strength is **37** (one vacancy).

## 2. Qualifications, oath, tenure, seat and temporary judges

**Qualifications (Article 124(3)):** An appointee must be an **Indian citizen** and satisfy **any one** criterion: (a) **High Court judge** for **five years** (successive HCs count); OR (b) **High Court advocate** for **ten years**; OR (c) a **"distinguished jurist"** in the President's opinion—never yet used and **unavailable for High Courts**. **No minimum age** is prescribed.

**Oath, salary and tenure:** Judges take oath before the **President** (or appointee) under the **Third Schedule** (contrast the President's oath in Art 60). Salaries, allowances and pensions are fixed by **Parliament** (**Article 125**) and cannot be cut to a judge's disadvantage mid-term except during a **Financial Emergency** (**Art 360(4)(b)**). Judges hold office until **65 years** (**Art 124(2)**)—age disputes are decided as **Parliament** prescribes by law (**Art 124(2A)**, 15th Amendment 1963)—and resign to the **President**. **Article 124(7)** bars retired SC judges from pleading or acting before **any court or authority** in India, though post-retirement tribunal, executive or Rajya Sabha postings remain constitutionally unbarred and ethically debated.

| Temporary Judge | Who appoints / requests | Consent / Consultation | Purpose & Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acting CJI (Art 126)** | **President** appoints SC judge | None specified (convention: seniormost) | CJI office vacant, or CJI absent/unable to act |
| **Ad hoc (Art 127)** | **CJI** requests sitting **HC judge** | **President's prior consent** + **HC CJ** | Lack of **quorum** of permanent judges; full SC powers |
| **Retired (Art 128)** | **CJI** requests retired SC/Federal/qualified HC judge | **President's prior consent** + **judge's consent** | Temporary sittings; allowances by President; **not deemed SC judge** |

Under **Article 130**, the Court sits in **Delhi**, or other place(s) chosen by the **CJI with the President's approval**—purely discretionary, not enforceable by writ.

> **Prelims pointer:** Four traps in Articles 124–130: (1) SC strength rises by **ordinary law**, not Art 368; (2) **no minimum age** exists; (3) **"distinguished jurist"** applies **only to SC**, never HCs; and (4) **Acting CJI (Art 126)** is appointed by the **President**, while **Ad hoc (Art 127)** and **Retired (Art 128)** judges are requested by the **CJI** with President's consent.

## 3. Appointment of judges and the Collegium system

Under **Article 124(2)**, the **President** appoints SC judges after **"consultation"** with such SC and HC judges as deemed necessary; consulting the **CJI is mandatory** for all non-CJI appointments. In the Constituent Assembly, Ambedkar rejected executive monopoly (UK), Senate confirmation (US) and a CJI veto:

> "I personally feel no doubt that the Chief Justice is a very eminent person. But after all, the Chief Justice is a man with all the failings, all the sentiments and all the prejudices which we as common people have, and I think to allow the Chief Justice practically a veto upon the appointment of Judges is really to transfer the authority to the Chief Justice which we are not prepared to vest in the President or the Government of the day." — B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 24 May 1949

### Evolution across the Four Judges Cases

The seniority convention for appointing the CJI broke twice—when **Justice A.N. Ray** superseded three judges in **1973** (after *Kesavananda Bharati*) and **Justice M.H. Beg** superseded Justice H.R. Khanna in **1977** (after *ADM Jabalpur*). Across four rulings, the Court built the **Collegium system** (a term absent from the Constitution):

| Case & Year | Bench | Core Holding |
|---|---|---|
| **First Judges Case** (*S.P. Gupta*, 1981) | 7 judges | **"Consultation" ≠ "concurrence"**; executive enjoys **primacy** and may reject CJI's advice for cogent reasons. |
| **Second Judges Case** (*SCAORA*, 1993) | 9 judges | Overruled *S.P. Gupta*. Consultation = **concurrence**; **judicial primacy**; created Collegium (**CJI + 2** seniormost SC judges); codified CJI **seniority norm**. |
| **Third Judges Case** (*Special Ref. No. 1*, 1998) | 9 judges | Expanded Collegium to **CJI + 4** for SC (CJI + 2 for HCs). CJI's sole view invalid; **two dissents** block a name; **reiteration** binds the Centre. |
| **Fourth Judges Case** (*SCAORA*, 2015) | 5 judges (4:1) | Struck down **99th Amendment Act, 2014** & **NJAC Act, 2014** for violating **judicial independence (Basic Structure)**. Revived Collegium; sought revised **MoP**. |

In **2015**, the six-member **NJAC** (CJI, two seniormost SC judges, Union Law Minister and two "eminent persons", with a two-member veto) fell because executive and lay members could veto judicial choices. Dissenting alone, **Justice J. Chelameswar** faulted Collegium opacity and questioned equating judicial primacy with independence.

### Collegium critique and 2025–2026 transparency push

While defenders credit the Collegium with insulating the bench from partisan capture, critics cite four flaws: (1) **extra-constitutional opacity** ("judges appointing judges" without written criteria or a permanent secretariat); (2) the **MoP deadlock** stalled since 2016 over a "national security" veto; (3) the Centre's *de facto* **"pocket veto"** via indefinite delays and selective list-splitting; and (4) a **diversity deficit**. Since Justice **M. Fathima Beevi** (October 1989), only **13 women** have served on the SC. As of **November 2026**, just **three of 37** sitting SC judges are women—**Justice B.V. Nagarathna** (Collegium member, due to be India's **first woman CJI** in September 2027 for 36 days), **Justice V. Mohana** (Bar elevation, May 2026), and **Justice Sunita Agarwal** (October 2026). Parliament was told in early 2026 that of 847 HC judges appointed during 2018–January 2026, only 33 were SC, 17 ST, 104 OBC, 46 minorities, and 130 women.

> **In the news:** **Asset disclosure and Collegium records (April–May 2025).** On **1 April 2025**, the SC Full Court mandated publishing SC judges' asset declarations on its website (upgrading 1997 confidential and 2009 voluntary resolutions). On **5 May 2025**, it also uploaded the HC/SC appointment process and Collegium approval records (Nov 2022–May 2025), including candidates' social category and judicial-family ties.

## 4. Removal of judges and independence safeguards

Under **Article 124(4)**, a judge is removed by **Presidential order** after an address by **both Houses in the same session**, passed by **special majority**, strictly for **proved misbehaviour or incapacity**. Parliament regulates the inquiry under **Article 124(5)** via the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968**.

> **Remember:** The word **"impeachment"** is **NOT used** in Article 124 (or Article 217); it appears solely for the President (Article 61).

```text
  Motion signed by >= 100 LS MPs OR >= 50 RS MPs
                         │
                         ▼
   Speaker (LS) / Chairman (RS) may ADMIT or REFUSE
                         │ (if admitted)
                         ▼
  ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │    3-Member Inquiry Committee (1968 Act)       │
  │  SC Judge + HC Chief Justice + Dist. Jurist    │
  └──────────────────────┬─────────────────────────┘
                         │ (if charges proved)
                         ▼
   Passed by BOTH Houses in the SAME SESSION by:
   >50% of TOTAL membership + >=2/3rd PRESENT & VOTING
                         │
                         ▼
          President issues Order of Removal
```

**No SC or HC judge has ever been removed by Parliament.** Justice **V. Ramaswami (1993)** survived a Lok Sabha vote via abstentions despite adverse findings; HC judges **Soumitra Sen** and **P.D. Dinakaran** resigned in 2011 before final removal.

> **In the news:** **Justice Yashwant Varma inquiry (2025–2026).** After burnt currency notes were found at then Delhi HC judge Justice Yashwant Varma's residence (March 2025), he was repatriated to Allahabad HC and indicted by an SC in-house panel. On **12 August 2025**, Lok Sabha Speaker Om Birla admitted a motion by **146 MPs** and formed a three-member Judges Inquiry Committee (reconstituted with SC Justice Aravind Kumar, Bombay HC CJ Shree Chandrashekhar and Senior Advocate B.V. Acharya). Though Justice Varma **resigned on 9 April 2026**, the panel's report—tabled **12 August 2026**—held the charges **"proved"**. In a letter circulated to MPs in **November 2026**, he argued proceedings became **"infructuous"** post-resignation, highlighting an accountability gap when judges quit mid-inquiry.

**Constitutional safeguards of independence:** security of tenure (not President's pleasure); fixed service conditions reducible only under **Art 360**; salaries, pensions and staff expenses **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** (**Art 146(3)**); ban on legislative discussion of judicial conduct except on a removal motion (**Arts 121, 211**); **contempt power (Art 129)**; staff autonomy (**Art 146**); post-retirement practice bar (**Art 124(7)**); executive separation (**Art 50**); and Parliament's power only to **extend**, never curtail, SC jurisdiction (**Art 138**).

## 5. Jurisdiction and powers of the Supreme Court

| Jurisdiction | Article(s) | Core Scope & Key Exception |
|---|---|---|
| **Original & Exclusive** | **Art 131** (**Art 71**) | Federal legal disputes; excludes pre-Constitution treaties & Art 262 river waters. Art 71: President/VP poll disputes. |
| **Writ** | **Art 32** | Original (concurrent with HC Art 226), **only for Fundamental Rights**; itself a Fundamental Right. |
| **Appellate** | **Arts 132–134** | Constitutional, civil & criminal appeals from HCs (certificates via **Art 134A**). |
| **Special Leave (SLP)** | **Art 136** | Plenary discretionary appeal from **any court/tribunal**, **except Armed Forces courts/tribunals**. |
| **Advisory** | **Art 143(1)–(2)** | Presidential reference: *may* decline (143(1)), *must* answer (143(2)); **non-binding**. |
| **Court of Record** | **Art 129** | Evidentiary record + civil/criminal **contempt** power (covers HCs & lower courts too). |
| **Review & Equity** | **Arts 137, 142** | Self-review (30 days) + "complete justice"; joint basis for **Curative Petitions**. |
| **Precedent & Bench** | **Arts 141, 145(3)** | Binds all courts **except SC itself**; >= **5 judges** for constitutional questions & Art 143. |

### 5.1 Original and exclusive jurisdiction (Articles 131 and 71)

Under **Article 131**, the SC holds **original and exclusive** jurisdiction over federal legal disputes between: Centre and State(s); Centre + State(s) vs State(s); or two or more States. Purely political controversies and suits by **private citizens** are excluded, along with: pre-Constitution treaties/covenants/sanads (Art 131 proviso), **inter-state river water disputes** (**Art 262**), **Finance Commission** references, Centre–State expense/pension adjustments (Art 290), ordinary commercial disputes, and State claims for damages against the Centre. Separately, **Article 71** makes the SC sole arbiter of **President and Vice-President election disputes**.

> **Example:** In **July 2024**, the SC held maintainable West Bengal's Article 131 suit against unilateral CBI probes post-withdrawal of consent; in **April 2024**, it referred Kerala's Article 131 suit on Union borrowing caps to a five-judge Bench.

### 5.2 Writ and appellate jurisdiction (Articles 32, 132–136)

**Writ jurisdiction (Art 32)** gives direct access via the five prerogative writs solely for **Fundamental Rights**—original yet concurrent with HC Article 226. Narrower in scope than Article 226 (which covers legal rights too), Article 32 is itself a **Fundamental Right** and cannot be refused at discretion; Parliament may empower other courts locally (**Art 32(3)**, unused). (Detailed writ doctrine appears in the companion note on constitutional remedies.)

**Appellate channels (Arts 132–136):**
- **Constitutional (Art 132) & Civil (Art 133):** On an **Article 134A** HC certificate (44th Amendment, 1978) of a substantial constitutional question (Art 132) or a substantial question of law of general importance (Art 133; **30th Amendment, 1972** abolished the ₹20,000 limit).
- **Criminal (Art 134):** **As of right** if an HC reverses an acquittal to award **death** or withdraws a trial and imposes death—extended by Parliament's **1970 Act** to acquittal reversals carrying **life or 10+ years**—or on an HC certificate.
- **Special Leave Petition — SLP (Art 136):** Plenary **discretionary** appeal against any judgment, decree, sentence or order of **any court or tribunal** in India (even interlocutory orders), **EXCEPT Armed Forces courts/tribunals** (courts-martial; AFT appeals lie by statute). Liberal SLP admission drives docket overload—75,410 filings vs 65,615 disposals in 2025 pushed SC pendency to **92,755** by July 2026 (nearing one lakh by October 2026).

### 5.3 Advisory jurisdiction (Article 143)

Modelled on Section 213 of the 1935 Act (absent in the US), **Article 143** allows **Presidential references**: under **Art 143(1)** (law/fact of public importance), the SC **may** answer or **decline** (e.g., declined the 1993 Ayodhya reference in ***Ismail Faruqui*, 1994**); under **Art 143(2)** (pre-Constitution treaties excluded from Art 131), the SC **must** answer. Heard by >= **five judges** (**Art 145(3)**), opinions under **both** clauses are **advisory, not binding**, though strongly persuasive (*Berubari Union*, 1960; *Third Judges Case*, 1998).

> **In the news:** **Special Reference No. 1 of 2025 (20 November 2025).** Answering President Droupadi Murmu's 14-question **Article 143(1)** reference (13 May 2025) after *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu* (8 April 2025), a five-judge Bench led by CJI B.R. Gavai held courts cannot set rigid Bill-assent timelines under Articles 200–201 or invoke **Article 142** for **"deemed assent"**, while permitting limited mandamus against prolonged, unexplained inaction.

### 5.4 Court of Record, Complete Justice and institutional powers

- **Court of Record (Art 129):** Permanent evidentiary value + inherent power to punish **civil and criminal contempt** (Contempt of Courts Act, 1971), covering contempt of **High Courts and subordinate courts** too (***Delhi Judicial Service Association*, 1991**).
- **Complete Justice (Art 142):** Nationwide equity tool—invoked in ***Union Carbide* (1989)**, ***Ayodhya* (2019)**, ***Shilpa Sailesh* (2023)** (direct divorce for irretrievable breakdown of marriage), and ***Chandigarh Mayor* (Feb 2024)**—yet barred from overriding express substantive statutes (***SCBA*, 1998**) or express constitutional text (**Special Reference No. 1 of 2025**).
- **Precedent (Art 141) & Bench rules (Art 145(3)):** Law declared binds all Indian courts **except the SC itself** (***Bengal Immunity*, 1955**). Substantial constitutional questions and Art 143 references need **>= 5 judges**. The CJI is **"Master of the Roster"** (***Shanti Bhushan*, 2018**), and **Article 139A** (42nd Amendment) allows transferring HC cases or withdrawing shared substantial legal questions. (Judicial review and separation of powers are covered in their companion note.)

> **Prelims pointer:** Watch the five favourite Jurisdiction traps: **Art 131** excludes Art 262 river waters & pre-Constitution treaties; **Art 136** excludes **Armed Forces** courts/tribunals; **Art 143(1)** is optional while **143(2)** is mandatory, yet **both** are non-binding; **Art 141** does NOT bind the SC itself; and **Art 145(3)** requires **>= 5 judges**.

## 6. Review petitions and Curative petitions

While judgments ordinarily attain finality (*res judicata*), two narrow post-verdict remedies guard against irreversible error.

**Review Petition (Article 137):** Filed within **30 days** before the **same bench** (SC Rules, 2013), review is **not a disguised appeal**; grounds are limited to new unavailable evidence, an **error apparent on the face of the record**, or analogous sufficient reason. Normally decided **in chambers by circulation**, **death-penalty reviews** guarantee an **open-court hearing** before >= 3 judges (***Mohd. Arif*, 2014**).

**Curative Petition (***Rupa Ashok Hurra*, 2002**):** Created by a five-judge Bench under **Articles 137 and 142** as a final resort *after* review dismissal to prevent **abuse of process** or cure a **gross miscarriage of justice** (e.g., breach of natural justice or undisclosed bias). Grounds must have been raised in review; a **Senior Advocate's certificate** is mandatory; and it circulates to the **three seniormost SC judges plus the original bench** (if available), listed in open court only if a majority sees merit.

> **Example:** On **10 April 2024**, a three-judge Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud allowed a rare curative petition in ***DMRC v. DAMEPL***, setting aside its 2021 verdict and vacating an arbitral liability exceeding **₹8,000 crore** for "grave miscarriage of justice". Earlier, ***Navneet Kaur* (2014)** commuted a death sentence on a curative petition citing mental illness and delay.

| Dimension | Review Petition | Curative Petition |
|---|---|---|
| **Basis** | Express in **Article 137** | Judicially created under **Arts 137 & 142** (*Rupa Ashok Hurra*, 2002) |
| **Stage & limit** | Post-judgment; within **30 days** | Only **after review dismissal** |
| **Certification** | Advocate-on-Record | Mandatory **Senior Advocate certificate** |
| **Bench** | Normally **same bench** | **3 seniormost SC judges + original bench** |
| **Hearing** | Chambers (open court for **death penalty**, *Mohd. Arif*) | Chambers first; open court only if majority approves |

## 7. Reforming the apex court: Mains way forward

> **Mains angle:** GS-2 answers on the Supreme Court must pair **judicial independence (Basic Structure)** with transparent appointments, proportionate accountability and institutional focus.

1. **Transparent MoP and Collegium Secretariat:** Finalise the stalled 2016 MoP with published merit/diversity criteria, a permanent vetting secretariat, and **binding timelines** for the Centre to clear or return names—ending both Collegium opacity and executive "pocket vetoes"—while institutionalising the **April–May 2025** asset and appointment disclosures.
2. **Graded accountability:** Because Article 124(4) removal is rightly rare and permits mid-inquiry resignations (**2025–2026 Justice Yashwant Varma** row), a constitutionally sound **Judicial Standards and Accountability** law should provide calibrated oversight for lesser misconduct without executive capture.
3. **Permanent Constitution Bench and SLP discipline:** Deploy the expanded **38-judge strength (2026 Act)** to institutionalise a permanent Constitution Bench, filter **Article 136 SLPs** strictly so High Courts stay final appellate courts for ordinary disputes, and weigh the **229th Law Commission's (2009)** regional Cassation Bench proposal (developed further in the companion note on High Courts and judicial reforms).
4. **Diversity, Article 142 restraint and cooling-off:** Proactive diversity pipelines (women, SC/ST/OBC/minorities), self-restraint under **Article 142** (**November 2025**), and a **two-year cooling-off period** before post-retirement executive or political offices will safeguard public trust.
