## 1. What parliamentary government means in India

Democratic constitutions divide into **parliamentary** and **presidential** systems by how the executive relates to the legislature. In a **presidential system**, both branches are elected separately for fixed terms under strict separation of powers. In a **parliamentary system**, the executive grows directly out of the legislature and remains continuously **responsible to it** for its policies and survival—fusing executive and legislative leadership to ensure daily accountability.

India adopted the parliamentary form of government both at the Centre (**Articles 74 and 75**) and in the States (**Articles 163 and 164**). Political scientists use four classic labels for this model:

- **Cabinet government:** Sir **Ivor Jennings** termed it the "Cabinet system" because the Cabinet—not the ceremonial head of State—is the real nucleus of executive power.
- **Responsible government:** The Cabinet survives only while it holds the confidence of the popularly elected lower House.
- **Westminster model:** Named after the Palace of Westminster in London, where these conventions evolved.
- **Prime Ministerial government:** Scholars such as **R.H.S. Crossman** and **John Mackintosh** argued that modern party discipline and media visibility shifted decisive dominance from the collective Cabinet to the Prime Minister.

> **Prelims pointer:** UPSC keeps returning to the defining feature of parliamentary government: **the executive remains responsible to the legislature and can be removed by it**. India has a parliamentary system because "the Council of Ministers is responsible to the Lok Sabha" (2015); its main advantage is that "the executive remains responsible to the legislature" (2017); it is a system in which "the Government is responsible to the Parliament and can be removed by it" (2020). Having a Parliament, an elected President or periodic elections is not what defines it.

## 2. Core features of India's parliamentary system

Eight interlocking features govern India's parliamentary executive under the Constitution.

**1. Nominal and real executives.** India has a **dual executive**: the **President** is the nominal (*de jure*) head of State, while the **Prime Minister** heading the **Council of Ministers** is the real (*de facto*) head of Government. Under **Article 74(1)**, the President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers—made binding by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**, with the **44th Amendment (1978)** letting the President return advice *once* for reconsideration.

**2. Majority party (or coalition) rule.** The party—or pre-poll/post-poll coalition—commanding a majority in the **Lok Sabha** forms the government; its leader is appointed Prime Minister by the President, who appoints other ministers on the Prime Minister's advice (mirrored by the Governor in states).

**3. Collective and individual responsibility.** **Collective responsibility** is the bedrock of the system. **Article 75(3)** declares that the Council of Ministers is **collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha** (and **Article 164(2)** makes State ministries responsible to the Legislative Assembly)—strictly to the lower House, **never** to the Rajya Sabha or Parliament as a whole. Ministers "swim and sink together": losing a no-confidence motion or money bill in the Lok Sabha brings down the entire ministry, including ministers from the Rajya Sabha. Alongside this sits **individual responsibility** under **Article 75(2)**: ministers hold office during the **pleasure of the President** (exercised on the Prime Minister's advice), enabling the PM to drop dissenting or underperforming ministers.

**4. Political homogeneity.** Under single-party rule, ministers share one party platform; in coalition governments (India's norm from 1989–2014 and again from 2024), homogeneity is sustained through common minimum programmes and cabinet consensus so ministers speak with one voice.

**5. Double membership and ministerial caps.** Ministers sit in both the executive and the legislature. Under **Article 75(5)** (and **Article 164(4)** in states), a non-MP may be appointed minister, **but ceases to hold office unless elected or nominated to either House within six consecutive months**. In ***S.R. Chaudhuri v. State of Punjab* (2001)**, the Supreme Court held this six-month window is a **one-time exception** during a legislature's term—a non-legislator cannot resign and be reappointed repeatedly without winning a seat. To curb oversized coalition ministries, the **91st Amendment (2003)** added **Articles 75(1A) and 164(1A)**, capping the Council of Ministers (including PM/CM) at **15 per cent** of the Lok Sabha or Assembly strength (minimum 12 in states).

**6. Leadership of the Prime Minister.** The PM chairs Cabinet meetings, allocates portfolios, advises Lok Sabha dissolution, and links the President and Cabinet (Article 78). **Lord Morley** described the PM as *primus inter pares* ("first among equals") and the "keystone of the cabinet arch"; **Sir William Vernon Harcourt** called the office *inter stellas luna minores* ("a moon among lesser stars"); and **Ivor Jennings** termed it "a sun around which planets revolve".

**7. Dissolution of the Lower House.** The **Lok Sabha** can be dissolved before its five-year term by the President on the Prime Minister's advice, sending deadlocks back to voters; the **Rajya Sabha** is a permanent chamber not subject to dissolution.

**8. Secrecy of procedure.** Cabinet deliberations remain confidential so ministers debate candidly before taking collective responsibility; each minister takes an **oath of secrecy** (Third Schedule) before entering office.

> **Remember:** Three classic Prelims traps: (1) Collective responsibility under **Article 75(3)** runs **only to the Lok Sabha**, never to Parliament as a whole (the 2013 paper offered "collectively responsible to the Parliament" as a statement, and it was wrong); (2) A non-MP can serve as minister for **six consecutive months** only once per House term (*S.R. Chaudhuri*, 2001); (3) The Prime Minister can belong to **either** Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha.

## 3. Parliamentary versus presidential government

Setting India's parliamentary accountability loop beside the American presidential design highlights the structural trade-off between continuous oversight and fixed separation.

```text
 PARLIAMENTARY (Fusion)      PRESIDENTIAL (Separation)
 ┌────────────────────┐      ┌──────────┐   ┌───────────┐
 │     ELECTORATE     │      │ELECTORATE│──►│ PRESIDENT │
 └─────────┬──────────┘      └────┬─────┘   │(Fixed 4yr)│
           ▼                      ▼         └─────┬─────┘
 ┌────────────────────┐      ┌──────────┐         │
 │ PARLIAMENT (LS/RS) │      │ CONGRESS │   Appoints non-
 └───┬────────────▲───┘      └────▲─────┘   MP Cabinet
Draws│      Daily │               │ Checks       │
PM & │   Account- │               │ & Balances   │
CoM  ▼    ability │               └──────────────┘
 ┌────────────────────┐
 │ PM & CABINET (CoM) │      Fixed tenures; no removal
 └────────────────────┘      by legislative no-confidence
```

| Dimension | Parliamentary system (India, UK) | Presidential system (USA) |
|---|---|---|
| **Executive** | **Dual:** nominal head of State + real head of Government (PM & Cabinet) | **Single:** President is both head of State and head of Government |
| **Core principle** | **Fusion and cooperation** between legislature and executive | **Strict separation of powers** with checks and balances |
| **Accountability** | **Continuous daily oversight** by lower House (Questions, no-confidence) | **Periodic accountability** to voters; removable only by **impeachment** |
| **Tenure stability** | **Uncertain:** depends on lower-House majority | **Fixed term** (four years in USA), insulated from legislative flux |
| **Cabinet expertise** | **Double membership** from Parliament (6-month non-MP window in India) | **Single membership:** non-legislators; President freely picks specialists |
| **Responsibility** | **Collective responsibility** to lower House | Secretaries serve strictly at the **President's pleasure** |
| **Dissolution** | Executive may advise **premature dissolution** of lower House | President **cannot dissolve** Congress |
| **Representation** | Plural Cabinet accommodates **regional and social diversity** | Mandate concentrated in **one directly elected individual** |

## 4. Indian parliamentary model versus the British Westminster model

Although India adapted Britain's parliamentary architecture, the framers modified it decisively for a written, federal republic.

| Feature | Indian parliamentary system | British Westminster system |
|---|---|---|
| **Head of State** | **Republic:** indirectly elected **President** (5-year term) | **Constitutional monarchy:** hereditary **King/Queen** |
| **Supremacy** | **Constitutional supremacy:** written Constitution, federalism, judicial review & **basic structure** | **Parliamentary sovereignty:** unwritten constitution; courts cannot void primary Acts of Parliament |
| **Prime Minister's House** | **Either House**—Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha | Strictly the **House of Commons** (lower House) by convention |
| **Non-MPs as ministers** | Allowed for up to **six consecutive months** (Art. 75(5)) | Ministers drawn strictly from **members of Parliament** |
| **Legal responsibility** | **None:** no ministerial countersignature on presidential acts; **Art. 74(2)** bars judicial probe of advice | **Exists:** Crown acts require ministerial countersignature ("King can do no wrong") |
| **Shadow Cabinet** | **No institutionalised Shadow Cabinet** | Formal opposition **"Shadow Cabinet"** prepares an alternative government |

India's flexibility on the Prime Minister's House has been used repeatedly: **Indira Gandhi** (1966), **H.D. Deve Gowda** (1996), **I.K. Gujral** (1997), and **Dr. Manmohan Singh** (2004 and 2009) all served as Prime Minister from the **Rajya Sabha**.

> **Prelims pointer:** The 2021 paper asked how India's model differs from Britain's: the British Parliament is sovereign in legislation, while the Indian Parliament's power to legislate is limited by the Constitution. India is a **parliamentary republic**, not a sovereign-parliament democracy: **no** parliamentary sovereignty, **no** legal responsibility of ministers, **no** Shadow Cabinet, and a **Rajya Sabha member or non-MP (for 6 months)** may serve as minister.

## 5. Why the Constituent Assembly chose the parliamentary system

The framers weighed the American and Swiss systems carefully and chose parliamentary government on four grounds:

1. **Responsibility over stability:** Introducing the Draft Constitution on **4 November 1948**, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar noted that no system maximises both stability and responsibility equally—America and Switzerland favoured stability, Britain favoured responsibility—and explained why India prioritised *daily* oversight:

> "The daily assessment of responsibility which is not available under the American system is, it is felt, far more effective than the periodic assessment and far more necessary in a country like India. The Draft Constitution in recommending the Parliamentary system of Executive has preferred more responsibility to more stability." — B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 4 November 1948

2. **Familiarity with colonial-era institutions:** Indians had operated cabinet-style representative legislatures under the **Government of India Acts of 1919 and 1935** (and the 1937–39 provincial ministries). As **K.M. Munshi** argued, abandoning decades of constitutional familiarity for an untested presidential model risked institutional breakdown.
3. **Avoiding executive–legislature deadlock:** A newly independent republic tackling Partition, integration and development could ill afford American-style gridlock between an executive and a hostile legislature.
4. **Representing India's pluralism:** A plural **Council of Ministers** gives diverse regions, languages, castes and minorities a visible stake in national power—avoiding the winner-take-all majoritarianism of a single directly elected President.

## 6. Merits, demerits and the presidential alternative debate

### Merits versus demerits

**Merits** include executive–legislature harmony; **continuous daily accountability** (Question Hour, Zero Hour, adjournment and no-confidence motions, committees); **prevention of personal despotism** by dispersing power across a Cabinet; a **ready alternative government** via the Opposition without immediate elections (as in 1979, 1990 and 1996); and **broad plural representation**.

**Demerits** are equally real: **unstable coalition governments** (six Prime Ministers between 1989 and 1999) breeding **policy discontinuity**; **dictatorship of the Cabinet** when a single party commands a brute majority (vividly seen during the 1975–77 Emergency); dilution of **separation of powers**; **"government by amateurs"** because ministers come primarily from elected politicians rather than domain specialists; and party-whip rigidity under the **Tenth Schedule** muting independent backbencher scrutiny.

### The debate on switching to a presidential system

Critics of parliamentary gridlock and coalition fragility periodically advocate switching to a presidential executive for fixed tenure, technocratic cabinet appointments and decisive leadership. Yet three decisive barriers have consistently rejected that switch:

- **Swaran Singh Committee (1976):** Appointed to review constitutional changes, it explicitly examined the presidential alternative and **rejected replacing the parliamentary system**, concluding it best preserved national unity and responsiveness.
- **NCRWC (Venkatachaliah Commission, 2002):** Working within parliamentary democracy and basic structure, it **rejected switching to a presidential model**, recommending instead Germany's **constructive vote of no-confidence** (a no-confidence motion backed by at least 20 per cent of members must simultaneously propose an alternative leader) and letting a hung House elect the Leader of the House directly.
- **Basic Structure barrier:** The Supreme Court has recognised **parliamentary democracy** as part of the **basic structure** (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973; *S.R. Bommai*, 1994; *Kuldip Nayar*, 2006)—so even an Article 368 amendment cannot replace it with a presidential system.

Politically too, a single directly elected President risks personalised authoritarianism where institutional counterweights are weaker than in the US, and risks alienating smaller states if populous regions dominate the presidential vote.

> **Example:** **Article 67 of Germany's Basic Law** pioneered the **constructive vote of no-confidence**, endorsed by the **NCRWC (2002)**. Because an opposition can topple a government only by simultaneously electing a successor, it prevents purely negative alliances like those that felled Indian governments in 1979, 1990 and 1999.

## 7. Contemporary strains and reforming parliamentary democracy

India's contemporary challenge is not the parliamentary model itself, but the steady weakening of legislative checks inside it.

**Presidentialisation and centralisation.** Confirming Crossman's "Prime Ministerial government" thesis, national elections have become increasingly leader-centric, with policy authority concentrating in the **Prime Minister's Office (PMO)**—at times eclipsing Cabinet collegiality.

**Declining sittings and scrutiny.** **PRS Legislative Research** data shows annual Lok Sabha sittings dropped from an average of **135 days in the 1st Lok Sabha (1952–57)** (and 121 days across 1952–70) to **55 days per year (274 total sittings) in the 17th Lok Sabha (2019–24)**—the lowest of any full-term Lok Sabha. Bills referred to **Parliamentary Standing Committees** fell from **60 per cent (14th LS)** and **71 per cent (15th LS)** to **28 per cent (16th LS)** and **16 per cent (17th LS)**, recovering only modestly to **21 per cent in the 18th Lok Sabha (up to August 2026)**. Frequent **ordinances** (Article 123), contested **Money Bill** certifications, and leaving the constitutional office of **Deputy Speaker** (Article 93) vacant since 2019 further tilt the scales toward the executive.

**Disruptions and live constitutional debates.** In the **Monsoon Session 2026 (July–August 2026)**, disruptions shrank Question Hour to just **1 per cent** of scheduled time in the Lok Sabha and **12 per cent** in the Rajya Sabha, with nine of eleven Lok Sabha bills passed without any MP other than the Minister speaking. Meanwhile, the pending **129th Amendment Bill, 2024** (*One Nation, One Election*, before a JPC into Winter Session 2026) tests whether synchronised "unexpired terms" narrow the parliamentary tradition of fresh mid-term mandates—even as the Lok Sabha's **17 April 2026 defeat of the 131st Amendment Bill** (298–230, short of two-thirds) showed a coalition-era House still capable of checking executive supermajority proposals.

**Way forward: repairing the parliamentary model from within.** Rather than abandoning parliamentary democracy, India needs five institutional reforms:

- **Fixed parliamentary calendar:** Mandate a minimum **100–120 sitting days annually** (NCRWC recommendation) to curb executive truncation of sessions.
- **Mandatory committee referral:** Send all major non-urgent bills to Standing Committees automatically, matching UK practice.
- **Narrowing the Tenth Schedule:** Limit anti-defection disqualification to **confidence motions, no-confidence motions, Money Bills and core financial votes** (Dinesh Goswami Committee & Law Commission), freeing MPs to debate ordinary laws on merit.
- **Strengthening Opposition institutions:** Establish dedicated **Opposition Days**, an informal Shadow Cabinet, and prompt election of an Opposition Deputy Speaker.
- **Combining stability with expertise:** Adopt the **constructive vote of no-confidence** to stabilise hung legislatures and expand structured **lateral entry** in administration without eroding democratic accountability.

> **In the news:** PRS Legislative Research figures up to **August 2026** highlight persistent legislative strain in the 18th Lok Sabha—only **21 per cent** of bills referred to committees, Question Hour collapsing to **1 per cent** in the Lok Sabha during Monsoon Session 2026, and the Deputy Speaker post vacant since 2019—while JPC scrutiny of the **129th Amendment Bill, 2024** (*One Nation, One Election*) keeps the balance between electoral stability and parliamentary accountability live into Winter Session 2026.

> **Mains angle:** Structure GS-2 answers across four pillars: (1) **Ambedkar's rationale** (daily responsibility over stability, 4 Nov 1948); (2) **Why a presidential switch fails** (Swaran Singh 1976, NCRWC 2002, Basic Structure barrier, India's pluralism); (3) **Empirical strains** (PRS sittings/committee trends up to 2026, Tenth Schedule whip, PMO centralisation); and (4) **Reforms inside the system** (fixed calendar, mandatory committee scrutiny, anti-defection reform, constructive no-confidence).
