## 1. The right to freedom

Articles 19 to 22 together make up the right to freedom, the heart of the liberties the Constitution guarantees. Article 19 protects six freedoms of citizens: of speech, assembly, association, movement, residence and profession. Article 20 protects anyone accused of a crime. Article 21 protects life and personal liberty, and has grown into the most expansive right in the Constitution. Article 21A guarantees elementary education. Article 22 sets limits on arrest and detention.

The theme running through all of them is balance. The framers wrote each freedom with its limits beside it, because a newly independent and divided country could not afford absolute liberties. The courts' great work since 1950 has been to decide where those limits lie. This note follows that work.

## 2. Article 19: the six freedoms

Article 19 originally protected seven freedoms. The right to acquire, hold and dispose of property, in Article 19(1)(f), was deleted by the 44th Amendment in 1978. The six that remain are available **only to citizens**: not to foreigners, and not to legal persons such as companies, although shareholders can claim them. And they protect against **State action** only, not against private individuals, except where the courts have said otherwise.

Each freedom can be restricted, but only on the grounds stated in the clause that goes with it, and the restriction must be reasonable.

| Freedom | Clause | Grounds for reasonable restrictions |
|---|---|---|
| Speech and expression | 19(1)(a) | 19(2): sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence |
| Assemble peaceably and without arms | 19(1)(b) | 19(3): sovereignty and integrity of India, public order |
| Form associations, unions or co-operative societies | 19(1)(c) | 19(4): sovereignty and integrity of India, public order, morality |
| Move freely throughout India | 19(1)(d) | 19(5): interests of the general public, protection of any Scheduled Tribe |
| Reside and settle in any part of India | 19(1)(e) | 19(5): interests of the general public, protection of any Scheduled Tribe |
| Practise any profession, or carry on any occupation, trade or business | 19(1)(g) | 19(6): interests of the general public, professional or technical qualifications, the State carrying on any trade to the exclusion of citizens |

Two of these grounds were added later. "Friendly relations with foreign states", "public order" and "incitement to an offence" came with the 1st Amendment (1951), after the Supreme Court's early free-speech judgments. "Sovereignty and integrity of India" came with the 16th Amendment (1963), after the India–China war, to curb secessionist speech. The words "or co-operative societies" in 19(1)(c) were added by the 97th Amendment (2011).

> **In the news:** In ***Kaushal Kishor v. State of Uttar Pradesh* (January 2023)**, a Constitution Bench held that the grounds in Article 19(2) are **exhaustive**: the State cannot add new restrictions on speech beyond them. It also held that the rights under Articles 19 and 21 can be enforced even against **private individuals**, not only the State, which marks a shift towards their "horizontal" application.

### Freedom of speech and expression

Article 19(1)(a) has been read widely. The Supreme Court has held that it includes:

- **freedom of the press**, though the press is not mentioned separately (*Romesh Thappar*, 1950);
- the **right to know**, and so the right to information about how the government works (*S.P. Gupta*, 1981), which the RTI Act, 2005, later gave statutory form;
- **commercial speech**, such as advertising (*Tata Press*, 1995);
- the **right to silence**: the children in *Bijoe Emmanuel* (1986), who stood respectfully but did not sing the national anthem, could not be expelled;
- the **right to fly the national flag** (*Naveen Jindal*, 2004);
- **voters' right to know** the criminal records, assets and education of candidates (*ADR*, 2002; *PUCL*, 2003), and the right to reject all candidates through **NOTA** (*PUCL*, 2013);
- expression through the **internet**. In *Anuradha Bhasin* (2020), arising from the shutdown in Jammu and Kashmir, the Court held that freedom of speech and the freedom to trade over the internet are protected, and that internet suspensions must be necessary, proportionate and open to review.

The courts have also struck down overbroad restrictions. In ***Shreya Singhal* (2015)** the Court struck down **Section 66A of the IT Act, 2000**, which punished "grossly offensive" or "menacing" online messages, as vague and as having a chilling effect on speech.

> **In the news:** On 15 February 2024, in ***Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India***, a Constitution Bench struck down the **electoral bonds scheme**. It held that anonymous political funding violated voters' right to information under Article 19(1)(a), and it directed that the donors' details be disclosed.

The law of **sedition** shows the same tension. In *Kedar Nath Singh* (1962) the Court upheld Section 124A of the Indian Penal Code, but confined it to speech that incites violence or public disorder. In May 2022, in *S.G. Vombatkere*, it put the section in abeyance. The **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023**, in force from 1 July 2024, drops the word "sedition". Its Section 152 instead penalises acts that endanger the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India.

### The other freedoms

- **Assembly.** Citizens may hold meetings and processions, but peaceably and without arms. The freedom does not include a right to strike: the Court held in *T.K. Rangarajan* (2003) that government employees have no fundamental, legal, moral or equitable right to strike. Orders banning assemblies, under Section 144 of the old Code of Criminal Procedure (now Section 163 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita), must be justified by the grounds in 19(3).
- **Association.** Citizens may form political parties, companies, clubs, trade unions and co-operatives. But there is no fundamental right to have a union recognised, or to strike.
- **Movement and residence.** These freedoms knit the country together by letting citizens live and move anywhere in it. They can be restricted to protect Scheduled Tribes, whose culture and land need safeguarding; the Inner Line Permit regimes of the North-East rest on this ground.
- **Profession.** Every citizen may follow any lawful occupation. There is no right to carry on a dangerous or immoral trade, such as trafficking. The State may prescribe qualifications for professions and may run trades itself, to the exclusion of private citizens.

## 3. Article 20: protection in respect of conviction

Article 20 protects anyone accused of a crime: citizens, foreigners and legal persons such as companies. It makes three guarantees.

1. **No ex post facto law.** No one can be convicted of an act that was not an offence when it was done, or given a heavier penalty than the law then provided. This applies only to criminal laws, not to civil or tax laws, and not to preventive detention.
2. **No double jeopardy.** No one can be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once. This protects only against proceedings before a court or judicial tribunal, not against departmental or administrative proceedings.
3. **No self-incrimination.** No one accused of an offence can be compelled to be a witness against themselves. This covers oral and documentary evidence, but not the compulsory production of material objects, fingerprints, specimen handwriting or blood samples. In ***Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010)** the Court held that **narco-analysis, polygraph and brain-mapping tests** cannot be done without consent, as they violate Articles 20(3) and 21.

> **Prelims pointer:** Article 20 cannot be suspended even during a national emergency. Its protection against retrospective punishment applies only to criminal laws, and its bar on double jeopardy only to proceedings before courts.

## 4. Article 21: life and personal liberty

Article 21 is a single sentence: "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law." It applies to citizens and non-citizens alike. Its history is the history of judicial interpretation in India.

### From Gopalan to Maneka Gandhi

In ***A.K. Gopalan* (1950)** the Supreme Court read Article 21 narrowly. The phrase "procedure established by law", which the framers took from the Japanese Constitution, meant only that some procedure enacted by the legislature had to be followed. Article 21 protected against arbitrary executive action, but not against arbitrary laws. The Court deliberately contrasted it with the American "due process of law", which lets courts judge whether a law itself is fair.

That view was reversed in ***Maneka Gandhi* (1978)**. The government had impounded Maneka Gandhi's passport without giving reasons. The Court held that the procedure under Article 21 must be "**just, fair and reasonable**", not "arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive". It also held that Articles 14, 19 and 21 are not separate compartments but a connected whole, a "golden triangle" that a law must satisfy together. In effect, the Court brought the American idea of due process into Article 21.

```text
     A.K. Gopalan (1950)          Maneka Gandhi (1978)
 any procedure made by law  →  procedure must be just,
 protects against the         fair and reasonable;
 executive only               Articles 14, 19 and 21
                              read together
```

### What "life" has come to mean

Since *Maneka Gandhi*, the Court has read into the word "life" a long list of rights, on the reasoning that life means more than mere animal existence: it means living with **human dignity**.

| Right | Case |
|---|---|
| To live with human dignity | *Francis Coralie Mullin* (1981) |
| To livelihood | *Olga Tellis* (1985), the Bombay pavement dwellers |
| To a speedy trial | *Hussainara Khatoon* (1979) |
| To free legal aid | *M.H. Hoskot* (1978) |
| To a healthy environment, including clean air and water | *Subhash Kumar* (1991); the *M.C. Mehta* cases |
| To health and emergency medical care | *Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity* (1996) |
| To privacy | *K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017) |
| To die with dignity: passive euthanasia and living wills | *Common Cause* (2018) |
| To marry a person of one's choice | *Shafin Jahan* (2018) |
| To gender identity, for transgender persons | *NALSA* (2014) |
| To be free from the adverse effects of climate change | *M.K. Ranjitsinh* (2024) |

Two of these deserve a closer look.

**Privacy.** On 24 August 2017 a nine-judge bench in ***Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India*** held unanimously that the right to privacy is a fundamental right, protected as part of life and personal liberty under Article 21 and the freedoms in Part III. It overruled the contrary views in *M.P. Sharma* (1954) and *Kharak Singh* (1962). It also said that the majority decision in ***ADM Jabalpur* (1976)**, which had held that no one could approach a court to enforce Article 21 during the Emergency, was seriously flawed and stood overruled. Privacy is not absolute: a restriction must pass the test of legality, legitimate aim and proportionality. The judgment led to the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023.

> **In the news:** The **Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025**, were notified in November 2025, bringing the DPDP Act, 2023, into operation. Most of the duties of data fiduciaries are being phased in over eighteen months.

**Climate change.** In ***M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India* (2024)**, a case about protecting the critically endangered Great Indian Bustard from power lines, the Supreme Court recognised a **right to be free from the adverse effects of climate change**, rooted in Articles 14 and 21.

> **Remember:** Article 21 cannot be suspended even during a national emergency. The 44th Amendment (1978) added this safeguard after the experience of *ADM Jabalpur*, when, in Justice H.R. Khanna's famous dissent, the lone voice on the bench insisted that the right to life did not depend on the Constitution's permission.

## 5. Article 21A: the right to education

The **86th Amendment Act, 2002**, inserted Article 21A, which makes free and compulsory education for **all children aged 6 to 14** a Fundamental Right, in the manner the State determines by law. The Court had already read a right to education into Article 21 in *Mohini Jain* (1992) and *Unni Krishnan* (1993). The same amendment changed Article 45, which now directs the State to provide early childhood care and education for children below six, and added a Fundamental Duty in Article 51A(k), requiring parents and guardians to provide opportunities for education to their children aged 6 to 14.

Parliament gave effect to Article 21A through the **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009**. The Act requires private unaided schools to reserve 25% of seats at the entry level for children from weaker sections and disadvantaged groups. The Supreme Court upheld this in 2012, but in *Pramati Educational Trust* (2014) it held that the RTE Act does not apply to minority schools, aided or unaided.

## 6. Article 22: arrest and detention

Article 22 distinguishes two kinds of detention. **Punitive detention** punishes a person for an offence they have committed, after trial in a court. **Preventive detention** holds a person without trial, to prevent them from committing an offence in the future, on mere suspicion.

### Under ordinary law

A person arrested under ordinary law has four rights:

1. to be informed of the grounds of arrest;
2. to consult, and be defended by, a lawyer of their choice;
3. to be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours, excluding the journey;
4. to be released after 24 hours unless the magistrate authorises further detention.

These rights are not available to an **enemy alien**, or to a person arrested under a **preventive detention law**.

### Under preventive detention

Article 22 also lays down safeguards for those held under a preventive detention law:

- Detention cannot exceed **three months** unless an **Advisory Board**, made up of persons qualified to be High Court judges, reports that there is sufficient cause. (The 44th Amendment reduced this period to two months, but that provision has not yet been brought into force.)
- The detainee must be told the **grounds of detention**, except facts the authority considers against the public interest to disclose.
- The detainee must be given the earliest opportunity to **make a representation** against the order.
- Parliament may prescribe the circumstances and classes of cases in which a person can be held beyond three months without an Advisory Board's opinion, the maximum period of detention, and the Board's procedure.

Parliament has exclusive power to make preventive detention laws for reasons connected with defence, foreign affairs and the security of India. Parliament and the state legislatures share the power for reasons connected with the security of a state, public order, and the maintenance of essential supplies and services. Laws made under these powers include the Preventive Detention Act, 1950 (which lapsed in 1969), the Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA), 1971 (repealed in 1978), the Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act (COFEPOSA), 1974, and the National Security Act (NSA), 1980.

> **Prelims pointer:** India is among the few democracies whose constitution expressly provides for preventive detention, even in peacetime. Of the four rights of an arrested person, none applies to a person held under a preventive detention law; that person has only the separate safeguards of Article 22(4) to (7).

> **Mains angle:** The right to freedom raises the questions examiners return to most often. **Speech:** how to balance free expression against hate speech and public order, the use of sedition and the new Section 152 of the BNS, and internet shutdowns, citing *Shreya Singhal* and *Anuradha Bhasin*. **Privacy:** data protection after *Puttaswamy* and the DPDP Act. **Article 21:** its expansion as an example of judicial creativity, praised for securing dignity and criticised as overreach. **Preventive detention:** its misuse and the case for stronger safeguards. A balanced answer acknowledges both the needs of security and the Constitution's commitment to liberty, and concludes with the proportionality test that the courts now use to reconcile them.

- **Article 19:** six freedoms for citizens only (property, 19(1)(f), was deleted in 1978). Each is open to reasonable restrictions on the grounds in its clause.
- **19(2) grounds:** sovereignty and integrity (16th Amendment, 1963); security of the State; friendly relations, public order and incitement (1st Amendment, 1951); decency or morality; contempt of court; defamation.
- *Kaushal Kishor* (2023): the 19(2) grounds are exhaustive; Articles 19 and 21 apply against private persons too.
- **Speech includes:** the press (*Romesh Thappar*, 1950); the right to know (*S.P. Gupta*, 1981); commercial speech (*Tata Press*); silence (*Bijoe Emmanuel*, 1986); the national flag (*Naveen Jindal*, 2004); voters' information (*ADR*, 2002; *PUCL*, 2003); NOTA (2013); the internet (*Anuradha Bhasin*, 2020).
- **Struck down:** Section 66A of the IT Act (*Shreya Singhal*, 2015); electoral bonds (*ADR*, 15 February 2024).
- **Sedition:** *Kedar Nath* (1962); in abeyance from May 2022; replaced by BNS Section 152 from 1 July 2024.
- **No right to strike** (*T.K. Rangarajan*, 2003). The ground of protecting Scheduled Tribes underlies the Inner Line Permit regimes.
- **Article 20:** for all persons. No ex post facto criminal law; no double jeopardy (courts only); no self-incrimination (narco tests need consent, *Selvi*, 2010). Never suspended.
- **Article 21:** *Gopalan* (1950), "procedure established by law" (from Japan). *Maneka Gandhi* (1978), "just, fair and reasonable", the golden triangle of Articles 14, 19 and 21.
- **Rights read into Article 21:** dignity, livelihood (*Olga Tellis*), speedy trial (*Hussainara*), legal aid (*Hoskot*), environment, health, privacy (*Puttaswamy*, 24 August 2017, nine judges), passive euthanasia (*Common Cause*, 2018), choice of partner (*Shafin Jahan*), gender identity (*NALSA*, 2014), freedom from climate change effects (*M.K. Ranjitsinh*, 2024).
- *ADM Jabalpur* (1976) was overruled in *Puttaswamy*; Justice H.R. Khanna dissented. Article 21 is never suspended (44th Amendment).
- **DPDP Act 2023;** rules notified November 2025 and phased over 18 months.
- **Article 21A:** ages 6 to 14 (86th Amendment, 2002); Article 45 now covers under-6s; Article 51A(k) is the parents' duty. RTE Act 2009: 25% of seats in private unaided schools. Minority schools are exempt (*Pramati*, 2014).

- **Preventive detention:** beyond 3 months needs an Advisory Board (the 44th Amendment's two months is not in force); grounds, representation. Laws: PDA 1950, MISA 1971, COFEPOSA 1974, NSA 1980.
