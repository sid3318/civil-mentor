## 1. Rationale and Constitutional Architecture of Part XVIII (Articles 352–360)

Every constitutional democracy confronts a foundational paradox: how to remain faithful to federalism, separated powers, and individual liberties during ordinary times while still equipping the state with sufficient centralised authority to survive existential shocks—foreign invasion, armed insurrection, provincial constitutional collapse, or sovereign financial panic. When the Constituent Assembly of India drafted the Constitution between December 1946 and November 1949, this dilemma was not an abstract seminar debate. The framers were working amid the bloodbath of Partition, the mass displacement of millions, the delicate integration of more than 550 princely states, armed hostilities in Jammu and Kashmir, the Telangana peasant insurrection, and fragile post-war fiscal balances. Against this volatile backdrop, they embedded an elaborate constitutional mechanism for crisis governance in **Part XVIII (`Articles 352 to 360`)**, titled **Emergency Provisions**.

### Constitutional Purpose: Unitary Conversion Without Formal Amendment

The explicit constitutional rationale of `Part XVIII` is to safeguard the **sovereignty, unity, integrity, and security of India**, preserve its **democratic political system**, and protect the **Constitution itself** against extraordinary threats that ordinary administrative processes cannot contain. What distinguishes India's constitutional architecture from classical federations such as the United States, Switzerland, or Australia is a deliberate structural design that Dr. B.R. Ambedkar described as **constitutional elasticity**.

In normal times, the Indian Constitution operates through a dual federal polity governed by the Seventh Schedule (`Part XI`), in which States possess autonomous constitutional authority over matters such as police, public order, agriculture, and state finance (examined in detail in **Chapters 9 and 10**). During an Emergency, however, the Central Government becomes all-powerful and the States pass under the total executive and legislative control of the Union. Crucially, **this conversion of the federal structure into a unitary system occurs automatically by operation of Part XVIII without requiring any formal constitutional amendment under `Article 368`**. Once the emergency ceases, the polity snaps back seamlessly to its normal federal equilibrium.

> "All federal systems including the American are placed in a tight mould of federalism. No matter what the circumstances, it cannot change its form and shape. It can never be unitary. On the other hand the Draft Constitution can be both unitary as well as federal according to the requirements of time and circumstances. In normal times, it is framed to work as a federal system. But in times of war it is so designed as to make it work as though it was a unitary system." — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, Vol. VII, 4 November 1948

Ambedkar pointed out that classical federations—locked inside rigid written compartments—had been compelled during the First and Second World Wars and the Great Depression to rely on tortured judicial doctrines (such as the American "war powers" and "implied powers" doctrines) to centralise authority temporarily. India's framers chose transparency over legal fiction: they wrote explicit, textually bounded emergency clauses directly into the Constitution, specifying exactly when centralisation begins, how far it reaches, how Parliament supervises it, and when normal federal autonomy resumes.

### Comparative Origins: Government of India Act, 1935 vs Weimar Constitution

One of the most frequently tested distinctions in UPSC Prelims concerns the exact constitutional pedigree of `Part XVIII`. Aspirants routinely conflate the two distinct foreign and colonial sources that shaped India's emergency clauses.

#### 1. The Administrative and Federal Framework — Government of India Act, 1935

The institutional mechanics of centralising federal power during crises derive directly from the **Government of India Act, 1935**:

- **`Section 102` of the GoI Act, 1935** empowered the Governor-General to issue a **"Proclamation of Emergency"** if the security of India was threatened by war or internal disturbance, enabling the Federal Legislature to legislate on subjects in the Provincial Legislative List. This provision became the direct structural ancestor of **`Article 352`** (National Emergency) and **`Article 250`**.
- **`Section 93` of the GoI Act, 1935** authorised the Governor of a province, if satisfied that a situation had arisen in which the government of the province could not be carried on in accordance with the Act, to assume all or any executive and legislative powers of the province (except those of the High Court). Notably, `Section 93` had been bitterly denounced by Indian nationalists during the freedom struggle as an instrument of imperial autocracy; yet after Partition, the framers adapted its core machinery into **`Article 356`**, shifting the power from an unaccountable colonial Governor to the Union President acting on responsible Cabinet advice and subject to Parliamentary approval.
- **`Section 45` of the GoI Act, 1935** had likewise contemplated a breakdown of constitutional machinery at the Federal Centre itself—a feature the Constituent Assembly **deliberately omitted** from the 1950 Constitution, because there can be no "President's Rule" at the Union level.

#### 2. Suspension of Fundamental Rights — Weimar Constitution of Germany (1919)

Because the Government of India Act, 1935 contained **no Bill of Rights**, it could not supply a model for suspending civil liberties during emergencies. For that dimension—embodied in **`Articles 358` and `359`**—the framers turned to **`Article 48` of the Weimar Constitution of Germany (1919)**, which empowered the Reich President to suspend enumerated fundamental rights temporarily during grave threats to public security and order.

The Weimar precedent was, however, a cautionary tale as much as a template: Adolf Hitler exploited `Article 48` (notably through the Reichstag Fire Decree of 1933) to dismantle German democracy lawfully from within. Several members of India's Constituent Assembly invoked the tragic fate of the Weimar Republic to warn against unchecked executive suspension of liberties—warnings that proved eerily prescient during the 1975–77 Internal Emergency and eventually led India's Parliament to enact the monumental safeguards of the `44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`.

#### 3. Financial Emergency — US National Recovery Experience and Colonial Financial Safeguards

The inspiration for **`Article 360` (Financial Emergency)** came neither from the 1935 Act's standard emergency sections nor from Weimar Germany alone, though Dr. Ambedkar noted its affinity with the **National Recovery Act of 1933** in the United States during the Great Depression—when the US Supreme Court initially struck down federal economic interventions on federalism grounds—as well as the 1935 Act's special responsibility of the Governor-General for safeguarding the financial stability and credit of India (`Section 12(1)(b)`). To prevent judicial invalidation of emergency fiscal measures during a sovereign credit crisis, the Drafting Committee introduced Draft `Article 280A` (now `Article 360`) late in the Assembly's deliberations in October 1949.

### Architectural Overview of Part XVIII: Three Emergencies and Two Support Pillars

`Part XVIII` comprises nine Articles (`Articles 352` to `360`), supplemented outside the Part by **`Article 365`** (located in **Part XIX — Miscellaneous**, a classic Prelims trap!) and **`Articles 250`–`251`** (located in **Part XI — Centre–State Legislative Relations**). Together, these provisions construct **three distinct emergency regimes** plus **two supporting constitutional pillars**:

| Article | Constitutional Title / Subject | Core Function in the Emergency Architecture |
|---|---|---|
| **`Article 352`** | Proclamation of Emergency *(National Emergency)* | Authorises the President to declare a grave emergency threatening security of India or any part due to **war, external aggression, or armed rebellion**; sets out Cabinet written advice, approval, duration, and revocation. |
| **`Article 353`** | Effect of Proclamation of Emergency | Extends Union executive directions to States on *any* matter and extends Parliament's law-making power to State List subjects (including spillover to non-emergency States via `42nd Amend`). |
| **`Article 354`** | Application of provisions relating to distribution of revenues | Empowers the President to modify financial distribution (`Arts. 268–279`) while a National Emergency is in operation; orders expire at the end of that financial year. |
| **`Article 355`** | Duty of the Union to protect States | **Support Pillar 1:** Imposes triple duty on the Union—protect every State against (1) external aggression, (2) **internal disturbance**, and (3) ensure State governance complies with the Constitution. |
| **`Article 356`** | Provisions in case of failure of constitutional machinery in States *(President's Rule)* | Authorises Presidential assumption of State executive functions and transfer of State legislative powers to Parliament upon breakdown of constitutional machinery (Governor's report or **"otherwise"**). |
| **`Article 357`** | Exercise of legislative powers under Proclamation under `Art. 356` | Allows Parliament to delegate State law-making to the President (**President's Acts**); clarifies that laws enacted during President's Rule **continue in force even after revocation** until altered/repealed. |

| **`Article 360`** | Provisions as to Financial Emergency | Authorises proclamation if financial stability or credit of India or any part is threatened; indefinite duration once approved; allows salary cuts **including SC & HC judges**. |

### Exact Constitutional Nomenclature and Structural Traps

Precision in constitutional terminology separates high-scoring Prelims candidates from careless readers. Notice three subtle textual facts embedded in `Part XVIII`:

1. **The Constitution NEVER uses the word "Emergency" for `Article 356`:** Popular textbooks and newspapers routinely call `Article 356` "State Emergency" or "Constitutional Emergency", alongside "President's Rule". Yet the bare text of the Constitution uses **"Proclamation of Emergency"** exclusively for **`Article 352`** (National Emergency) and **"Financial Emergency"** for **`Article 360`**. For **`Article 356`**, the marginal heading and text carefully refer only to **"failure of constitutional machinery in States"**.
2. **`Article 355` STILL retains the phrase "internal disturbance":** While the `44th Amendment Act, 1978` deleted "internal disturbance" from **`Article 352`** and substituted "armed rebellion", Parliament deliberately left **"internal disturbance"** untouched inside **`Article 355`**. Consequently, the Union still has a constitutional duty to protect States against severe internal disturbances—a duty discharged through deployment of Central Armed Police Forces (CAPFs) under `List I, Entry 2A` or, in extreme constitutional breakdown, via `Article 356`—even though "internal disturbance" can no longer trigger a National Emergency under `Article 352`.
3. **Emergency provisions do NOT suspend the entire Constitution or touch the Supreme Court/High Courts:** Even during the gravest National Emergency, Parliament continues to function, State Legislatures are **not** suspended under `Article 352`, the Basic Structure remains inviolable, and under `Article 356` the proviso expressly bars the President from assuming or suspending any power vested in a **High Court**.

> [!prelims]
> **Four High-Yield Prelims Traps on Part XVIII Architecture:**
> 1. **Borrowing Split:** Administrative/emergency structure = **Government of India Act, 1935** (`Sections 102 & 93`); suspension of Fundamental Rights during Emergency = **Weimar Constitution of Germany**. Never invert the two!
> 2. **Nomenclature Trap:** The Constitution **never uses the word "Emergency"** for `Article 356` (it is titled *"Provisions in case of failure of constitutional machinery in States"*).
> 3. **Location of `Article 365`:** Although functionally paired with `Articles 355` and `356`, `Article 365` sits in **Part XIX (Miscellaneous)**—*not* in Part XVIII!
> 4. **"Internal Disturbance" Survival:** Deleted from `Article 352` by the `44th Amendment (1978)`, **still present** in `Article 355`!

## 2. National Emergency (Article 352)

`Article 352` is the most potent emergency instrument in the Indian Constitution. By issuing a **Proclamation of Emergency**, the President triggers a comprehensive constitutional transformation spanning Centre–State executive, legislative, and financial relations, parliamentary and legislative tenures, and the enforceability of Fundamental Rights. Because its abuse during 1975–77 exposed the fragility of unchecked emergency authority, `Article 352` today represents a carefully engineered balance between national-security responsiveness and the ironclad democratic safeguards added by the **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`**.

### Grounds of Proclamation: War, External Aggression, and Armed Rebellion

Under **`Article 352(1)`**, the President may issue a Proclamation of Emergency if satisfied that a **grave emergency exists whereby the security of India or of any part of the territory thereof is threatened** by any of three specific grounds:

1. **War** — formal armed conflict between India and a foreign state.
2. **External Aggression** — hostile foreign armed incursion or attack even without a formal declaration of war (as occurred during the 1962 Chinese invasion and the 1971 hostilities with Pakistan).
3. **Armed Rebellion** — organised, violent internal insurrection involving weapons that threatens the security of the nation or any part of it.

#### External Emergency vs Internal Emergency

Constitutional jurisprudence classifies a `Article 352` proclamation into two distinct legal categories whose consequences—especially for `Article 19` under `Article 358`—differ sharply:

- **External Emergency:** Declared on the ground of **war** or **external aggression**.
- **Internal Emergency:** Declared on the ground of **armed rebellion**.

#### Replacement of "Internal Disturbance" with "Armed Rebellion" (`44th Amendment, 1978`)

In the original 1950 Constitution, the third ground in `Article 352(1)` was **"internal disturbance"**. That phrase proved fatally elastic: on **25 June 1975**, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi invoked "internal disturbance" to declare an Internal Emergency in response to political opposition protests, Jayaprakash Narayan's *Total Revolution* movement, and the Allahabad High Court's judgment invalidating her 1971 Lok Sabha election—despite the absence of any armed uprising or collapse of state security.

To close this loophole permanently, the **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`** deleted the vague and sweeping phrase **"internal disturbance"** from `Article 352(1)` and substituted the far narrower, objective threshold of **"armed rebellion"**. Ordinary political agitations, strikes, communal riots, or state-level law-and-order failures can **never** constitutionally justify a National Emergency under `Article 352`; there must be an actual or imminently threatened violent revolt using arms against the constitutional order.

#### Imminent Danger and Multiple Simultaneous Proclamations

Two further structural rules govern the threshold for proclaiming a National Emergency:

- **Preventive / Imminent Danger (`Explanation` to `Article 352(1)`):** A Proclamation of Emergency may be issued **even before the actual occurrence** of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion, provided the President is satisfied that there is **imminent danger** thereof. Waiting for an enemy army to cross the border or an armed revolt to seize provincial capitals would defeat the very purpose of preventive national defence.
- **Simultaneous Proclamations (`Article 352(9)`):** Inserted originally by the **`38th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975`** (and retained as `clause (9)` by the `44th Amendment`), this provision empowers the President to issue **different Proclamations on different grounds**—war, external aggression, armed rebellion, or imminent danger thereof—**whether or not a Proclamation already issued under `Article 352(1)` is in operation**. Historically, this amendment was enacted retrospectively to validate the simultaneous existence of the **December 1971 External Emergency** and the **June 1975 Internal Emergency**, both of which operated concurrently until March 1977.

### Written Recommendation of the Union Cabinet and Territorial Scope

#### Sole Constitutional Mention of "Cabinet" (`Article 352(3)`)

On the night of **25 June 1975**, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi wrote directly to President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed advising him to proclaim an Internal Emergency under `Article 352` **without convening or consulting the Union Cabinet**. Relying on Rule 12 of the Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules—which permitted the Prime Minister to take urgent decisions and report them post facto—she informed her Cabinet colleagues only at an emergency meeting at **6:00 AM on 26 June 1975**, *after* the President had already signed the proclamation and opposition leaders had been arrested overnight.

To prevent any future Prime Minister from bypassing collective Cabinet responsibility on a matter of existential constitutional gravity, the **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`** inserted **`Article 352(3)`**. It lays down an absolute procedural condition precedent:

> The President **shall not issue** a Proclamation under `Article 352(1)`—or a Proclamation **varying** such a Proclamation—unless the **decision of the Union Cabinet** (*"that is to say, the Council consisting of the Prime Minister and other Ministers of Cabinet rank appointed under Article 75"*) that such a Proclamation may be issued **has been communicated to him in writing**.

Notice four vital Prelims nuances packed into `Article 352(3)`:
1. **Sole textual occurrence of "Cabinet":** `Article 352(3)` is the **ONLY place in the entire Constitution of India where the word "Cabinet" appears** (and is expressly defined). It was absent from the original 1950 text, which used only "Council of Ministers" (`Articles 74–75`).
2. **Narrower than Council of Ministers:** The decision must be that of the **Union Cabinet** (the Prime Minister plus Cabinet-rank Ministers—excluding Ministers of State and Deputy Ministers), **NOT** the Prime Minister acting alone, and **NOT** an oral or informal consultation.
3. **Mandatory Written Communication:** Verbal advice—even from the entire Cabinet—is constitutionally insufficient; the written communication of the Cabinet's decision must reach the President **prior to** the issuance (or variation) of the proclamation.
4. **Applies to Proclamation and Variation, NOT Revocation:** Notice that `Article 352(3)` requires written Cabinet communication for *issuing* or *varying* a proclamation; **revoking** a proclamation under `Article 352(2)` does not require the `352(3)` written Cabinet lock (ordinary `Article 74` advice or mandatory `Article 352(7)` Lok Sabha disapproval applies).

#### Territorial Scope: Whole of India or a Specified Part (`42nd Amendment, 1976`)

Under the original 1950 text, a Proclamation of Emergency under `Article 352` had to apply uniformly to the **entire country**, even if an armed conflict or security crisis was strictly localised to one border state or region. The **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`** cured this rigidity by amending `Article 352(1)` to empower the President to limit the operation of a National Emergency either to the **whole of India** OR to **such specified part of the territory of India** as may be named in the Proclamation. This practical reform was **retained** by the `44th Amendment Act, 1978`, recognising that surgical regional application avoids suspending federal autonomy across peaceful states when a security threat is geographically contained.

### Judicial Review: From the 38th Amendment Bar to Minerva Mills

Can the Supreme Court or a High Court scrutinise the validity of a National Emergency proclamation? The constitutional trajectory on this question swung through three dramatic stages:

1. **Pre-1975 Judicial Caution (*Makhan Singh* & *Bhutnath Mate*):** During the 1962 and 1971 external emergencies, the Supreme Court treated the President's "satisfaction" regarding whether a grave emergency existed—or whether it should continue—largely as a political question within executive judgment, declining to second-guess security assessments (*Bhutnath Mate v. State of West Bengal*, 1974).
2. **Absolute Ouster Bar (`38th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975`):** Enacted swiftly on **1 August 1975** during the Emergency, the `38th Amendment` inserted a finality clause (`Article 352(5)` as it then stood, alongside parallel bars in `Articles 123, 213, 239B, 356, 359, and 360`) declaring that the President's satisfaction in issuing a National Emergency **"shall be final and conclusive and shall not be questioned in any court on any ground."**
3. **Restoration of Judicial Review (`44th Amendment, 1978` & *Minerva Mills*, 1980):** The **`44th Amendment Act, 1978`** completely **deleted** the `38th Amendment`'s judicial-ouster clause from `Article 352` (as well as from `Articles 356` and `360`). Shortly thereafter, in ***Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India* (31 July 1980, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court—especially through Justice P.N. Bhagwati's landmark analysis of `Article 352`—explicitly affirmed that **a Proclamation of Emergency is NOT immune from judicial review**. While courts will not substitute their own assessment of the sufficiency or adequacy of security threats for that of the executive, the Court can strike down a proclamation (or its mala fide continuation) if the President's satisfaction is **mala fide**, **perverse**, **absurd**, or based on **wholly extraneous and irrelevant grounds**—in short, where there was *no genuine satisfaction at all*.

### Parliamentary Approval, Duration, and the Lok Sabha Dissolution Safeguard

Before 1978, Parliamentary control over a National Emergency was alarmingly weak: once approved by a simple majority within two months, a proclamation continued **indefinitely** until the executive chose to revoke it—allowing the 1962 Emergency to run for over five years and the 1971 Emergency for more than five years without a single periodic renewal vote! The **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`** completely overhauled this framework under **`Articles 352(4), (5), and (6)`**.

#### 1. Time Limit for Initial Parliamentary Approval (`Article 352(4)`)

Every Proclamation of Emergency (other than a proclamation revoking an earlier one) must be laid before **both Houses of Parliament** and **ceases to operate at the expiration of ONE MONTH** from its date of issue unless, before that period expires, it has been approved by resolutions of **both Houses separately**. *(Originally, the approval window was **two months**; the `44th Amendment` halved it to **one month** to prevent prolonged unchecked executive rule.)*

#### 2. The Lok Sabha Dissolution Safeguard (`Proviso` to `Articles 352(4)` and `(5)`)

What if the Lok Sabha has already been dissolved when the President proclaims a National Emergency, or is dissolved during the one-month approval window (or during a six-month renewal period) without having passed the approving resolution? Because the Rajya Sabha is a permanent House that is never dissolved, the Constitution provides an ingenious continuity safeguard:

- The proclamation **survives until 30 days from the first sitting of the newly reconstituted Lok Sabha**,
- **PROVIDED** the **Rajya Sabha** has in the meantime approved the proclamation within the original one-month (or six-month renewal) window!
- If the newly elected Lok Sabha approves it within those 30 days, the emergency continues; if it rejects or fails to approve it, the proclamation automatically lapses at the end of the 30th day. *(Crucial Prelims point: If **Rajya Sabha itself rejects** the proclamation or fails to approve it in time, the proclamation dies immediately—there is **NO Joint Sitting** under `Article 108` for emergency resolutions!)*

#### 3. Special Majority Requirement (`Article 352(6)`)

Every resolution approving the proclamation of a National Emergency—or approving its continuance for a further six-month period—must be passed by **each House of Parliament separately** by a **Special Majority** (identical to the `Article 368` parliamentary majority):

1. A **majority of the total membership** of that House (absolute majority—more than 50% of total sanctioned strength, irrespective of vacancies or absentees); **AND**
2. A **majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting**.

*(Before the `44th Amendment`, a mere **simple majority** sufficed to approve a National Emergency—an astonishingly low bar that made partisan ratification effortless.)*

#### 4. Duration and Indefinite Periodic Renewal (`Article 352(5)`)

Once approved by both Houses of Parliament by special majority, the National Emergency continues in operation for **six months** from the date of passing of the second of the two approving resolutions. It can be **extended for an indefinite period**—there is **NO constitutional maximum limit** on how long a National Emergency can last—**provided** both Houses of Parliament pass fresh approving resolutions by **special majority every six months**. This mandatory six-monthly parliamentary renewal (inserted by the `44th Amendment`) ensures that no government can perpetuate an emergency passively once the crisis passes.

```flow

```

### Revocation: Presidential Authority vs Mandatory Lok Sabha Disapproval

A Proclamation of Emergency may be terminated through **two independent routes**, illustrating an asymmetric constitutional safeguard intentionally tilted toward the popularly elected House (`Lok Sabha`):

#### Route 1: Executive Revocation by the President (`Article 352(2)`)

The President may revoke a Proclamation of Emergency **at any time** by issuing a subsequent Proclamation. Crucially, **a proclamation of revocation does NOT require parliamentary approval**—nor does it trigger the `Article 352(3)` written Cabinet requirement (it operates on ordinary executive advice under `Article 74`).

#### Route 2: Mandatory Revocation on Lok Sabha Disapproval (`Articles 352(7)` and `352(8)`)

Prior to 1978, Parliament had no mechanism to compel an unwilling executive to lift an emergency. The **`44th Amendment Act, 1978`** broke that executive monopoly by inserting two revolutionary clauses:

- **Mandatory Revocation by Simple Majority (`Article 352(7)`):** The President **MUST** revoke a Proclamation of Emergency if the **Lok Sabha** passes a resolution disapproving the proclamation or disapproving its continuance. Two razor-sharp contrasts with the approval stage are favourite UPSC Prelims traps:
  1. **House Involved:** **ONLY the Lok Sabha** votes on a disapproval resolution; the **Rajya Sabha has ZERO role** in forcing revocation!
  2. **Majority Required:** Only a **SIMPLE MAJORITY** (majority of members present and voting) of the Lok Sabha suffices to end an Emergency—whereas approving or renewing it demands a **Special Majority** of **both Houses**!
- **Special Sitting Requisition Mechanism (`Article 352(8)`):** What if the ruling party refuses to convene the Lok Sabha to avoid facing a disapproval vote? `Article 352(8)` prevents such evasion: if **not less than one-tenth (`1/10th`) of the total number of members of the Lok Sabha** submit a **written notice** of intention to move a disapproval resolution—
  - to the **Speaker**, if the Lok Sabha is **in session**, OR
  - to the **President**, if the Lok Sabha is **not in session**—
  then a **special sitting of the Lok Sabha MUST be held within 14 days** from the date on which such notice is received, specifically to consider that resolution.

> 7. **`20 & 21`** permanently non-suspendable (`Art. 359`), `Art. 19` external-only (`Art. 358`), recital rule, and judicial review restored.

### Effects on Centre–State Executive, Legislative, and Financial Relations

A Proclamation of National Emergency triggers drastic, immediate shifts in India's federal distribution of powers across three interconnected domains—converting the dual polity into a tightly coordinated unitary command structure without dissolving state governments.

#### 1. Executive Effects (`Article 353(a)`)

In normal times, the Centre's power to issue executive directions to States is strictly bounded by specific constitutional provisions (`Articles 256` and `257`, covering compliance with Parliamentary laws, railways, and national communications). Under **`Article 353(a)`**, however, the moment a National Emergency operates, the executive power of the Union extends to **giving directions to ANY State as to the manner in which the executive power thereof is to be exercised**—covering **any matter whatsoever**, including State List subjects like police, public order, prisons, and local administration.

Crucially, **State Governments (Chief Ministers and Councils of Ministers) are NOT dismissed or suspended** during a National Emergency under `Article 352` (a foundational contrast with `Article 356` President's Rule!). They continue to exist and function, but they are brought under the **complete administrative control and superintendence of the Union**.

#### 2. Legislative Effects (`Articles 353(b), 250, and 251`)

During a National Emergency, **`Article 250`** empowers **Parliament to make laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India with respect to ANY matter enumerated in the State List (`List II`)**. Similarly, under `Article 353(b)`, Parliament may confer powers and impose duties on the Union or its officers regarding matters not in the Union List.

Four technical rules govern this emergency legislative takeover:
- **State Legislatures Are NOT Suspended:** Unlike President's Rule (`Article 356`), State Legislatures continue to sit and remain fully competent to legislate on State List subjects. The constitutional distribution of legislative powers is **not abolished**; rather, Parliament acquires an overriding **concurrent legislative jurisdiction** over the State List.
- **Parliamentary Supremacy in Repugnancy (`Article 251`):** If any provision of a State law conflicts with a Parliamentary law enacted under `Article 250`, the **Parliamentary law prevails**, and the State law remains inoperative to the extent of repugnancy *so long as the Parliamentary law remains in effect*.
- **Six-Month Sunset Clause (`Article 250(2)`):** Laws made by Parliament on State List subjects during a National Emergency **automatically cease to have effect on the expiration of SIX MONTHS after the Emergency has ceased to operate**, except with respect to things already done or omitted to be done before that expiry. *(Compare this sharply with `Article 357(2)` under President's Rule, where Parliamentary laws **survive indefinitely** after revocation!)*
- **Presidential Ordinances on State Subjects:** While a National Emergency is in force, if Parliament is not in session, the President may also promulgate **ordinances (`Article 123`)** on State List subjects!
- **Spillover Effect of a Partial Emergency (`Proviso` to `Article 353` — `42nd Amendment, 1976`):** When a National Emergency is declared only in a **specified part of India**, the Union's executive directions (`Art. 353(a)`) and Parliament's legislative authority (`Art. 353(b)`) extend **not merely to the State where the emergency operates, but ALSO to ANY OTHER STATE** if and in so far as the security of India or any part thereof is threatened by activities in or in relation to the emergency-affected territory. For example, if armed rebellion broke out in a border state and insurgents operated supply networks from a neighbouring state, the Centre could issue `Art. 353` directives and legislate on State subjects in that neighbouring state too.

#### 3. Financial Effects (`Article 354`)

While a National Emergency is in operation, **`Article 354(1)`** empowers the **President** by order to **modify the constitutional distribution of revenues between the Union and the States** laid down in **`Articles 268 to 279`** (covering tax-sharing under the Divisible Pool, Finance Commission devolution, and statutory grants-in-aid). The President may reduce or altogether cancel the transfer of financial resources from the Centre to the States so that national revenues can be mobilised for defence or emergency management.

Two built-in constitutional checks govern `Article 354`:
1. **End-of-Financial-Year Cap:** Every such Presidential financial modification order **cannot extend beyond the expiration of the financial year** in which the Proclamation of Emergency ceases to operate (i.e., it expires no later than **31 March** following the revocation of the emergency).
2. **Parliamentary Scrutiny (`Article 354(2)`):** Every order issued under `Article 354(1)` must be **laid before each House of Parliament** as soon as may be after it is made.

### Effects on the Tenure of the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies

In normal circumstances, the term of the **Lok Sabha** (`Article 83(2)`) and every **State Legislative Assembly** (`Article 172(1)`) is **five years** from the date appointed for its first meeting. During an active Proclamation of National Emergency, however, holding nationwide or state-wide general elections may be logistically impossible or dangerous to national security.

Accordingly, the provisos to **`Article 83(2)`** and **`Article 172(1)`** empower **Parliament by law** to extend the normal five-year term of:
- the **Lok Sabha**, and
- **any State Legislative Assembly**

subject to **two strict constitutional limits**:
1. **One Year at a Time:** Each Parliamentary enactment may extend the life of the House by **not exceeding ONE YEAR at a time** (though Parliament can pass successive one-year extensions for any number of times while the Emergency remains in force).
2. **Six-Month Post-Emergency Cap:** Regardless of how long an extension was enacted for, it **cannot continue in any case beyond a period of SIX MONTHS after the Proclamation of Emergency has ceased to operate**.

> [!example]
> **Historical Precedent — Extension of the Fifth Lok Sabha (1976–1977):**
> Elected in March 1971, the Fifth Lok Sabha's normal five-year term was due to expire on **18 March 1976**. Exercising the proviso to `Article 83(2)` during the ongoing Emergency, Parliament passed two successive statutes—the *House of the People (Extension of Duration) Act, 1976* (extending its term by one year up to March 1977) and the *House of the People (Extension of Duration) Amendment Act, 1976* (extending it by a second year up to March 1978). Simultaneously, the `42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976` raised the *normal* tenure of the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies from five to six years—a distortion repealed by the `44th Amendment Act, 1978`, which restored the five-year term. In January 1977, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi abruptly recommended dissolution of the extended Fifth Lok Sabha and called fresh general elections, which the Janata Party won decisively in March 1977.

### Effects on Fundamental Rights: Article 358 vs Article 359

No topic in `Part XVIII` is tested more relentlessly in UPSC Prelims—or carries deeper philosophical weight for Mains—than the distinction between **`Article 358`** and **`Article 359`**. Both provisions govern the impact of a **National Emergency (`Article 352`)** on **Fundamental Rights (`Part III`)**, yet they operate through completely different constitutional mechanisms. *(Note: Neither President's Rule under `Article 356` nor Financial Emergency under `Article 360` affects Fundamental Rights in any manner!)*

#### 1. Article 358: Automatic Suspension of the Six Freedoms of Article 19

Under **`Article 358(1)`**, the moment a Proclamation of National Emergency is issued on the ground of **war or external aggression** (i.e., an **External Emergency**), the **six Fundamental Freedoms under `Article 19`** (speech and expression, peaceful assembly, associations/unions/cooperatives, free movement, residence, and profession/trade) are **automatically suspended**. No separate Presidential Order is needed.

While this suspension lasts, the State (defined broadly under `Article 12`, covering Parliament, State Legislatures, and all executive authorities) is completely freed from the constitutional restraints of `Article 19`: it may enact any law or take any executive action that abridges `Article 19` freedoms, and no citizen can challenge such law or action during or *after* the emergency on the ground of `Article 19` violation. The moment the National Emergency ends, **`Article 19` revives automatically**, and any inconsistent law enacted during the emergency **ceases to have effect** to the extent of its inconsistency—except with respect to acts already done or omitted during the emergency (for which no retrospective remedy lies).

**Two Transformative Restrictions Added by the `44th Amendment Act, 1978`:**
1. **External Emergency ONLY (`Article 358(1)`):** Before 1978, `Article 358` suspended `Article 19` automatically during *every* National Emergency—including an internal emergency. The `44th Amendment` restricted `Article 358` strictly to **war or external aggression**. Consequently, **during an Internal Emergency declared on the ground of "armed rebellion", `Article 19` is NOT automatically suspended under `Article 358`!**
2. **The Recital / Emergency-Nexus Lock (`Article 358(2)`):** Previously, *any* law or executive action—even one completely unrelated to the emergency, such as a municipal tax dispute or routine commercial regulation—enjoyed blanket immunity from `Article 19`. Inserting **`Article 358(2)`**, the `44th Amendment` narrowed immunity strictly to:
   - only those **laws that contain an explicit recital** declaring that the law is in relation to the Proclamation of Emergency in operation when it was made; and
   - only those **executive actions taken under a law containing such a recital**!
   *(Also note the `42nd Amendment` proviso to `Art. 358(1)`: if an External Emergency is proclaimed in only a part of India, laws/actions in other States still enjoy `Art. 358` immunity if linked to security threats arising from the emergency-affected part.)*

#### 2. Article 359: Presidential Order Suspending Enforcement of Specified Fundamental Rights

Unlike `Article 358`, **`Article 359(1)`** does **NOT** operate automatically and does **NOT** abolish the Fundamental Rights themselves. Instead, during a National Emergency—whether **External** (war/external aggression) **OR Internal** (armed rebellion)—the **President** may issue a **separate Presidential Order** declaring that the **right to move any court for the enforcement of such of the Fundamental Rights conferred by Part III** (as may be specifically mentioned in the Order) shall remain suspended for the period during which the Proclamation is in force, or for any **shorter period** specified in the Order. Under `Article 359(2)`, the Order may extend to the **whole of India or any part thereof**, and under **`Article 359(3)`**, every such Order must be **laid before each House of Parliament** as soon as may be.

Crucially, understand the interplay between **`Article 359(1)`**, **`Article 359(1A)`** (inserted by the `38th Amendment, 1975`), and **`Article 359(1B)`** (inserted by the `44th Amendment, 1978`):
- Under **`Article 359(1)`**, what is suspended is strictly the **remedy (enforcement in courts)**—not the theoretical existence of the right itself. Once the Presidential Order expires, pending proceedings revive.
- However, under **`Article 359(1A)`** (added by the `38th Amendment, 1975` and retained with vital exceptions by the `44th Amendment`), while a Presidential Order naming certain Fundamental Rights is in operation, the State is also freed from the corresponding Part III restrictions when making laws or taking executive action—meaning such emergency-era laws are **not void *ab initio***, though they cease to operate once the Presidential Order ends (and no remedy lies afterwards for acts done under them during the Order).
- **Two Monumental Democratic Safeguards Added by the `44th Amendment Act, 1978`:**
  1. **Permanent Immunity of `Articles 20` and `21` (`Articles 359(1)` and `359(1A)`):** The President **CANNOT** suspend the right to move any court for the enforcement of **`Article 20`** (protection in respect of conviction for offences—no ex-post-facto criminal law, no double jeopardy, no self-incrimination) and **`Article 21`** (protection of life and personal liberty). Even in the darkest hour of war or armed rebellion, the right to life, personal liberty, and *habeas corpus* can **NEVER** be extinguished!
  2. **The Recital / Emergency-Nexus Lock (`Article 359(1B)`):** Mirroring `Article 358(2)`, immunity under `Article 359(1A)` protects **ONLY** those laws that contain an explicit **recital** that the law is in relation to the Proclamation of Emergency in operation, and **ONLY** executive actions taken under a law containing such a recital!

```compare
# Article 358 vs Article 359: Suspension of Fundamental Rights During National Emergency

Mode of Activation :: **Automatic** the instant an External Emergency is proclaimed; no separate order needed | **NOT automatic**; requires a **specific Presidential Order** (laid before both Houses per `Art. 359(3)`)
Emergency Grounds :: Operates **ONLY in External Emergency** (war or external aggression) — **NEVER** on armed rebellion (`44th Amend`) | Operates in **BOTH External Emergency AND Internal Emergency** (war, external aggression, or armed rebellion)
Substantive vs Remedial Effect :: Suspends **`Article 19` itself** for the entire emergency; State may freely abridge Art. 19 | Suspends only the **right to move courts (enforcement/remedy)** for named FRs (while `Art. 359(1A)` shields emergency-recital laws during Order)

Emergency-Nexus Recital (`44th Amend`) :: Protects ONLY laws reciting emergency nexus & executive action under them (`Art. 358(2)`) | Protects ONLY laws reciting emergency nexus & executive action under them (`Art. 359(1B)`)
```

> [!case]
> **From *Makhan Singh* (1963) to *ADM Jabalpur* (1976) and *K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017): The Battle Over *Habeas Corpus***
> - ***Makhan Singh Tarsikka v. State of Punjab* (2 September 1963, 7-Judge Bench, 6–1):** Examining detentions under the Defence of India Rules, 1962 during the China war, the Supreme Court held that an `Article 359(1)` Presidential Order barred *habeas corpus* petitions (both under `Arts. 32/226` and `Section 491 CrPC`) if grounded in violation of the suspended FRs (`Arts. 14, 21, 22`). Crucially, however, the Court preserved narrow statutory challenges—holding that a detainee could *still* approach courts if the detention was *ultra vires* the Defence of India Act, *mala fide*, or ordered by an unauthorised officer!
> - ***Mohd. Yaqub v. State of Jammu & Kashmir* (10 November 1967, 7-Judge Bench):** Held that a Presidential Order under `Article 359(1)` is **not** a "law" within the meaning of `Article 13(2)` and therefore cannot be tested against the very Fundamental Rights whose enforcement it suspends.
> - ***ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla* (28 April 1976, 5-Judge Bench, 4–1 — The "Habeas Corpus Case"):** During the 1975–77 Internal Emergency, President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed issued a sweeping Presidential Order on **27 June 1975** suspending enforcement of `Articles 14, 21, and 22`—without linking it to any specific statute. Nine High Courts bravely held that detainees under the **Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA), 1971** could still challenge patently illegal or *mala fide* detentions. Reversing all nine High Courts by a notorious **4–1 majority** (CJI A.N. Ray and Justices M.H. Beg, Y.V. Chandrachud, and P.N. Bhagwati), the Supreme Court held that **`Article 21` was the "sole repository" of the right to life and personal liberty**; once its enforcement was suspended under `Article 359`, no citizen had *any locus standi* to move *any* court for *habeas corpus*—even if detention was patently illegal, *mala fide*, or completely without legal authority!
> - **Justice Hans Raj Khanna's Immortal Lone Dissent:** Standing alone—a courage that cost him supersession for Chief Justice of India in January 1977—Justice Khanna declared that the sanctity of life and personal liberty pre-existed the Constitution as an inalienable rule-of-law principle, not a bounty gifted by `Article 21`. Even if `Article 21` were suspended, the State still had no power to deprive a citizen of life or liberty without statutory authority. Quoting Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes, he appealed *"to the brooding spirit of the law, to the intelligence of a future day."*
> - **Constitutional & Judicial Reversal:** Parliament answered *ADM Jabalpur* first through the **`44th Amendment Act, 1978`**, making `Articles 20` and `21` permanently non-suspendable. Nearly four decades later, in ***Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India* (24 August 2017, 9-Judge Unanimous Bench)**, the Supreme Court formally **overruled *ADM Jabalpur***—authored ironically by Justice Dr. D.Y. Chandrachud (son of Justice Y.V. Chandrachud), who praised Justice Khanna's dissent while Justice Sanjay Kishan Kaul declared that *ADM Jabalpur* must be buried *"ten fathom deep, with no chance of resurrection."*

> [!prelims]
> **Five Classic UPSC Prelims Traps on Article 352, 358 & 359:**
> 1. **Sole Mention of "Cabinet":** `Article 352(3)` (inserted by `44th Amend, 1978`) is the **ONLY** Article in the Constitution that mentions and defines the **"Union Cabinet"**.
> 2. **Approval vs Revocation Asymmetry:** Approving/renewing `Art. 352` needs **Special Majority in BOTH Houses** within **1 month**; revoking it via Parliament needs **Simple Majority in LOK SABHA ONLY** (Rajya Sabha has no vote; `1/10th` LS members notice triggers a special sitting within **14 days**).
> 3. **Does `Art. 358` Operate in an Armed Rebellion?** **NO!** Since the `44th Amendment (1978)`, `Article 358` automatically suspends `Article 19` **ONLY** in an External Emergency (war or external aggression)—*never* in an Internal Emergency (armed rebellion).
> 4. **Can `Article 19` Be Suspended During an Armed Rebellion via `Article 359`?** **YES!** While `Art. 358` does not operate during armed rebellion, the President *can* still suspend the **enforcement** of `Article 19` by issuing a specific Presidential Order under `Article 359(1)` (only `Articles 20 & 21` are completely off-limits to `Art. 359`!).
> 5. **Recital Shield:** Under `Arts. 358(2)` and `359(1B)`, routine statutes lacking an explicit emergency recital enjoy **zero immunity** even while an Emergency is active.

#### The 1975–77 Emergency Excesses and the Shah Commission of Inquiry (1977–1978)

The twenty-one months between **25 June 1975** and **21 March 1977** demonstrated how swiftly unchecked emergency provisions could erode constitutional democracy from within. Because the 1971 External Emergency was already operating, the 1975 Internal Emergency operated **simultaneously** (retroactively validated by the `38th Amendment`). Over **1,10,000 people**—including prominent national opposition leaders (Jayaprakash Narayan, Morarji Desai, Atal Bihari Vajpayee, L.K. Advani, George Fernandes, Madhu Dandavate)—were detained without trial under the **Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA), 1971** and Defence of India Rules. Pre-censorship was imposed on the press, electricity to major Delhi newspapers was cut on the night of 25 June, **16 High Court judges** were punitively transferred, the `39th Amendment (1975)` tried to insulate the Prime Minister's election from judicial review (struck down in *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain*, November 1975), and the sweeping **`42nd Amendment Act, 1976`** rewrote vast stretches of the Constitution.

After the Janata Party government took office in March 1977, it constituted the **Shah Commission of Inquiry** on **28 May 1977** under `Section 3` of the Commissions of Inquiry Act, 1952, chaired by former Chief Justice of India **Justice J.C. Shah**. Submitting three authoritative reports (**Interim Report I** on **11 March 1978**, **Interim Report II** on **26 April 1978**, and the **Final Report** on **6 August 1978**), the Shah Commission documented systemic constitutional violations:

- **No Genuine Threat to National Security:** There was neither an economic breakdown nor a law-and-order crisis justifying a National Emergency; intelligence reports prior to 25 June 1975 contained no warning of imminent national breakdown.
- **Subversion of Cabinet Government:** Prime Minister Indira Gandhi acted **unilaterally**, bypassing her own Council of Ministers and informing the Cabinet only *after* the proclamation was signed—a finding that directly inspired **`Article 352(3)`**.
- **Misuse of Preventive Detention and Bureaucratic Subversion:** MISA was deployed routinely against peaceful political opponents and journalists without statutory grounds; civil servants and police officers—famously rebuked by L.K. Advani as having *"crawled when asked merely to bend"*—abdicated institutional neutrality; coercive demolitions (notably at Turkman Gate, Delhi) and forced sterilisations violated basic human dignity.
- **Media Censorship and Judicial Pressure:** All India Radio, Doordarshan, and the print press were subjected to illegal censorship and advertising boycotts, while judicial independence was undermined through non-extensions and punitive transfers.

### National Emergency (Article 352) vs Martial Law (Article 34)

A classic conceptual distinction tested repeatedly in M. Laxmikanth and UPSC Prelims compares **National Emergency (`Article 352`, Part XVIII)** with **Martial Law (`Article 34`, Part III)**. Although both address grave threats to order, they are constitutionally distinct regimes:

| Dimension | Martial Law (`Article 34`) | National Emergency (`Article 352`) |
|---|---|---|
| **Constitutional Definition** | **NOT defined** anywhere in the Constitution; implicit concept derived from English common law. | **Explicitly and exhaustively codified** in `Part XVIII` (`Articles 352–354, 358–359`). |
| **Impact on Fundamental Rights** | Affects **ONLY Fundamental Rights** (Parliament may pass an **Act of Indemnity** under `Art. 34` validating military acts during martial law). | Affects **BOTH Fundamental Rights** (`Arts. 358–359`) **AND Centre–State executive, legislative, and financial relations**, plus tenure of legislatures. |
| **Impact on Civil Government & Ordinary Courts** | **Suspends ordinary civil administration and ordinary law courts**; military authorities administer law directly. | **Civil government and ordinary courts continue functioning** uninterrupted; State ministries are controlled, not replaced by military officers. |
| **Grounds of Imposition** | Imposed solely to **restore breakdown of law and order** due to extreme violence, riot, or insurrection. | Imposed strictly on three specific grounds: **war, external aggression, or armed rebellion**. |
| **Territorial Extent** | Imposed in **some specific disturbed area** of the country only. | May be imposed across the **whole of India or any specified part** thereof. |
| **Specific Constitutional Authorisation** | **No express provision** authorising the executive to *declare* martial law (`Art. 34` only empowers Parliament to indemnify acts done while it was in force). | **Expressly codified** proclamation, written Cabinet advice, approval, duration, and revocation procedures. |

*(Cross-reference: `Article 34` and the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958 — which operates *short* of martial law — are covered alongside `Article 33` in **Chapter 5: Fundamental Rights**.)*

## 3. President's Rule and Failure of Constitutional Machinery in States (Articles 355, 356, and 365)

No single provision in India's Constitution has generated more political turbulence, federal litigation, or commission reports than **`Article 356`**. Popularly called **President's Rule**—and loosely referred to as "State Emergency" or "Constitutional Emergency" even though the Constitution deliberately avoids the word "Emergency" here—`Article 356` empowers the Union to take over the executive and legislative administration of a State whenever its constitutional machinery breaks down. Far from operating in isolation, `Article 356` sits inside a tight three-Article constitutional circuit anchored by **`Article 355`** (the Union's protective duty) and **`Article 365`** (the sanction for disobeying Union directives), complemented operationally by **`Article 357`** (delegation and survival of laws).

### Article 355: The Union's Triple Constitutional Duty

The constitutional justification for federal intervention in a State begins with **`Article 355`**, modelled partly on `Article IV, Section 4` of the US Constitution (the Guarantee Clause) and `Section 119` of the Australian Constitution, yet distinctly Indian in scope. `Article 355` imposes an affirmative **triple constitutional duty** on the Union:

1. **Protection Against External Aggression:** To protect every State against external aggression.
2. **Protection Against Internal Disturbance:** To protect every State against severe **internal disturbance**—crucially, the `44th Amendment (1978)` left the words *"internal disturbance"* intact in `Article 355` even while replacing them with *"armed rebellion"* in `Article 352`.
3. **Constitutional Compliance Guarantee:** To **ensure that the government of every State is carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution**.

In ***Sarbananda Sonowal v. Union of India* (2005, 3-Judge Bench)**—striking down the Illegal Migrants (Determination by Tribunals) Act, 1983—the Supreme Court read `Article 355` expansively, holding that large-scale illegal cross-border immigration into Assam amounted to "external aggression" and "internal disturbance" engaging the Union's protective duty under `Article 355`. More broadly, the Sarkaria Commission (1988) and the nine-judge bench in *S.R. Bommai* (1994) stressed that `Article 355` is **not** a licence for immediate dismissal of State ministries: before invoking the extreme medicine of `Article 356`, the Union is duty-bound under `Article 355` to exhaust all milder preventive and corrective measures—such as deploying Central Armed Police Forces (CAPFs) in aid of civil power (`List I, Entry 2A`), issuing formal administrative directions (`Arts. 256–257`), and sending constitutional warnings to help an elected State government survive a crisis.

### Twin Constitutional Triggers: Article 356 and Article 365

President's Rule can be proclaimed on **two distinct constitutional grounds**—one located inside `Part XVIII` (`Article 356(1)`) and the other tucked inside `Part XIX` (`Article 365`):

#### Trigger 1: Breakdown of Constitutional Machinery (`Article 356(1)`)

Under **`Article 356(1)`**, the President is empowered to issue a Proclamation if, **on receipt of a report from the Governor of a State OR "otherwise"**, the President is satisfied that a **situation has arisen in which the government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution**.

Notice the explosive constitutional weight of the single word **"otherwise"**: the President does **NOT** strictly need a Governor's report to impose President's Rule! Even if a Governor refuses to recommend dismissal—or sends no report at all—the Union Council of Ministers can advise the President to invoke `Article 356` on the basis of independent material (intelligence reports, parliamentary inquiries, or undeniable constitutional impasse). During the Constituent Assembly debates, **H.V. Kamath** vehemently protested the inclusion of *"otherwise"* as a *"blank cheque"* inviting arbitrary central interference, yet Dr. Ambedkar defended it as necessary should a partisan Governor fail to report a genuine constitutional collapse.

#### Trigger 2: Failure to Comply with Union Directions (`Article 365`)

Located in **Part XIX (Miscellaneous)**, **`Article 365`** supplies the coercive tooth behind the Union's administrative directions under `Articles 256, 257, 339(2), 350A, 353,` and `360`. It declares that **where any State has failed to comply with, or to give effect to, any directions given in the exercise of the executive power of the Union under any of the provisions of the Constitution**, it shall be **lawful for the President to hold** that a situation has arisen in which the government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. Once the President forms that satisfaction via `Article 365`, `Article 356(1)` is lawfully triggered.

> "In fact I share the sentiments expressed by my honorable Friend Mr. Gupte yesterday that the proper thing we ought to expect is that such articles will never be called into operation and that they would remain a dead letter. If at all they are brought into operation, I hope the President, who is endowed with these powers, will take proper precautions before actually suspending the administration of the provinces." — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, Vol. IX, 4 August 1949

### Parliamentary Approval, the One-Year Wall, and Revocation

Unlike `Article 352`, which demands a one-month approval window and a special majority, President's Rule under **`Articles 356(3)–(5)`** follows a lower parliamentary threshold combined with a strict statutory ceiling on total duration.

#### 1. Initial Approval (`Article 356(3)`)

Every Proclamation under `Article 356(1)` (other than a revoking proclamation) must be laid before both Houses of Parliament and **ceases to operate at the expiration of TWO MONTHS** from its date of issue unless, before that period expires, it has been approved by resolutions of **both Houses of Parliament separately** by a **SIMPLE MAJORITY** (majority of members present and voting in each House).

- **Lok Sabha Dissolution Safeguard (`Proviso` to `Article 356(3)`):** Mirroring `Article 352`, if the Lok Sabha is dissolved at the time the proclamation is issued or dissolves during the two-month period without approving it, the proclamation survives until **30 days from the first sitting of the newly reconstituted Lok Sabha**, **PROVIDED the Rajya Sabha has approved it in the meantime**.
- **No Joint Sitting:** As with `Article 352`, if either House (e.g., an Opposition-majority Rajya Sabha) refuses to pass the approving resolution, there is no Joint Sitting—the proclamation automatically lapses at the end of two months (or earlier upon rejection), and under *S.R. Bommai* (1994), the dismissed State government and suspended Assembly automatically revive!

#### 2. Duration, the Six-Month Cycle, and the Three-Year Ceiling (`Article 356(4)`)

Once approved by both Houses, a Proclamation of President's Rule remains in force for **SIX MONTHS** from the date of its **issue** (note: in `Article 356(4)` as originally structured and applied in practice, initial six months runs from the date of issue of the proclamation—e.g., Manipur's 13 February 2025 proclamation ran until 12 August 2025, then extended for six months with effect from 13 August 2025—whereas `Article 352(5)` measures six months from the date of passing of the second parliamentary resolution!). It can be extended for **six months at a time** by fresh simple-majority resolutions of both Houses, up to an **absolute maximum period of THREE YEARS**.

*(Historical Amendment Note: The `42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976` had doubled the single-stretch approval/renewal duration of `Article 356(4)` from **six months to one year**; the **`44th Amendment Act, 1978`** restored the stricter **six-month** cycle and simultaneously erected the famous "one-year wall" under `Article 356(5)`.)*

#### 3. The One-Year Wall (`Article 356(5)` — `44th Amendment, 1978`)

To prevent the Centre from routinely keeping States under indefinite central rule up to three years without holding elections, the **`44th Amendment Act, 1978`** substituted a brand-new **`Article 356(5)`** (simultaneously deleting the `38th Amendment`'s judicial-ouster clause). Under **`Article 356(5)`**, **President's Rule CANNOT be extended beyond ONE YEAR** (i.e., beyond two six-month spells) unless **BOTH of the following twin cumulative conditions** are simultaneously satisfied:

1. A **Proclamation of National Emergency (`Article 352`)** is in operation in the **whole of India**, OR in the **whole or any part of that State**, at the time of passing such resolution; **AND**
2. The **Election Commission of India (ECI)** **certifies** that the continuance in force of the Proclamation is necessary on account of **difficulties in holding general elections** to the Legislative Assembly of the concerned State.

Because India has not had a National Emergency since 1977, `Article 356(5)` acts in practice as a formidable **one-year constitutional wall**: no State can ordinarily remain under President's Rule beyond 12 months unless Parliament enacts a specific **Constitutional Amendment under `Article 368`** carving out an exception for that State!

> [!example]
> **How Punjab Required Five Constitutional Amendments (`48th, 59th, 64th, 67th, 68th`) to Cross the One-Year & Three-Year Walls:**
> During the peak of militancy in Punjab, President's Rule imposed on **6 October 1983** needed the **`48th Amendment Act (1984)`** to cross one year without meeting `Article 356(5)`. Even more dramatically, the prolonged spell imposed on **11 May 1987** (lasting nearly five years until **25 February 1992**) required **four successive constitutional amendments**:
> - **`59th Amendment Act, 1988`** (30 March 1988): Exempted Punjab's 11 May 1987 proclamation from `Article 356(5)` up to 3 years—and controversially inserted **`Article 359A`** allowing an "internal disturbance" emergency and even suspension of `Article 21` in Punjab! Following widespread outrage, the V.P. Singh government enacted the **`63rd Amendment Act, 1989`** (effective **6 January 1990**) repealing `Article 359A` and the `59th Amendment`'s proviso.
> - **`64th Amendment Act, 1990`** (16 April 1990): Extended Punjab's three-year ceiling to **3 years and 6 months**.
> - **`67th Amendment Act, 1990`** (4 October 1990): Extended it to **4 years**.
> - **`68th Amendment Act, 1991`** (12 March 1991): Extended it to **5 years**—enabling central rule until elections were finally held in February 1992.

#### 4. Revocation (`Article 356(2)`)

A Proclamation of President's Rule may be **revoked or varied by the President at any time** by issuing a subsequent Proclamation under **`Article 356(2)`**. Crucially—unlike `Article 352`—**revocation does NOT require parliamentary approval**, and Parliament (even the Lok Sabha) has **NO special disapproval resolution mechanism** to force early revocation (though either House can decline to renew it at the end of the six-month term).

```flow
# Constitutional Pathway of President's Rule (Articles 355, 356, 357 & 365)
Trigger 1: Art. 356(1) Breakdown | Governor's report OR 'otherwise' — constitutional impasse || Trigger 2: Art. 365 Default | State failure to comply with Union executive directions
President Issues Proclamation | State Council of Ministers dismissed; Assembly placed in SUSPENDED ANIMATION ONLY (no dissolution before parliamentary nod — Bommai)
Parliamentary Ratification Within 2 Months | Approved by BOTH Houses separately by SIMPLE Majority (30-day new LS rule if RS approves; NO Joint Sitting)
Duration Up to 1 Year | 6 months at a time (renewed by Simple Majority in both Houses)
The 1-Year Wall (Art. 356(5)) | Beyond 1 yr (up to max 3 yrs) requires BOTH: (1) Art. 352 Emergency active in India/State AND (2) ECI certification of election difficulty
Termination / Revocation | President revokes anytime alone via Art. 356(2) (no parliamentary approval needed) — Art. 357 laws SURVIVE until State repeals/alters!
```

### Consequences of President's Rule: Executive, Legislative, Financial, and Judicial

When President's Rule takes effect under **`Articles 356(1)` and `357`**, the constitutional balance inside the State changes far more drastically than under `Article 352`, while still preserving specific institutional firewalls:

#### 1. Executive Takeover (`Article 356(1)(a)`)

The President assumes all or any functions of the State Government and all or any powers vested in the Governor or any body or authority in the State (other than the State Legislature). The **State Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister is immediately dismissed**. In practice, the **Governor** carries on the State administration on behalf of the President, assisted by the **Chief Secretary** and **Advisers** (typically senior retired IAS/IPS officers) appointed by the President. Because the President acts through the Union Cabinet, this is why `Article 356` is popularly dubbed **President's Rule** or **Central Rule**.

#### 2. Legislative Takeover and Survival of Laws (`Articles 356(1)(b)` and `357`)

The President declares that the **powers of the State Legislature shall be exercisable by or under the authority of Parliament**. The State Legislative Assembly is **either suspended (placed in "suspended animation") or dissolved**—and post-*S.R. Bommai* (1994), it can **ONLY be suspended**, never dissolved, until **both Houses of Parliament approve** the proclamation!

Under **`Article 357(1)`**, once Parliament assumes the State Legislature's powers:
- **Delegation to the President (`Article 357(1)(a)`):** Parliament routinely passes a *State Legislature (Delegation of Powers) Act* authorising the **President** to enact laws for the State—and even empowering the President to **sub-delegate** that authority to any other specified authority—generally in consultation with a **Consultative Committee of MPs** drawn from that State. Such statutes enacted by the President are known as **President's Acts**.
- **Authorisation of Expenditure (`Article 357(1)(c)`):** When the **Lok Sabha is not in session**, the President may authorise expenditure from the **Consolidated Fund of the State** pending its sanction by Parliament; while Parliament is in session, **Parliament passes the State Budget and State Appropriation Bills** directly (as Parliament did for Manipur's Budget in 2025).
- **Presidential Ordinances (`Article 123`):** When Parliament is in recess, the President may also promulgate **ordinances** for the governance of the State.
- **PERMANENT SURVIVAL OF LAWS (`Article 357(2)` — Critical Prelims Fact!):** Under `Article 357(2)` (as amended by the `42nd Amendment, 1976` and deliberately **retained** by the `44th Amendment`), **a law made by Parliament, the President, or any other delegated authority during President's Rule DOES NOT EXPIRE when President's Rule ends!** Unlike `Article 250` laws during a National Emergency (which lapse automatically six months post-emergency), `Article 357` laws **continue in force indefinitely after the revocation of President's Rule** until they are **repealed, altered, or re-enacted by the restored State Legislature** (or other competent authority).

#### 3. Complete Judicial Immunity of the High Court (`Proviso` to `Article 356(1)`)

The explicit **proviso to `Article 356(1)`** erects an iron constitutional wall around the State judiciary: **nothing in `Article 356(1)` authorises the President to assume to himself any of the powers vested in or exercisable by a High Court, or to suspend in whole or in part the operation of any provision of the Constitution relating to High Courts.** Thus, the constitutional status, jurisdiction, writ powers (`Article 226`), independence, and judges of the State High Court remain **100% untouched** during President's Rule.

#### 4. Unaffected Institutions: Fundamental Rights and Local Bodies

President's Rule has **NO effect whatsoever on the Fundamental Rights** of citizens in the State (`Articles 358` and `359` do not apply to `Article 356`). Similarly, **local self-government bodies** (Panchayats and Municipalities under Parts IX and IXA) are **not** dissolved as a consequence of President's Rule.

> [!prelims]
> **UPSC Prelims PYQ Master-Trap (`Prelims 2017` & `2018`) — What Is vs Is NOT a *Necessary* Consequence of `Article 356`?**
> In **UPSC Prelims 2017**, candidates were asked which of the following are *NOT necessarily* the consequences of proclaiming President's Rule in a State: **(1) Dissolution of the State Legislative Assembly**, **(2) Removal of the Council of Ministers in the State**, **(3) Dissolution of the local bodies**.
> - **Answer:** **(1) and (3)** are **NOT necessarily** consequences! Only **(2) Removal of the Council of Ministers** is inevitable. The Assembly need **NOT** be dissolved—it is ordinarily placed in **suspended animation** (and post-*Bommai* *cannot* be dissolved prior to parliamentary approval), and local bodies remain completely untouched!
> - **Four More Recurring Art. 356 Traps:** (1) Laws made under `Art. 357` **outlive** President's Rule until repealed/altered by the State Legislature; (2) **High Courts** are constitutionally insulated (`Proviso` to `Art. 356(1)`); (3) **Fundamental Rights** are totally unaffected; (4) `Article 356` applies **ONLY to States—NOT Union Territories**!

### Application to Union Territories: Distinct Constitutional and Statutory Mechanisms

A subtle distinction frequently missed by aspirants is that **`Article 356` applies strictly to "States"**—it does **NOT** directly govern Union Territories with legislatures. Instead, constitutional breakdown in those three Union Territories is triggered through separate tailor-made provisions:

| Union Territory | Exact Legal Provision | Key Structural Differences From Article 356 |
|---|---|---|
| **NCT of Delhi** | **`Article 239AB`** *(inserted by `69th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1991`)* | Constitutional provision (in Part VIII) allowing the President, on the **Lieutenant Governor's report or otherwise**, to suspend `Article 239AA` provisions if administration cannot be carried on in accordance with `Art. 239AA` or any law made thereunder. Invoked once (**Feb 2014 – Feb 2015** after Arvind Kejriwal resigned). |
| **Puducherry** | **`Section 51`, Government of Union Territories Act, 1963** | **Statutory** (not constitutional) provision authorising the President to suspend the UT legislature/ministry on LG's report or otherwise. Invoked multiple times (most recently **February–May 2021** after V. Narayanasamy lost majority); Puducherry has spent over 7 years cumulatively under central rule. |
| **UT of Jammu & Kashmir** | **`Section 73`, Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** | **Statutory** provision governing the UT since **31 October 2019** (prior to 2019, the erstwhile *State* of J&K first entered **Governor's Rule** for 6 months under `Section 92` of the J&K Constitution before `Article 356` kicked in—as happened on 20 June 2018 → `Art. 356` on 19 Dec 2018 → `Section 73` UT central rule from **31 October 2019 until 13 October 2024**, when it was revoked for Omar Abdullah's elected government!). |

### Judicial Review and the S.R. Bommai Watershed (1994)

For the first four decades after 1950, the Union Government treated `Article 356` as a political hammer to topple opposition-ruled State ministries—beginning with Kerala's democratically elected Communist ministry under E.M.S. Namboodiripad in **July 1959** and culminating in wholesale partisan sweeps: the Janata Party government dismissed **nine Congress-ruled State governments** simultaneously in **April 1977** after winning the Lok Sabha polls, and Indira Gandhi retaliated by dismissing **nine non-Congress State governments** in **February 1980** upon returning to power!

In ***State of Rajasthan v. Union of India* (order 29 April 1977; detailed reasons 6 May 1977, 7-Judge Bench)**, six Congress-ruled States filed original suits (`Article 131`) challenging Union Home Minister Charan Singh's letter advising their Chief Ministers to dissolve their Assemblies. While Justice P.N. Bhagwati cautiously remarked that the `38th Amendment`'s finality bar (`Art. 356(5)`) would not shield *mala fide* or wholly extraneous proclamations where there was *"no satisfaction at all"*, the Court ultimately refused to enter what it called the **"political thicket"**, upholding the mass dismissals and leaving `Article 356` practically unchecked for another seventeen years.

Everything changed on **11 March 1994**, when a **nine-judge Constitution Bench** (Justices S. Ratnavel Pandian, A.M. Ahmadi, Kuldip Singh, J.S. Verma, P.B. Sawant, K. Ramaswamy, S.C. Agrawal, Yogeshwar Dayal, and B.P. Jeevan Reddy) delivered India's most celebrated federal judgment: ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India***. Hearing clubbed petitions involving six States—**Karnataka (1989, S.R. Bommai's Janata Dal ministry dismissed without a floor test)**, **Nagaland (1988)**, **Meghalaya (1991)**, and **Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Himachal Pradesh (December 1992, dismissed following the Babri Masjid demolition)**—the Bench transformed `Article 356` from an unchecked political weapon into a strictly justiciable constitutional last resort.

> [!case]
> **The Complete Ten-Point Ratio of *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (11 March 1994, 9-Judge Constitution Bench):**
> 1. **Presidential Satisfaction Is Subject to Judicial Review:** The President's power under `Article 356` is **conditional, NOT absolute**. A proclamation can be struck down if presidential satisfaction is **mala fide**, **perverse**, **irrational**, or based on **wholly extraneous or irrelevant grounds**.
> 2. **Article 74(2) Does Not Shield Objective Material:** While `Article 74(2)` bars courts from probing *what advice* Ministers tendered to the President, **it does NOT bar courts from inspecting the underlying *material*** (Governor's report, official records) on which the advice was formed.
> 3. **Burden Shifts to the Centre:** Once a petitioner establishes a *prima facie* case of illegality or *mala fides*, the **onus lies squarely on the Union Government** to produce relevant material justifying the proclamation.
> 4. **No Dissolution Prior to Parliamentary Approval:** The President has **NO power to dissolve the State Legislative Assembly** until **BOTH Houses of Parliament have approved** the proclamation under `Article 356(3)`. Until parliamentary approval is secured, the President may **ONLY place the Assembly in suspended animation**.
> 5. **Automatic Revival Upon Parliamentary Rejection:** If Parliament rejects or fails to approve the proclamation within two months, the proclamation lapses and the **dismissed State ministry and suspended Assembly automatically stand revived**.
> 6. **Judicial Power to Restore Dismissed Ministries and Dissolved Assemblies:** If a Court holds a proclamation unconstitutional, it possesses full remedial power to **restore the dismissed State Government and revive even a dissolved Legislative Assembly** (if fresh elections have not already intervened).
> 7. **Floor Test Is the Sole Constitutional Test of Majority:** Whether a Chief Minister enjoys the confidence of the House must be tested **exclusively on the floor of the Legislative Assembly**—never through headcounts in the Raj Bhavan lawn, letters of withdrawal, or the Governor's subjective hunch (except in extraordinary conditions of widespread violence preventing a sitting).
> 8. **Change of Guard at the Centre Is No Ground:** A new political party winning a landslide in Lok Sabha elections has **NO constitutional authority** to dismiss State Governments run by rival parties merely on the claim that they have lost popular mandate (expressly overruling the lax approach of *State of Rajasthan*, 1977).
> 9. **Secularism and Federalism Are Basic Structure:** **Federalism** and **Secularism** are integral parts of the **Basic Structure**. A State Government that actively undermines secularism or subverts a basic feature of the Constitution cannot be said to govern *"in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution"*. Applying this test, the Court **UPHELD** the December 1992 dismissals of **Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Himachal Pradesh**, while **INVALIDATING** the proclamations in **Karnataka (1989), Meghalaya (1991), and Nagaland (1988)**!
> 10. **Strict "Last Resort" Standard:** `Article 356` is an exceptional power for genuine **constitutional impasse**—never for ordinary law-and-order glitches, maladministration, corruption allegations, or intra-party squabbles.

### Post-Bommai Jurisprudence: Rameshwar Prasad, Harish Rawat, Nabam Rebia, and In Re: Article 370

Over the three decades since 1994, four landmark constitutional rulings have enforced, refined, and—most recently in 2023—tested the outer boundaries of *S.R. Bommai*:

```cases
# Evolution of Judicial Review Under Article 356 (1977–2023)
State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) | 7-Judge Bench · Judicial Deference | Refused to enter 'political thicket' over dismissal of 9 Congress state govts; Bhagwati J noted narrow mala fide exception even despite 38th Amend bar.
S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (11 Mar 1994) | 9-Judge Bench · Federal Magna Carta | Reviewable despite Art. 74(2); burden on Centre; NO dissolution pre-approval; Floor Test mandatory; secularism = basic structure; courts can revive Assembly (voided KN/ML/NL; upheld MP/RJ/HP).
Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (24 Jan 2006) | 5-Judge Bench (3–2) · Bihar Dissolution | Struck down midnight 23 May 2005 dissolution of hung Bihar Assembly BEFORE its first sitting as mala fide & based on unverified 'horse-trading' ipse dixit of Governor Buta Singh; declined revival only because polls were underway.
Harish Chandra Singh Rawat v. UoI (Apr–May 2016) | Uttarakhand HC & SC · Floor Test Triumph | Uttarakhand HC (CJ K.M. Joseph, 21 Apr 2016) quashed 27 Mar 2016 Art. 356 proclamation imposed 1 day before floor test; SC ordered supervised floor test on 10 May 2016; Rawat proved majority (33–28) → Centre revoked Art. 356 on 11 May 2016.
Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (13 Jul 2016) | 5-Judge Unanimous · Historic Restoration | Quashed Arunachal Governor J.P. Rajkhowa's unconstitutional preponement of Assembly session & messages under Arts. 163/174/175; restored status quo ante (Nabam Tuki Congress govt) — first direct SC restoration post-Art. 356!
In Re: Article 370 (11 Dec 2023) | 5-Judge Unanimous · Art. 356(1)(b) Scope | Held post-proclamation actions under Art. 356 reviewable on 'reasonable nexus' / absence of mala fides; rejected blanket bar on 'irreversible' actions; ruled Parliament under Art. 356(1)(b) can exercise BOTH legislative AND non-legislative powers of State Legislature.
```

#### Deep Dive: *Rameshwar Prasad* (2006), *Harish Rawat* (2016), and *Nabam Rebia* (2016)

- ***Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India* (24 January 2006, 5-Judge Bench, 3–2; operative order 7 October 2005):** Following a hung verdict in the February 2005 Bihar Assembly elections, President's Rule was imposed on 7 March 2005 with the Assembly in suspended animation. On 22 May 2005, Governor Buta Singh sent a report alleging attempts at "horse-trading" to cobble an NDA majority; the Union Cabinet met at 11:00 PM and faxed the recommendation to President A.P.J. Abdul Kalam in Moscow, dissolving the Assembly at midnight **before it had even held its first sitting**. Delivering the majority verdict (CJI Y.K. Sabharwal, Justices B.N. Agrawal and Ashok Bhan), the Court held that while an Assembly can theoretically be dissolved before its first meeting in extreme circumstances, **Governor Buta Singh's report was mere *ipse dixit* (unsupported personal assertion)** designed to shut out a rival political formation, rendering the proclamation **unconstitutional and *mala fide***. The Court also clarified that **Governor's personal immunity under `Article 361` does NOT prevent courts from scrutinising the *mala fides* of his official actions**.
- ***Harish Rawat v. Union of India* (April–May 2016 — Uttarakhand):** After nine Congress MLAs rebelled during the March 2016 Finance Bill vote, President's Rule was proclaimed on **27 March 2016**—barely **24 hours before** Chief Minister Harish Rawat was scheduled to face a floor test! On **21 April 2016**, a Division Bench of the Uttarakhand High Court (Chief Justice K.M. Joseph and Justice V.K. Bist) quashed the proclamation in scathing terms, observing that *"Article 356 is not a magic wand"* to bypass a floor test. On appeal, the Supreme Court initially stayed the High Court order, framed seven sharp constitutional questions, and then **ordered a Court-supervised floor test on 10 May 2016** (keeping the nine disqualified rebel MLAs out of voting). When Rawat won **33–28**, the Union Government itself revoked President's Rule on **11 May 2016**, restoring the elected ministry.
- ***Nabam Rebia & Bamang Felix v. Deputy Speaker* (13 July 2016, 5-Judge Unanimous Bench):** In Arunachal Pradesh, Governor J.P. Rajkhowa unilaterally preponed the Assembly session from 14 January 2016 to 16 December 2015 without ministerial advice and dictated its agenda (leading rebel MLAs to meet in a community hall, followed by President's Rule on **26 January 2016** while the dispute was *sub judice*!). A five-judge Constitution Bench (Justices J.S. Khehar, Dipak Misra, M.B. Lokur, P.C. Ghose, and N.V. Ramana) ruled that `Article 163` gives the Governor **no general discretion to interfere in Assembly scheduling or Tenth Schedule disqualifications**, quashed the Governor's orders, and **turned the clock back by restoring the dismissed Congress government**—the most dramatic real-world execution of *Bommai*'s restoration doctrine!

> [!case]
> ***In Re: Article 370 of the Constitution* (11 December 2023, 5-Judge Unanimous Bench) — A Pivotal Shift in `Article 356` Jurisprudence:**
> Examining the August 2019 abrogation of Jammu & Kashmir's special status (`CO 272` and `CO 273`) and its bifurcation into two Union Territories under the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019—all executed while J&K was under **President's Rule (`Article 356`, proclaimed 19 December 2018)**—the Constitution Bench (CJI D.Y. Chandrachud, Justices S.K. Kaul, Sanjiv Khanna, B.R. Gavai, and Surya Kant) laid down four far-reaching propositions on `Article 356`:
> 1. **Standard for Post-Proclamation Actions:** Actions taken by the President or Parliament *after* a valid `Article 356` proclamation are **subject to judicial review** and must bear a **reasonable/proximate nexus** with the object of the proclamation; the challenger must establish a *prima facie* case of ***mala fides*, extraneous purpose, or palpable irrationality**, after which the onus shifts to the Union.
> 2. **Rejection of a Blanket "Irreversibility" Bar:** Rejecting petitioners' plea that an emergency custodian under `Article 356` can take only temporary, reversible actions, the Court held that **neither `Article 356` nor `Article 357(2)` prohibits "irreversible" executive or legislative acts**—after all, everyday governance and `Article 357(2)` statutes routinely create enduring consequences, and even *Bommai* permitted Assembly dissolution post-parliamentary approval.
> 3. **Both Legislative and Non-Legislative Powers Under `Article 356(1)(b)`:** Reading the plain text of `Article 356(1)(b)` (*"the powers of the Legislature of the State shall be exercisable by or under the authority of Parliament"*), the Court held that Parliament's substitute authority is **NOT restricted to ordinary law-making**—it encompasses **non-legislative constitutional functions** of a State Legislature too, including expressing "views" under the proviso to **`Article 3`**!
> 4. **Proviso to `Article 3` Suspended:** Because the December 2018 Proclamation had validly suspended the proviso to `Article 3` (which requires referring a state-reorganisation bill to the State Legislature), Parliament's consent sufficed—though the Court **declined to rule** on the deeper question of whether Parliament can permanently extinguish a State into a Union Territory under `Article 3`, noting the Solicitor General's assurance that J&K's statehood would be restored at the earliest.

> [!debate]
> **Constitutional Tension After *In Re: Article 370* (2023) — Federalism vs Plenary Emergency Powers:**
> - **Supporters of the Verdict** argue that reading an unstated "irreversibility bar" into `Article 356` would paralyse governance during prolonged President's Rule (blocking budgets, treaties, land acquisitions, and statutory reforms) and contradict `Article 357(2)`'s express rule that emergency laws endure after revocation.
> - **Constitutional Critics** warn that allowing Parliament during President's Rule to substitute for a State Legislature under `Article 3`—when the State's elected representatives are absent—creates a dangerous structural precedent whereby an assertive Union majority could theoretically downgrade or redraw *any* State during a temporary spell of central rule without consulting its people, eroding the spirit of *S.R. Bommai* (1994).

### Empirical Record, Sarkaria–Punchhi Reforms, and Manipur President's Rule (2025–2026)

#### Proper vs Improper Situations for Invoking Article 356 (Sarkaria Commission & *Bommai*)

In its landmark **1988 Report**, the **Sarkaria Commission on Centre–State Relations**—whose operational guidelines were explicitly endorsed by Justice B.P. Jeevan Reddy in *S.R. Bommai* (1994)—classified concrete scenarios where invoking `Article 356` is constitutionally **proper** versus **improper**:

| Constitutionally PROPER Situations (`Art. 356` Justified) | Constitutionally IMPROPER Situations (`Art. 356` Unconstitutional) |
|---|---|
| **Hung Assembly:** No party or coalition can form a stable ministry after general elections despite genuine exploration of alternatives. | **Refusal to Allow Floor Test:** Ministry resigns or faces defections, and Governor recommends `Art. 356` without exploring alternative claimants or allowing a floor test. |
| **Refusal to Form Government:** Majority party declines to form a ministry and Governor cannot assemble a viable coalition. | **Purely Subjective Majority Assessment:** Governor judges loss of majority in Raj Bhavan rather than on the Assembly floor. |
| **Resignation With No Alternative:** Ministry resigns after defeat on the floor and no alternative party is willing or able to command a majority. | **Lok Sabha Election Rout:** Ruling party in the State suffers heavy defeat in Parliamentary elections while retaining an Assembly majority (*Bommai* rule). |
| **Defiance of Constitutional Directions (`Art. 365`):** State persistently disregards lawful Union executive directions despite formal warning. | **Ordinary Internal Disturbance / Law & Order:** Serious law-and-order trouble not amounting to internal subversion or complete constitutional breakdown. |
| **Internal Subversion / Physical Breakdown:** State government deliberately subverts the Constitution (e.g., undermining secularism, basic structure) or natural disaster/insurgency makes governance impossible. | **Maladministration, Corruption, or Intra-Party Squabbles:** Financial stringency, administrative inefficiency, corruption allegations, or factional rivalry within the ruling party; invoking `Art. 356` **without prior warning** (except in extreme urgency). |

#### Key Recommendations of Sarkaria (1988), NCRWC (2002), and Punchhi (2010) Commissions

- **Sarkaria Commission (1988):** Use `Article 356` strictly as a **"measure of last resort"**; issue a **prior warning** giving the State an opportunity to rectify defaults; require the Governor to send a **"speaking report"** disclosing objective material facts (made part of the proclamation laid before Parliament); **never dissolve the Assembly before parliamentary approval**; appoint eminent, non-partisan Governors in consultation with the Chief Minister.
- **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution — NCRWC (Venkatachaliah Commission, 2002):** Endorsed Sarkaria and *Bommai*; recommended amending `Article 356` to require a **prior warning** and to specify clearly that the Assembly cannot be dissolved before both Houses approve; urged ensuring `Article 356` is not revoked merely to avoid a floor defeat.
- **Punchhi Commission (2010):** Recommended **codifying the *S.R. Bommai* guidelines directly into `Articles 355` and `356`**; introduced the innovative proposal of a **"Localised Emergency"** (empowering the Union to take over administration of a **specific troubled district or area** for a strictly limited period—such as 3 months—without dismissing the elected State Government or dissolving the entire Assembly!); and recommended fixed 5-year tenure and impeachment procedure for Governors to insulate them from partisan pressure.

#### Statistical Footprint of President's Rule (1950–2026)

Far from remaining Dr. Ambedkar's "dead letter", central rule has been imposed **more than 130 times—reaching 135 instances across States and Union Territories** (as of 2026, following Manipur's February 2025 proclamation):
- **First Invocation:** **Punjab (20 June 1951 – 17 April 1952)**, triggered by infighting within the ruling Congress party (Gopi Chand Bhargava ministry)—followed by PEPSU in 1953 and Andhra in 1954.
- **Most Frequent Invocations:** **Manipur (`11` times**, surpassing Uttar Pradesh after February 2025), followed by **Uttar Pradesh (`10` times)**, and **Jammu & Kashmir (`9` times)** and **Punjab (`9` times, including PEPSU / 8 as post-reorganisation State)**.
- **Longest Cumulative Duration:** **Jammu & Kashmir** (over **12–15 years** combined across Governor's Rule, `Art. 356`, and `Sec. 73` UT rule—including the 2018–2024 six-year spell revoked on **13 October 2024**), followed by **Punjab** (over 10 years, ~3,878 days), **Puducherry** (over 7 years, ~2,739 days), and **Manipur** (over 6.5 years cumulatively).
- **States Never Placed Under President's Rule:** **Chhattisgarh** and **Telangana** are the **only two Indian States** that have **NEVER** experienced President's Rule since their formation! *(Classic Prelims trivia!)*
- **Post-*Bommai* Decline:** Between 1967 and 1993, `Article 356` averaged over 3 invocations per year (peaking at **14 impositions in 1977** alone and over 50 spells under Indira Gandhi's prime ministership); since *S.R. Bommai* (1994), its frequency dropped sharply to roughly once every 1–2 years, largely confined to genuine hung assemblies or acute security breakdowns.

> [!news]
> **Manipur President's Rule (13 February 2025 – 4 February 2026) — Textbook Case Study of Articles 356(1), 356(2), 356(4), and 356(5):**
> - **Trigger & Imposition:** Amid nearly two years of ethnic conflict between the Meitei and Kuki-Zo communities (since May 2023) and the withdrawal of support by the National People's Party (NPP) in November 2024, Chief Minister **N. Biren Singh** resigned on **9 February 2025**. After Governor **Ajay Kumar Bhalla** reported that no political party was in a position to form a government, President **Droupadi Murmu** imposed President's Rule under **`Article 356(1)`** on **13 February 2025** (`G.S.R. 134(E)`)—marking **Manipur's 11th spell** (the highest for any Indian State!). Faithfully following *S.R. Bommai*, the 60-member Manipur Legislative Assembly (whose tenure runs until March 2027) was placed in **suspended animation**, *not* dissolved.
> - **Two-Month Parliamentary Ratification (`Art. 356(3)`):** Both Houses adopted statutory resolutions approving the proclamation within the two-month deadline—**Lok Sabha on 3 April 2025** and **Rajya Sabha on 4 April 2025** (in late-night extended sittings, moved by Union Home Minister Amit Shah)—and Parliament also passed Manipur's State Budget under `Article 357`.
> - **Six-Month Extension (`Art. 356(4)`):** Parliament approved a further six-month extension with effect from **13 August 2025** (adopted by **Lok Sabha on 30 July 2025** and **Rajya Sabha on 5 August 2025**).
> - **Strategic Revocation Before the One-Year Wall (`Art. 356(2)` & `356(5)`):** Because extending President's Rule past **12 February 2026** (one year) was constitutionally barred by **`Article 356(5)`** (no `Article 352` National Emergency was in force, so extension would have required a full Constitutional Amendment!), President Droupadi Murmu issued Gazette Notification `S.O. 509(E)` under **`Article 356(2)`** revoking President's Rule on **4 February 2026**—nine days shy of the one-year deadline! Because the Assembly had remained in suspended animation, it revived automatically without fresh elections, and NDA legislature party leader **Yumnam Khemchand Singh** (BJP) was sworn in as Chief Minister the same evening alongside two Deputy Chief Ministers—**Nemcha Kipgen** (Kuki-Zo, BJP, Manipur's first woman Deputy CM) and **Losii Dikho** (Naga, NPF).
> - **Companion 2024 Milestone:** Earlier, on **13 October 2024**, the Union Government had revoked the nearly five-year UT central rule in **Jammu & Kashmir** (operating since 31 October 2019 under `Section 73` of the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019), enabling Omar Abdullah's elected government to take office following Assembly elections.

> 2. **Delegation Rule:** During `Art. 352`, **Parliament alone** legislates on State List subjects; during `Art. 356`, Parliament can **delegate law-making to the President** (`President's Acts`).

## 4. Financial Emergency (Article 360)

Completing the triad of `Part XVIII`, **`Article 360`** equips the Union with sweeping macroeconomic and fiscal command authority to avert sovereign default or systemic financial collapse. Although India has never invoked it, `Article 360` contains two of the sharpest structural traps in all of UPSC Prelims—its **indefinite duration without periodic renewal** and its **unprecedented authority to cut the salaries of Supreme Court and High Court judges**.

### Grounds of Proclamation and Judicial Review

Under **`Article 360(1)`**, if the President is satisfied that a situation has arisen whereby the **financial stability or credit of India or of any part of the territory thereof is threatened**, the President may by a Proclamation make a declaration of **Financial Emergency**.

The trajectory of judicial review under `Article 360` mirrors `Articles 352` and `356`:
- The **`38th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975`** inserted `Article 360(5)` making the President's satisfaction **"final and conclusive"** and barring courts from questioning a Financial Emergency proclamation on any ground.
- The **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`** **deleted `Article 360(5)`**, restoring judicial review. Consequently, a proclamation of Financial Emergency is justiciable on the same limited constitutional grounds—*mala fides*, perversity, or reliance on wholly extraneous considerations.

Notice also that, unlike `Article 352(3)`, `Article 360` does **not** require a special written Cabinet communication clause—it operates on ordinary aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers under `Article 74`.

### Parliamentary Approval, Indefinite Duration, and Revocation

Pay razor-sharp attention to the procedural mechanics of **`Article 360(2)`**, because examiners routinely bait candidates into assuming it copies either `Article 352` or `Article 356`:

1. **Approval Window and Majority (`Article 360(2)(c)`):** A Proclamation of Financial Emergency must be approved by **both Houses of Parliament separately** within **TWO MONTHS** from its date of issue by a **SIMPLE MAJORITY** (majority of members present and voting in each House).
2. **Lok Sabha Dissolution Safeguard (`Proviso` to `Article 360(2)`):** If the Lok Sabha is dissolved at the time of proclamation or dissolves during the two-month period without approving it, the proclamation survives until **30 days from the first sitting of the newly reconstituted Lok Sabha**, **PROVIDED the Rajya Sabha has approved it in the meantime**.
3. **INDEFINITE DURATION — NO MAXIMUM PERIOD AND NO RENEWAL (`Unique Prelims Goldmine!`):** Once a Proclamation of Financial Emergency is approved by both Houses of Parliament, **it continues in force INDEFINITELY until it is revoked by the President!** Two astonishing consequences follow:
   - There is **NO maximum time limit** prescribed for its operation (unlike `Article 356`'s 3-year cap); **AND**
   - **Repeated six-monthly parliamentary approval is NOT required** for its continuation (unlike **BOTH** `Article 352` and `Article 356`, both of which demand fresh parliamentary renewal every six months!).
4. **Revocation (`Article 360(2)(a)`):** The President may revoke a Financial Emergency **at any time** by issuing a subsequent Proclamation. **Parliamentary approval is NOT required** for revocation, nor is there any Lok Sabha disapproval resolution clause.

### Far-Reaching Constitutional and Fiscal Consequences

While a Financial Emergency operates, `Articles 360(3)` and `360(4)` subordinate the entire fiscal apparatus of the States—and even the pay protections of India's highest constitutional judiciary—to Union direction:

1. **Extension of Union Executive Authority Over State Finances (`Article 360(3)`):** The executive authority of the Union extends to giving directions to **any State** to observe such **canons of financial propriety** as may be specified in the directions, as well as any **other directions** the President deems necessary and adequate for maintaining financial stability and credit.
2. **Reduction of Salaries and Allowances of State Personnel (`Article 360(4)(a)(i)`):** Such Union directions may specifically mandate a **reduction of salaries and allowances of ALL or any class of persons serving in connection with the affairs of a State**.
3. **Reservation of State Money Bills and Financial Bills (`Article 360(4)(a)(ii)`):** Union directions may require **ALL Money Bills or other Financial Bills** (under `Article 207`) passed by a State Legislature to be **reserved for the consideration of the President**—handing New Delhi an absolute fiscal veto over every rupee of State taxation and expenditure.
4. **Reduction of Salaries of Union Servants AND Supreme Court / High Court Judges (`Article 360(4)(b)`):** The President becomes directly competent to issue directions for the **reduction of salaries and allowances of**:
   - **ALL or any class of persons serving in connection with the affairs of the Union**, **AND**
   - **the Judges of the Supreme Court and the High Courts!**

In normal times, the salaries and allowances of Supreme Court judges (`Article 125(2)`) and High Court judges (`Article 221(2)`) are constitutionally **charged on the Consolidated Fund** and **cannot be varied to their disadvantage after appointment**—a bedrock pillar of judicial independence. **`Article 360(4)(b)` is the SOLE constitutional exception to that protection** (as expressly noted in the text of `Articles 125(2)` and `221(2)` themselves!).

During the Constituent Assembly debates, **Pandit Hriday Nath Kunzru** fiercely criticised `Article 360` as *"a serious threat to the financial autonomy of the States"*, warning that it reduced States to fiscal vassals. Dr. Ambedkar replied that in a national sovereign credit crisis—like the 1930s Great Depression—fiscal discipline could not succeed if States pursued divergent borrowing and spending paths, ensuring that the Union could enforce coordinated austerity without triggering federal litigation.

> [!prelims]
> **Four High-Yield Prelims Traps on Financial Emergency (`Article 360`):**
> 1. **NO Six-Month Renewal!** Once approved by both Houses (within **2 months**, **Simple Majority**), `Article 360` runs **indefinitely** with **NO repeated parliamentary approval** and **NO maximum cap**.
> 2. **Sole Exception to Judicial Pay Protection:** `Article 360(4)(b)` is the **ONLY** provision in the Constitution permitting reduction of salaries and allowances of **Supreme Court and High Court judges** during their tenure! *(Remember: `Art. 360` does **NOT** mention reducing the President's or Governor's emoluments—the text covers persons serving Union/State affairs plus SC & HC judges.)*
> 3. **Fundamental Rights & State Ministries Safe:** `Article 360` does **NOT** suspend Fundamental Rights, does **NOT** dismiss State Governments, and does **NOT** empower Parliament to legislate directly on State List subjects (instead, it uses financial directions and **reservation of State Money/Financial Bills** for the President).
> 4. **Never Invoked:** India has **NEVER** proclaimed a Financial Emergency since 1950—**not even during the 1991 Balance of Payments crisis** or the **2020 COVID-19 macroeconomic contraction**!

### Why Article 360 Has Never Been Proclaimed: The 1991 Crisis and Contemporary Relevance

India came closest to the brink of `Article 360` in **mid-1991**, when foreign exchange reserves plummeted to barely **$1.2 billion** (enough to finance less than three weeks of essential imports), fiscal deficits crossed 8% of GDP, and the government had to airlift physical gold reserves to the Bank of England and Union Bank of Switzerland as collateral for emergency liquidity. Even in that acute sovereign-credit emergency—and again during the **2020 COVID-19 pandemic**, when GDP contracted sharply and public petitions unsuccessfully urged the Supreme Court to direct a Financial Emergency (*Centre for Accountability and Systemic Change v. Union of India*, April 2020, dismissed by the SC)—New Delhi **deliberately avoided `Article 360`**.

> [!mains]
> **Mains Insight — Why India Avoided Article 360 in 1991 & 2020 (and Why It Remains a Deterrent Reserve Power):**
> 1. **Market Signalling Risk:** Formal invocation of a "Financial Emergency" signals sovereign insolvency to global credit-rating agencies, foreign institutional investors, and multilateral lenders, triggering capital flight and raising borrowing costs far more severely than quiet crisis management.
> 2. **Sufficiency of Ordinary Fiscal & Statutory Tools:** India already possesses robust peacetime central fiscal levers without triggering `Part XVIII`—namely **`Article 293(3)` borrowing ceilings** on indebted States, the **FRBM Act, 2003**, **RBI monetary and liquidity instruments**, the **Disaster Management Act, 2005** (used in 2020 alongside temporary MP/ministerial salary cuts enacted by ordinary legislation), and cooperative **GST Council** coordination.
> 3. **Political & Federal Cost:** Reserving every State Money Bill and cutting salaries of judges and state employees nationwide would trigger severe federal friction and erode institutional morale. Yet `Article 360` endures as a constitutional "nuclear deterrent" against extreme sovereign default or sub-national debt contagion.

## 5. Critical Assessment, Constituent Assembly Debates, and Mains Perspectives

Few parts of the Indian Constitution were contested with greater moral urgency in the Constituent Assembly—or tested more severely across seven decades of republican history—than **`Part XVIII`**. Evaluating India's Emergency Provisions for UPSC Mains demands moving beyond simplistic binaries ("totalitarian menace" versus "necessary shield") to trace how constitutional text, political practice, parliamentary self-correction, and judicial review have interacted over time.

### The Constituent Assembly Divide: Totalitarian Fear vs Constitutional Life-Breath

When the Draft Emergency Articles came up for debate in August and October 1949, critics across the ideological spectrum voiced five fundamental apprehensions: **(1)** that the federal character of the Constitution would be destroyed and the Union made all-powerful; **(2)** that all powers of both Union and States would concentrate entirely in the Union executive; **(3)** that the President would become a constitutional dictator; **(4)** that the financial autonomy of the States would be nullified; and **(5)** that Fundamental Rights would become meaningless, eroding India's democratic foundations.

#### The Voices of Warning

- **H.V. Kamath:** Delivering the sharpest indictment in the Assembly—and invoking the grim precedent of Weimar Germany's `Article 48`—Kamath warned that `Part XVIII` risked erecting an authoritarian state:

> "I fear that by this single chapter we are seeking to lay the foundation of a totalitarian state, a police state, a state completely opposed to all the ideals and principles that we have held aloft during the last few decades, a State where the rights and liberties of millions of innocent men and women will be in continuous jeopardy, a State where if there be peace, it will be the peace of the grave and the void of the desert... It will be a day of shame and sorrow when the President makes use of these powers having no parallel in any Constitution of the democratic countries of the world." — H.V. Kamath, Constituent Assembly Debates, Vol. IX, August 1949

- **Prof. K.T. Shah:** Condemned the emergency clauses as *"a chapter of reaction and retrogression"*, identifying two troubling currents running throughout Part XVIII: *(a) to arm the Centre with special powers against the units, and (b) to arm the government against the people*, warning that *"the name only of liberty or democracy will remain under the Constitution."*
- **T.T. Krishnamachari:** Despite serving on the Drafting Committee itself, candidly feared that *"by means of these provisions the President and the Executive would be exercising a form of constitutional dictatorship."*
- **Pandit H.N. Kunzru:** Criticised both the over-centralisation of `Article 356` (warning against making the Centre a routine arbiter of provincial politics) and `Article 360`, declaring that Financial Emergency posed *"a serious threat to the financial autonomy of the States."*

#### The Voices of Defence

In reply, the architects of the Constitution defended `Part XVIII` as an indispensable protective armour for a newly independent, deeply diverse, partition-scarred republic:

- **Sir Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar:** Described the Emergency Provisions as **"the very life-breath of the Constitution"**, arguing that preserving the nation's territorial integrity and democratic order during war or constitutional breakdown was the precondition for every other constitutional right.
- **Mahavir Tyagi:** Characterised the chapter as a vital **"safety-valve"** designed to preserve the constitutional edifice by allowing it to bend temporarily during a storm rather than shatter permanently.
- **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar:** Frankly acknowledged that no humanly drafted power is immune from misuse (*"I do not altogether deny that there is a possibility of these articles being abused or employed for political purposes"*), yet insisted that the proper safeguard lay in **constitutional morality**, prior presidential warnings, parliamentary accountability, and an alert electorate—expressing his celebrated hope that `Articles 355` and `356` would **remain a "dead letter"**.

### From Ambedkar's Dead Letter Hope to 135 Invocations: Phases of Constitutional Practice

History vindicated **both** the critics and the defenders—in sequence. On one hand, `Article 352` enabled swift national mobilisation during the **1962 Chinese invasion** and the **1971 Bangladesh Liberation War**, while `Article 360` has never once been abused. On the other hand, the **1975–77 Internal Emergency** fulfilled H.V. Kamath's darkest prophecy almost to the letter, and `Article 356` became anything but a "dead letter"—invoked **135 times** across States and Union Territories since **Punjab (20 June 1951)**, reaching **11 spells in Manipur**, **10 in Uttar Pradesh**, and **9 each in Punjab and Jammu & Kashmir**, leaving only **Chhattisgarh and Telangana** untouched.


### How Constitutional Self-Correction Curbed Misuse: The 44th Amendment, Bommai, and Federal Pluralism

The defining story of `Part XVIII` is **institutional resilience**. Rather than repealing emergency powers—which could leave the Republic defenceless against war or insurrection—India's constitutional ecosystem erected **four interlocking guardrails** that transformed practice dramatically:

1. **Structural Constitutional Engineering (`44th Amendment, 1978`):** By installing the seven locks on `Article 352` and the one-year wall on `Article 356(5)`, Parliament raised the political and procedural price of emergency abuse so steeply that `Article 352` has **not been invoked once in nearly fifty years**, and even the recent Manipur spell (2025–2026) had to be revoked nine days before the one-year wall!
2. **Assertive Judicial Review (*Minerva Mills*, *S.R. Bommai*, *Rameshwar Prasad*, *Harish Rawat*, *Nabam Rebia*, *Puttaswamy*):** Courts systematically dismantled executive immunity, forced objective material disclosure despite `Article 74(2)`, made the **Floor Test** non-negotiable, demonstrated real-world willingness to **revive dismissed ministries**, and buried *ADM Jabalpur* permanently.
3. **Rise of Regional Parties and Federal Pluralism:** Since the 1990s, the emergence of powerful regional parties, split control between Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha (making `Article 356(3)` and `Article 352(6)` approval in the Upper House a genuine veto point!), and vigilant state-level civil society have turned partisan dismissal into a high-risk political gamble.
4. **Presidential Vigilance:** Presidents themselves have exercised quiet constitutional statesmanship—most famously **President K.R. Narayanan**, who returned Union Cabinet recommendations for President's Rule in **Uttar Pradesh (October 1997)** and **Bihar (September 1998)** for reconsideration under the `Article 74(1)` proviso, prompting the Union Cabinet to drop both proposals!

### Persistent Fault Lines, Punchhi's Localised Emergency, and Mains Way Forward

Despite this hard-won progress, four subtle contemporary vulnerabilities remain high-priority UPSC Mains themes:

1. **Governor's Role and Floor-Test Friction:** Even when `Article 356` is not invoked, partisan gubernatorial manoeuvres—delaying invitations to form governments, withholding assent to State Bills (`Articles 200–201`), or timing floor tests—threaten cooperative federalism.
2. **Post-*In Re: Article 370* Latitude Under `Article 356(1)(b)`:** Permitting Parliament during President's Rule to substitute for an absent State Legislature in giving non-legislative `Article 3` "views" and undertaking irreversible territorial reorganisation leaves an unresolved federal vulnerability if misused in future.
3. **Undeclared Fiscal and Security Centralisation:** Critics note that peacetime instruments—from `Article 293(3)` borrowing curbs and central agency investigations to prolonged internet shutdowns and preventive detention statutes—can occasionally achieve centralising effects without formally invoking `Part XVIII`.
4. **The All-or-Nothing Rigidity of `Article 356`:** When severe ethnic violence or insurgency afflicts only a few districts of a State, the Constitution currently offers no intermediate emergency tool between routine law-and-order support (`Art. 355`) and dismissing the entire elected State Government (`Art. 356`).

> [!debate]
> **Evaluating the Punchhi Commission's "Localised Emergency" Proposal (2010):**
> - **Case FOR Localised Emergency:** Amending `Articles 355–356` to allow the Union to assume limited administrative and security control over a **specific disturbed district or region** (for a strict cap, e.g., 3 months) preserves democratic self-rule in peaceful parts of the State, prevents wholesale dismissal of an elected ministry, and addresses situations like ethnic flare-ups or border insurgencies proportionately.
> - **Case AGAINST Localised Emergency:** State governments and federalists fear that lowering the political threshold for Union intervention could **normalise frequent central takeovers** of sensitive districts (such as opposition strongholds or state capitals), creating dual lines of command inside a single State bureaucracy and weakening the accountability of State Police (`List II, Entry 2`).

## 1. Rationale and Constitutional Architecture of Part XVIII (Articles 352–360)

- **Core Purpose (`Part XVIII`, `Arts. 352–360`):** Protects sovereignty, unity, integrity, security, and democratic order by converting India's federation into a **unitary system WITHOUT any formal `Art. 368` amendment** — constitutional elasticity defended by **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar (4 Nov 1948)** against rigid US federalism.
- **Borrowing Split (Classic Prelims Trap):** Administrative/federal takeover = **GoI Act, 1935** (`Sec. 102` → `Art. 352`/`250`; `Sec. 93` → `Art. 356`; `Sec. 45` Centre breakdown omitted). **FR suspension (`Arts. 358–359`)** = **Weimar Constitution of Germany (`Art. 48`)**. `Art. 360` drew on US NRA Great Depression lesson & GoI 1935 `Sec. 12(1)(b)`.
- **Three Regimes & Two Pillars:** **`Art. 352`** (*Proclamation of Emergency*); **`Art. 356`** (*Failure of constitutional machinery in States* — **Constitution NEVER uses "Emergency" for `Art. 356`!**); **`Art. 360`** (*Financial Emergency*). Support pillars: **`Art. 355`** (**STILL retains "internal disturbance"**!) and **`Art. 365`** (**Part XIX Miscellaneous**, NOT Part XVIII!).

## 2. National Emergency (Article 352)

- **Grounds (`Art. 352(1)`):** War or external aggression (**External Emergency**) OR **armed rebellion** (**Internal Emergency** — **`44th Amend, 1978`** replaced vague **"internal disturbance"**). Valid on **imminent danger** (`Explanation`); multiple simultaneous proclamations on distinct grounds allowed (`Art. 352(9)` — `38th Amend`).
- **Written Cabinet Advice (`Art. 352(3)`):** Inserted by **`44th Amend`** post-June 1975 unilateral PM advice. President **cannot** issue/vary `Art. 352` without **written decision of Union Cabinet** (PM + Cabinet-rank Ministers under `Art. 75`) — **SOLE occurrence & definition of "Cabinet" in the Constitution!**
- **Scope & Judicial Review:** **`42nd Amend (1976)`** allowed whole OR **specified part of India**. **`38th Amend (1975)`** barred judicial review → **`44th Amend`** deleted bar → ***Minerva Mills* (`31 Jul 1980`, 5-Judge)** held reviewable for *mala fides* or extraneous grounds.
- **Approval & Duration (`Arts. 352(4)–(6)`):** **Both Houses separately** within **1 month** (was 2 mos) by **Special Majority** (>50% total + 2/3rd present & voting; was simple; **NO Joint Sitting**). Runs **6 months** at a time (from 2nd resolution date), renewable **indefinitely** every 6 months by Special Majority. **LS Dissolved Safeguard:** survives **30 days from new LS's 1st sitting**, **PROVIDED Rajya Sabha approved**.
- **Revocation (`Arts. 352(2), (7), (8)`):** **President alone** anytime (no parliamentary approval or `352(3)` lock) **OR mandatory** if **Lok Sabha ONLY** passes disapproval by **SIMPLE Majority** (**RS has ZERO role!**). Written notice by **`1/10th` of LS total members** to **Speaker** (in session) or **President** (recess) compels a **special sitting within 14 days**.
- **Federal & Legislative Effects:**
  - **Executive (`Art. 353(a)`):** Union directs States on **any matter**; **State ministries NOT dismissed**.
  - **Legislative (`Arts. 353(b), 250, 251`):** Parliament legislates on **State List** (President issues ordinances in recess); **State Assemblies NOT suspended**; laws **EXPIRE 6 months** post-emergency (`Art. 250(2)`). `42nd Amend` `Art. 353` proviso: partial emergency extends reach to *other States* if security threatened.
  - **Financial (`Art. 354`):** President modifies revenue transfers (`Arts. 268–279`); laid before Parliament; ends by **close of financial year** in which emergency ceases.
  - **Tenures (`Arts. 83(2), 172(1)`):** Parliament extends LS/State Assembly terms **1 yr at a time**, max **6 months** post-emergency (5th LS extended twice 1976–77; `42nd` raised term 5→6 yrs, `44th` restored 5 yrs).
- **Fundamental Rights (`Art. 358` vs `Art. 359`):**
  - **`Art. 358`:** **Auto-suspends `Art. 19` itself** nationwide **ONLY in External Emergency** (war/external aggression — **NEVER armed rebellion**, `44th Amend`).
  - **`Art. 359`:** Needs **separate Presidential Order** (laid before Parliament, `359(3)`); suspends **court enforcement** of named FRs in **both External & Internal** emergencies (whole/part, full/shorter term) — **NEVER `Arts. 20 & 21`** (`44th Amend`). (`Art. 19` enforcement *can* still be paused in armed rebellion via `Art. 359(1)`!)
  - **Recital Lock (`Arts. 358(2), 359(1B)`):** Shields **ONLY laws reciting emergency nexus** & executive action under them.
  - **Cases:** ***Makhan Singh* (`2 Sep 1963`, 7-Judge)** preserved *ultra vires*/*mala fide* statutory review; ***Mohd. Yaqub* (1967)** (`Art. 359` Order not `Art. 13(2)` law); ***ADM Jabalpur* (`28 Apr 1976`, 4–1)** denied *habeas corpus* over **Justice H.R. Khanna's** lone dissent → **overruled by *K.S. Puttaswamy* (`24 Aug 2017`, 9-Judge unanimous)**.
- **Three Emergencies, Shah Commission & News:** (1) **26 Oct 1962 – 10 Jan 1968** (China; covered 1965 war); (2) **3 Dec 1971 – 27 Mar 1977** (Pakistan); (3) **25 Jun 1975 – 21 Mar 1977** ('internal disturbance', concurrent with 1971) → **Justice J.C. Shah Commission** (est. `28 May 1977`, 3 reports Mar–Aug 1978) → `44th Amend` (never invoked since 1977). **News (`11 Jul 2024`):** MHA declared **25 June** ***Samvidhaan Hatya Diwas*** (50th anniversary Jun 2024 – Jun 2026).
- **Art. 352 vs Martial Law (`Art. 34`):** Martial Law is **undefined**, suspends **civil govt & ordinary courts**, affects **FRs only** (**Act of Indemnity**), localised area only; `Art. 352` is codified, keeps courts/ministries functioning, alters federalism + FRs.

## 3. President's Rule and Failure of Constitutional Machinery in States (Articles 355, 356, and 365)

- **Union Duty (`Art. 355`):** Protect States against external aggression, **internal disturbance**, and constitutional breakdown (*Sarbananda Sonowal*, 2005).
- **Twin Triggers:** **`Art. 356(1)`** — **Governor's report OR "otherwise"** (Kamath opposed 'otherwise'); **`Art. 365`** (Part XIX) — disobeying constitutional Union directions triggers `Art. 356`. Ambedkar (`4 Aug 1949`): hoped it remains a **"dead letter"**.
- **Approval, 1-Year Wall & Revocation (`Arts. 356(2)–(5)`):** Both Houses approve within **2 months** by **Simple Majority** (30-day new LS rule if RS approves; NO Joint Sitting). **6-month** cycles (`42nd` raised to 1 yr, `44th` restored 6 mos), **max 3 years**. **One-Year Wall (`Art. 356(5)`, `44th Amend`):** Beyond 1 yr needs **BOTH**: (1) **`Art. 352` Emergency active** in India/State **AND** (2) **ECI election-difficulty certificate**. Punjab (`1987–92`) needed **`59th` (added `Art. 359A`, repealed by `63rd`, 1989), `64th` (3.5 yrs), `67th` (4 yrs), `68th` (5 yrs)** Amendments (+ `48th`, 1984). **President alone revokes (`Art. 356(2)`)**.
- **Consequences & Prelims 2017 Traps:** **State Council of Ministers dismissed** (only certain consequence); Assembly **suspended** (never dissolved pre-approval per *Bommai*); **local bodies & FRs untouched**; **High Courts 100% insulated (`Proviso` to `Art. 356(1)`)**. Under **`Art. 357`**, Parliament passes budget/laws or delegates to President (**President's Acts**); President authorises spend in LS recess & issues ordinances. **CRITICAL (`Art. 357(2)`):** Laws enacted during President's Rule **SURVIVE indefinitely post-revocation** until repealed/altered by State Legislature!
- **Union Territories (`Art. 356` = States ONLY):** **Delhi** = `Art. 239AB` (`69th Amend`); **Puducherry** = `Sec. 51` GoUT Act 1963; **UT of J&K** = `Sec. 73` J&K Reorg Act 2019 (UT rule `31 Oct 2019 – 13 Oct 2024`; erstwhile State used `Sec. 92` Governor's Rule for 6 mos first).
- ***S.R. Bommai* (`11 Mar 1994`, 9-Judge Bench — 10-Point Ratio):** Replaced *State of Rajasthan* (1977) deference. (1) Reviewable for *mala fides*/perversity/extraneous grounds; (2) `Art. 74(2)` does **NOT shield objective material**; (3) **burden shifts to Centre**; (4) **NO Assembly dissolution before BOTH Houses approve**; (5) Parliamentary rejection auto-revives ministry; (6) **Courts can restore ministry & Assembly**; (7) **Floor Test** sole majority proof; (8) Lok Sabha sweep ≠ dismissing State govts; (9) **Secularism & Federalism = Basic Structure** (**upheld** MP, RJ, HP 1992; **voided** KN 1989, ML 1991, NL 1988); (10) strict **last resort**.
- **Post-*Bommai* Rulings:** ***Rameshwar Prasad* (`24 Jan 2006`, 5-Judge 3–2):** voided Bihar midnight dissolution before 1st sitting (`Art. 361` no shield for *mala fides*). ***Harish Rawat* (Apr–May 2016):** Uttarakhand HC quashed `Art. 356`; SC supervised `10 May` floor test (33–28) → revoked `11 May`. ***Nabam Rebia* (`13 Jul 2016`, 5-Judge):** quashed Arunachal Governor's interference & **restored Nabam Tuki govt**. ***In Re: Article 370* (`11 Dec 2023`, 5-Judge):** post-proclamation actions reviewable on reasonable nexus/*mala fides*; **rejected blanket "irreversibility" bar**; **`Art. 356(1)(b)` covers BOTH legislative AND non-legislative powers** (incl. `Art. 3` views).
- **Commissions, Stats & Manipur 2025–2026:** **Sarkaria (1988)** (last resort, prior warning, speaking report, floor test); **NCRWC (2002)**; **Punchhi (2010)** (codify *Bommai*, **"Localised Emergency"**, 5-yr Governor term). **135 uses** since **Punjab (`20 Jun 1951`)**: highest **Manipur (`11`)**, **UP (`10`)**, **Punjab & J&K (`9` each)**; **NEVER in Chhattisgarh & Telangana**. **Manipur (2025–26):** CM N. Biren Singh resigned `9 Feb 2025` → **`13 Feb 2025`** `Art. 356(1)` (11th spell; Assembly **suspended**) → approved LS `3 Apr` / RS `4 Apr 2025` → extended from `13 Aug 2025` (LS `30 Jul` / RS `5 Aug`) → **revoked `4 Feb 2026`** under `Art. 356(2)` (9 days before `Art. 356(5)` 1-yr wall!) → **Yumnam Khemchand Singh** sworn in as CM.

## 4. Financial Emergency (Article 360)

- **Grounds & Review (`Art. 360(1)`):** Threat to **financial stability or credit of India or any part**. `38th Amend` bar deleted by **`44th Amend`** (justiciable); ordinary `Art. 74` advice.
- **Approval, Indefinite Duration & Revocation (`Art. 360(2)`):** Both Houses within **2 months** by **Simple Majority** (30-day new LS rule if RS approves). **PRELIMS TRAP:** Once approved, runs **INDEFINITELY** — **NO maximum cap & NO 6-month renewal!** Revoked by **President alone** anytime.
- **Consequences (`Arts. 360(3)–(4)`):** Union **canons of financial propriety**; salary cuts for **State staff**; **reservation of ALL State Money/Financial Bills** for President; salary cuts for **Union staff AND Supreme Court & High Court Judges (`Art. 360(4)(b)` — SOLE exception to `Arts. 125 & 221`!)**. FRs & State ministries safe. **NEVER proclaimed** (avoided in 1991 BoP crisis & 2020 pandemic due to market-signalling risks & peacetime `Art. 293(3)`/FRBM/RBI levers).
