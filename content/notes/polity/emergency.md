## 1. The rationale and constitutional design of Part XVIII

Working amid Partition, princely integration, and hostilities in Kashmir, the framers placed **Emergency Provisions** in **Part XVIII (Articles 352 to 360)** to protect India's sovereignty, unity, integrity, security, and democratic order during crises. Unlike rigid federations such as the United States, India's Constitution turns unitary during an emergency **without any formal constitutional amendment**—an elasticity Dr. B.R. Ambedkar underlined in the Constituent Assembly:

> "All federal systems including the American are placed in a tight mould of federalism. No matter what the circumstances, it cannot change its form and shape. It can never be unitary. On the other hand the Draft Constitution can be both unitary as well as federal according to the requirements of time and circumstances. In normal times, it is framed to work as a federal system. But in times of war it is so designed as to make it work as though it was a unitary system." — B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 4 November 1948

Part XVIII's administrative framework draws on the **Government of India Act, 1935** (Sections 102 and 93), whereas **suspending Fundamental Rights during an emergency** comes from Germany's **Weimar Constitution**. Note the exact constitutional labels: **Article 352** is a **"Proclamation of Emergency"** (*National Emergency*); **Article 356** covers **"failure of constitutional machinery in States"** (*President's Rule*—**the Constitution never uses the word "Emergency" for Article 356**); and **Article 360** deals with **"financial emergency"**.

> **Prelims pointer:** Separate the borrowings: the **emergency framework** is from the **GoI Act, 1935**, while **FR suspension** is from the **Weimar Constitution**. Article 356 never uses the word "Emergency".

## 2. National Emergency (Article 352)

### Grounds, Cabinet advice and judicial review

Under **Article 352(1)**, the President may proclaim a National Emergency if the security of India or any part of it is threatened by **war**, **external aggression** (**external emergency**), or **armed rebellion** (**internal emergency**)—even *before* actual occurrence if there is **imminent danger**. After the 1975–77 Emergency abused the original phrase **"internal disturbance"**, the **44th Amendment (1978)** replaced it with **"armed rebellion"**. Earlier, the **42nd Amendment (1976)** allowed a proclamation to operate in only a **specified part of India**, and the **38th Amendment (1975)** allowed simultaneous proclamations on separate grounds.

The 38th Amendment had barred judicial review of presidential satisfaction, but the **44th Amendment (1978)** repealed that bar; in ***Minerva Mills* (1980)**, the Supreme Court held that a proclamation can be struck down if *mala fide* or based on wholly extraneous grounds. Crucially, the 44th Amendment also inserted **Article 352(3)**, barring the President from proclaiming an emergency **without a written recommendation from the Union Cabinet** (the Prime Minister and Cabinet-rank ministers under Article 75)—preventing a repeat of June 1975, when Indira Gandhi advised the President unilaterally and informed her Cabinet the next morning.

> **Prelims pointer:** **Article 352(3)** is the **only place in the entire Constitution where the word "Cabinet" appears** (inserted by the 44th Amendment, 1978; absent in the original 1950 text).

### Parliamentary approval, duration and revocation

Tightened by the **44th Amendment (1978)**, Article 352 imposes strict legislative checks:

- **Approval:** Both Houses must approve **separately within one month** (cut from two months) by a **special majority**—majority of total membership **plus** two-thirds present and voting (raised from simple majority). If Lok Sabha dissolves within that month without approving, it survives until **30 days from the new Lok Sabha's first sitting**, provided **Rajya Sabha** approved it.
- **Duration:** Runs **six months** at a time, extendable **indefinitely** with fresh special-majority approval every six months.
- **Revocation:** The **President** may revoke it anytime without parliamentary approval, **or** revocation becomes **mandatory** if the **Lok Sabha** passes a disapproval resolution by **simple majority**. Written notice by **one-tenth of Lok Sabha members** to the Speaker (or President, if out of session) forces a special sitting within **14 days**. **Rajya Sabha has no role in revocation.**

### Effects on federal relations, legislatures and Fundamental Rights

A National Emergency reshapes governance across five domains:

1. **Executive (Art. 353(a)):** Union executive directions extend to **any matter**; state ministries continue under complete central control.
2. **Legislative (Arts. 353(b), 250, 251):** Parliament legislates on **any State List subject** (and the President may issue ordinances in recess). **State Legislatures are not suspended**, though parliamentary law prevails over repugnant state laws (Art. 251) and **expires six months after the emergency ends**. Under the 42nd Amendment, even a regional emergency extends Union powers to other states if security demands.
3. **Financial (Art. 354):** The President may modify Centre–State revenue transfers; orders are laid before Parliament and end with the financial year in which the emergency ceases.
4. **Legislative tenures (Arts. 83(2), 172(1)):** Parliament may extend the five-year term of the **Lok Sabha** and **State Assemblies** by **one year at a time** (never beyond **six months** post-emergency; used twice for the 5th Lok Sabha in 1976–77).
5. **Fundamental Rights (Arts. 358 and 359):** Under **Article 358**, Article 19 suspends automatically nationwide during an external emergency; under **Article 359**, a Presidential Order pauses court enforcement of named rights during either external or internal emergencies. After ***ADM Jabalpur* (1976)** denied *habeas corpus* over **Justice H.R. Khanna's** lone dissent (overruled in ***K.S. Puttaswamy*, 2017**), the **44th Amendment** made **Articles 20 and 21 permanently non-suspendable**, restricted Article 358 to external emergencies, and protected only laws explicitly reciting an emergency nexus.

| Dimension | Article 358 | Article 359 |
|---|---|---|
| **Rights covered** | **Article 19 only** | **Any FR** named in Order **except Arts. 20 & 21** |
| **Mechanism** | **Automatic** upon proclamation | Needs a **separate Presidential Order** |
| **Effect** | Suspends **Article 19 itself** | Suspends only **court enforcement (remedy)** |
| **Grounds** | **External emergency only** (war / aggression) | **Both external & internal** emergencies |
| **Duration & area** | Whole emergency; **entire country** | Full or **shorter period**; whole or **part** of India |
| **Law immunity** | Laws reciting emergency nexus only (44th Amdt) | Laws reciting emergency nexus only (44th Amdt) |

### Three proclamations and the 44th Amendment watershed

India invoked Article 352 thrice: **26 Oct 1962 – 10 Jan 1968** (Chinese aggression in NEFA, covering the 1965 war too); **3 Dec 1971 – 27 Mar 1977** (Pakistani aggression); and **25 Jun 1975 – 21 Mar 1977** ("internal disturbance", running alongside the 1971 proclamation). Following 1975–77 excesses (MISA detentions, press censorship, 42nd Amendment), the **Shah Commission (1977–78)** exposed abuses and spurred the **44th Amendment (1978)**—raising barriers so high that Article 352 has never been used since 1977.

> **Remember:** Seven **44th Amendment (1978)** curbs on Art. 352: "armed rebellion"; written Cabinet advice (Art. 352(3)); 1-month window; special majority; 6-month renewal; LS simple-majority revocation (1/10th notice → 14-day sitting); Art. 19 external-only, **Arts. 20 & 21 non-suspendable**, judicial review restored.

## 3. President's Rule: Articles 355, 356 and 365

### Constitutional triggers and Ambedkar's "dead letter" hope

**Article 355** mandates the Union to protect every state against **(1) external aggression**, **(2) internal disturbance** (note: **Article 355 still retains "internal disturbance"**!), and **(3) failure to govern in accordance with the Constitution**. Two provisions trigger central rule:

- **Article 356(1):** If the President, on a **Governor's report or "otherwise"**, is satisfied that state government cannot run constitutionally ("otherwise" allows action even without a Governor's report).
- **Article 365:** Failure by a state to comply with Union executive directions lawfully permits the President to hold that constitutional machinery has broken down—invoking Article 356.

In the Constituent Assembly, **H.V. Kamath** objected strongly to the word "otherwise", and members feared a revival of the colonial Section 93. Dr. Ambedkar conceded the risk of abuse but hoped a President would first warn an erring state and order elections before stepping in:

> "In fact I share the sentiments expressed by my honorable Friend Mr. Gupte yesterday that the proper thing we ought to expect is that such articles will never be called into operation and that they would remain a dead letter. If at all they are brought into operation, I hope the President, who is endowed with these powers, will take proper precautions before actually suspending the administration of the provinces." — B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 4 August 1949

### Approval, the one-year wall and consequences

An Article 356 proclamation needs approval by **both Houses within two months** by **simple majority** (30-day rule if Lok Sabha dissolves and Rajya Sabha approves). It runs **six months** at a time up to a **maximum of three years**. Under **Article 356(5)** (**44th Amendment**), **extensions beyond one year require BOTH conditions**: **(1)** a **National Emergency (Art. 352)** is operating in India or in that state/part, **AND (2)** the **Election Commission of India** certifies elections cannot be held due to difficulties. (Punjab's central rule of 1987–92 needed the **59th, 64th, 67th and 68th Amendments** to run that long.) The President may revoke it anytime under **Article 356(2)** without parliamentary approval.

Under President's Rule, the State Council of Ministers is **dismissed**, the **Governor** governs with advisers, and the Assembly is placed in **suspended animation** or **dissolved** (only *after* parliamentary approval post-*Bommai*). Under **Article 357**, Parliament passes the state budget and laws or delegates law-making to the President (**President's Acts**); the President may authorise expenditure when Lok Sabha is out of session and issue ordinances in recess. Crucially, **Article 357(2)** ensures **laws made during President's Rule survive after it ends** until altered or repealed by the State Legislature, while the proviso to **Article 356(1)** leaves **High Court powers untouched**.

> **Prelims pointer:** Prelims 2017 asked what does *not necessarily* follow President's Rule: the Assembly need not be dissolved (it may only be suspended), and local bodies are not touched; only the removal of the Council of Ministers is certain. Three more Art. 356 traps: **(1)** Art. 357 laws **outlive President's Rule** (unlike Art. 352's 6-month expiry); **(2)** **High Courts remain unaffected**; **(3)** **Art. 356 covers States only**—UTs use **Art. 239AB** (Delhi), **Sec. 51, GoUT Act 1963** (Puducherry), and **Sec. 73, J&K Reorganisation Act 2019** (UT of J&K).

### Judicial review: *S.R. Bommai* (1994) and later rulings

Overturning early deference in ***State of Rajasthan* (1977)**, a **nine-judge bench** in ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (11 March 1994)** laid down binding guardrails:

- **Justiciability & burden:** Presidential satisfaction is **reviewable** for *mala fides*, perversity, or extraneous grounds. Article 74(2) bars probing ministerial *advice*, **not** the **objective material** behind it; the **burden shifts to the Centre** to produce relevant material.
- **No premature dissolution & revival:** The Assembly can only be **suspended** (never dissolved) before **both Houses approve**; rejection revives the ministry automatically. Courts can **restore a dismissed ministry and dissolved Assembly**.
- **Floor test & secularism:** Majority must be proved **only on the Assembly floor**; a new Union ruling party cannot automatically dismiss opposition state ministries; **secularism is basic structure** (upholding the 1992 dismissals of MP, Rajasthan, and Himachal Pradesh); ordinary law-and-order glitches do not justify Article 356.

Subsequent benches enforced and refined *Bommai*: ***Rameshwar Prasad* (2006)** voided the 2005 Bihar dissolution as *mala fide*; ***Nabam Rebia* (2016)** **restored** the dismissed Arunachal Pradesh government (mirroring the 2016 Uttarakhand restoration). In ***In Re: Article 370* (11 December 2023)**, a five-judge bench examining J&K's 2019 reorganisation under President's Rule held that Parliament's powers under **Article 356(1)(b)** include non-legislative functions, rejected a blanket bar on **"irreversible" actions**, and tested post-proclamation steps on **reasonableness and absence of *mala fides* or palpable irrationality**—sparking debate over widened central latitude.

### Commissions and historical trajectory

The **Sarkaria Commission (1988)** called for using Article 356 strictly as a **"measure of last resort"** after a prior **warning**, with a **"speaking" Governor's report**, floor tests, and no pre-approval dissolution. The **Punchhi Commission (2010)** urged codifying *Bommai* in the Constitution and introducing a **"localised emergency"** for district-level crises without dismissing the state ministry. Since **Punjab (June 1951)**, President's Rule has been imposed more than **130 times** across states and UTs—most often in **Manipur (11)** and **Uttar Pradesh (10)**, **never in Chhattisgarh or Telangana**—declining sharply post-*Bommai*.

> **In the news:** **Manipur President's Rule (2025–2026)** was imposed on **13 February 2025** after Chief Minister N. Biren Singh resigned amid ethnic violence (Manipur's 11th spell; Assembly in **suspended animation**). Approved by Lok Sabha (**3 April**) and Rajya Sabha (**4 April 2025**) and extended six months from **13 August 2025** (approved 30 July / 5 August), it was revoked by President Droupadi Murmu on **4 February 2026** under **Article 356(2)**—nine days before one year, avoiding Article 356(5)'s dual hurdle—paving the way for an elected government under Chief Minister Yumnam Khemchand Singh.

## 4. Financial Emergency (Article 360)

Under **Article 360(1)**, the President may declare a **Financial Emergency** if the **financial stability or credit of India or any part of it** is threatened; the **44th Amendment** restored judicial review by deleting the 38th Amendment's bar. Approval requires **both Houses within two months** by **simple majority** (30-day rule if Lok Sabha dissolves). **Once approved, it continues indefinitely until revoked by the President—with NO maximum period and NO six-month renewal.**

Its effects allow Union directions to states on **canons of financial propriety**, **salary cuts** for state personnel, **reservation of all state Money Bills and financial bills** for the President, and—under **Article 360(4)(b)**—salary reductions for Union personnel **and Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts**. India has **never invoked Article 360**, not even during the 1991 balance-of-payments crisis or the 2020 pandemic.

> **Prelims pointer:** Two traps in **Art. 360**: **(1)** once approved, it runs **indefinitely with no 6-month renewal**; **(2)** it is the **sole constitutional exception** allowing salary cuts for SC and HC judges (otherwise protected by Arts. 125 & 221).

| **State organs** | **Both continue** under Union control | **Ministry dismissed**; Assembly suspended/dissolved* | **Both continue** under fiscal orders |
| **Parl State-List laws** | **Expire 6 months** post-emergency | **Survive** until repealed/altered by State | No State List takeover (Money Bills reserved) |

## 6. Critical evaluation and Mains perspective

Part XVIII sharply divided the Constituent Assembly: **H.V. Kamath**, **K.T. Shah**, **H.N. Kunzru**, and **T.T. Krishnamachari** feared "constitutional dictatorship" and federal erosion, while **Sir Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar** called emergency powers the **"very life-breath"** of the Constitution in crisis. Both views proved right—1975–77 and partisan Article 356 dismissals showed real vulnerability, yet the **44th Amendment (1978)** and ***S.R. Bommai* (1994)** demonstrated durable constitutional self-correction.

Four reforms would strengthen federalism further: **(1)** codifying *Bommai* and Sarkaria safeguards (prior warning, speaking report, floor test, no pre-approval dissolution) in Article 356; **(2)** appointing non-partisan Governors with fixed tenure after Chief Ministerial consultation; **(3)** operationalising Punchhi's **localised emergency** for district-level crises; and **(4)** establishing conventions that irreversible federal changes under Article 3 await elected state legislatures rather than substitute parliamentary consent.

> **Mains angle:** Anchor GS-2 answers on four pillars: **(1) Constitutional design** (Ambedkar's federal-to-unitary elasticity vs. his "dead letter" warning); **(2) Safeguards** (1975–77 → Shah Commission → 44th Amendment; *Bommai* 1994, *Rameshwar Prasad* 2006, *Nabam Rebia* 2016); **(3) Contemporary tensions** (*In Re: Article 370* Dec 2023 on Art. 356(1)(b); Manipur 2025–26 lifted before the 1-year Art. 356(5) hurdle); and **(4) Way forward** (Sarkaria/Punchhi reforms, neutral Governors, localised emergency).

<!-- distilled -->

- **Part XVIII (Arts. 352–360):** Turns India's federation unitary without amendment (Ambedkar, 4 Nov 1948). Framework from **GoI Act, 1935**; FR suspension from **Weimar Constitution**.
- **Wording trap:** Constitution never uses **"Emergency"** for **Art. 356** ("failure of constitutional machinery in States").
- **Art. 352 grounds:** War, external aggression (external), or **armed rebellion** (internal; replaced "internal disturbance", **44th Amdt 1978**); whole or **part of India** (42nd Amdt); imminent danger suffices.
- **Sole mention of "Cabinet":** **Art. 352(3)** (44th Amdt) requires **written Cabinet advice**—only occurrence of "Cabinet" in the Constitution.
- **Art. 352 approval:** Both Houses within **1 month** by **special majority**; **6-month** cycles, extendable **indefinitely** (30 days for new LS if dissolved and RS approves).
- **Art. 352 revocation:** President alone, **OR mandatory** on **Lok Sabha simple-majority** disapproval (1/10th LS notice → 14-day sitting; **Rajya Sabha has no role**).
- **Art. 352 effects:** Union executive directions on *any* matter; Parliament legislates on State List (Assemblies **not suspended**; laws **expire 6 months** post-emergency); revenue sharing modifiable (Art. 354); LS/Assembly terms extendable **1 yr at a time** (max 6 months post-emergency).
- **Art. 358 vs 359:** Art. 358 **auto-suspends Art. 19** nationwide (**external emergency only**); Art. 359 pauses **court enforcement** of named FRs via Presidential Order (external/internal), **never Arts. 20 & 21** (44th Amdt; *ADM Jabalpur* 1976 overruled by *Puttaswamy* 2017). Both protect only laws reciting emergency nexus.
- **Three National Emergencies:** **1962** (China), **1971** (Pakistan), **1975–77** (internal) → **Shah Commission** → **44th Amdt**; *Minerva Mills* (1980) allowed review for *mala fides*.
- **President's Rule triggers:** **Art. 355** (retains "internal disturbance"), **Art. 356** (Governor's report or **"otherwise"**), **Art. 365** (non-compliance with Union directions). Ambedkar (4 Aug 1949): **"dead letter"** hope.
- **Art. 356 lifecycle:** Both Houses within **2 months** (**simple majority**); 6-month cycles, **max 3 yrs**. Beyond **1 yr** (Art. 356(5)): **Art. 352 in force** AND **ECI certification** both required. President alone revokes (Art. 356(2)).
- **Art. 356 traps:** Ministry dismissed; Art. 357 laws **survive post-revocation** until State repeals/alters; **High Courts untouched**; applies to States only (UTs: Art. 239AB / Sec. 51 GoUT Act / Sec. 73 J&K Reorg Act).
- ***S.R. Bommai* (1994, 9-judge):** Reviewable despite Art. 74(2) (material examinable); burden on Centre; **no dissolution pre-approval**; **floor test** sole proof; **secularism** basic structure; courts can **revive** dismissed ministries.
- **Post-*Bommai* rulings:** ***Rameshwar Prasad* (2006)** (Bihar); ***Nabam Rebia* (2016)** (Arunachal restored); ***In Re: Article 370* (Dec 2023)** (Art. 356(1)(b) covers non-legislative powers; no blanket irreversibility bar; tested for *mala fides*/irrationality).
- **Commissions:** **Sarkaria (1988)** (last resort, warning, speaking report); **Punchhi (2010)** (codify *Bommai*, **"localised emergency"**).
- **Record & Manipur (2025–26):** **130+** uses (first Punjab 1951; Manipur 11, UP 10; never Chhattisgarh/Telangana). **Manipur**: **13 Feb 2025** (suspended animation) → extended 13 Aug 2025 → revoked **4 Feb 2026** (9 days before the 1-yr Art. 356(5) hurdle).
- **Financial Emergency (Art. 360):** Approved in **2 months** (**simple majority**); **indefinite, NO renewal, NO max cap**. Fiscal directions, state Money Bill reservation, **salary cuts for state/Union staff AND SC/HC judges**. **Never declared.**
