## 1. The Governor: Appointment, Tenure and Constitutional Position

The Constitution of India establishes a symmetrical parliamentary architecture at both the Union and State levels. Just as `Part V` constructs the Union Executive around a nominal President and a real Prime Minister heading a Council of Ministers responsible to the Lok Sabha, **`Part VI` (`Articles 153 to 167`)** organises the **State Executive** around four constitutional components: the **Governor**, the **Chief Minister**, the **State Council of Ministers**, and the **Advocate General of the State** (`Article 165`). Unlike the Union, however, there is **no office of Vice-Governor** in the States; if a sudden contingency vacates the office of Governor, the President makes temporary arrangements under `Article 160`—conventionally entrusting the Chief Justice of the State High Court with officiating charge.

At the apex of this constitutional edifice sits the **Governor**, an office engineered by the framers to perform a delicate **dual role**. On the one hand, the Governor is the **chief executive head (constitutional or *de jure* head) of the State**, in whose name all formal executive actions of the State Government are taken, mirroring the President at the Centre. On the other hand, the Governor serves as the **vital constitutional link between the Union and the State**—a sentinel expected to keep the Union informed of constitutional developments in the State, uphold national integrity, and safeguard federal equilibrium during political crises. This dual identity explains why the Governor is simultaneously the most subtle and the most contested constitutional functionary in Indian federalism.

Originally, **`Article 153`** mandated simply that *"there shall be a Governor for each State."* Recognising administrative flexibility during the linguistic reorganisation of India, the **`7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956`** inserted a crucial **proviso to `Article 153`** clarifying that nothing in the Article prevents the **appointment of the same person as Governor for two or more States**. To accompany this common-Governor arrangement, the same `7th Amendment` inserted **`Article 158(3A)`**, directing that when one person serves as Governor for two or more States, the emoluments and allowances payable to the Governor shall be allocated among those States in such proportion as the **President may by order determine**.

# The State Executive & Governor at a Glance
Part VI (Arts. 153–167) | Four Organs, No Vice-Governor | Governor, Chief Minister, State Council of Ministers & Advocate General (`Art. 165`); no Vice-Governor
7th Amendment (1956) | Common Governor Provision | Added proviso to `Art. 153` & `Art. 158(3A)`: same person can govern 2+ States; President fixes salary share

Article 156 | Doctrine of Pleasure | Normal 5-year term; NO impeachment & NO grounds in Constitution — tempered by *B.P. Singhal* (2010)

### Constitutional Origin and the Constituent Assembly Debate

Understanding why the Governor is centrally appointed—rather than popularly elected or chosen by the State Legislature—requires revisiting one of the most consequential pivots in the **Constituent Assembly Debates**. When the **Provincial Constitution Committee**, chaired by **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel**, submitted its initial report in July 1947, it proposed a **directly elected Governor** chosen by universal adult suffrage for a four-year term, inspired by the **American model** of autonomous State Governors. That early draft reflected the pre-Partition assumption of looser provincial autonomy under the Cabinet Mission Plan.

Once Partition became a grim reality in August 1947, the political calculus shifted decisively toward national cohesion, administrative unity, and an uncompromised parliamentary system in the States. In the **Draft Constitution of February 1948**, the Drafting Committee chaired by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar placed **two alternative models** before the Assembly under Draft Article 131: direct election by the people of the State, or appointment by the President from a **panel of four candidates** elected by the State Legislative Assembly through proportional representation. On **30 and 31 May 1949**, after intense debate involving Jawaharlal Nehru, B.R. Ambedkar, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, K.M. Munshi, T.T. Krishnamachari, and dissenting voices such as H.V. Kamath and Shibban Lal Saksena, the Assembly rejected both direct election and the Assembly-panel formula. It opted instead for the **Canadian model**, under which the Governor of a Province (styled Lieutenant-Governor in Canada) is appointed directly by the Federal Head of State.

#### Six Reasons Why Direct Election of the Governor Was Rejected

The Constituent Assembly—and standard constitutional scholarship synthesised by M. Laxmikanth and Granville Austin—articulated **six compelling constitutional and practical reasons** for rejecting an elected Governor:

1. **Incompatibility with Parliamentary Government:** Having already chosen the British Westminster parliamentary system in the States—where real executive authority rests with a Chief Minister responsible to the Legislative Assembly—creating a directly elected Governor would introduce a contradictory presidential element into the heart of State governance.
2. **Inevitability of Friction with the Chief Minister:** A Governor elected by the entire electorate of the State would possess a popular mandate broader than that of any single MLA, including the Chief Minister. Such dual legitimacy would almost inevitably spark turf wars, constitutional deadlocks, and competing claims to speak for the people whenever the Governor and the Chief Minister differed.
3. **Wasteful Expenditure for a Constitutional Figurehead:** Because the Governor was intended to function predominantly as a **constitutional (nominal) head** acting on ministerial advice, staging a massive statewide election across millions of voters purely to choose a ceremonial head would squander vast financial resources, administrative energy, and political bandwidth.
4. **Politicisation of a Neutral Office:** A statewide direct election would inevitably be contested along partisan lines, turning the contest into a purely political or personal campaign. A winner emerging from bitter electoral combat could scarcely claim the detached impartiality required to arbitrate hung assemblies or safeguard minority interests.
5. **Partisan Affiliation Destroying Impartiality:** An elected Governor would ordinarily belong to a political party and owe allegiance to its organisational machinery, making it impossible to function as an above-party constitutional umpire—especially if the opposing party won control of the Legislative Assembly.
6. **Threat of Separatist Provincial Tendencies and Weakening of National Unity:** Speaking on **31 May 1949**, Jawaharlal Nehru warned that a directly elected Governor could become a focal point of *"separatist provincial tendencies"* and encourage parochial conflict against the Union. Central appointment under the Canadian model, by contrast, preserved a smooth constitutional link between New Delhi and every provincial capital while enabling the Union to pick eminent outsiders detached from local factionalism.

Even the **Assembly-panel alternative** (President choosing one of four nominees submitted by the State Legislature) was discarded because it would compel the President to select from a list shaped by local majority-party politics, effectively subordinating the Governor to the very ruling faction whose constitutional conduct the Governor might need to check, while also offending the rejected nominees.

> [!debate]
> **Appointed Governor vs Elected Governor — Did the Framers Get It Right?**
> - **Defence of Central Appointment:** Preserves a clean parliamentary chain of accountability centred on the elected Chief Minister; avoids dual popular mandates; enables appointment of distinguished non-local figures; and maintains a vital constitutional channel between the Union and States in a diverse "holding-together" federation.
> - **Critique of Practice:** Because `Article 155` places appointment effectively in the hands of the Union Cabinet and `Article 156(1)` pairs it with removal at pleasure, critics—from the Rajamannar Committee (1971) to State Governments in the 2020s—argue that Raj Bhavans have frequently been staffed with active ruling-party politicians acting as partisan watchdogs against Opposition-led States. The reform consensus therefore seeks not direct election, but a **bipartisan collegium** and **security of tenure**.

### Nature of the Office: Independent Constitutional Post, Not Central Employment

Under **`Article 155`**, *"The Governor of a State shall be appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal."* Because the President exercises executive powers on the aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers (`Article 74`), the Governor is in practical terms nominated by the Central Government. This operational reality often fuels the loose political allegation that the Governor is merely an "agent" or "employee" of the Union Ministry of Home Affairs.

Legally and constitutionally, however, that characterisation is emphatically false. The Supreme Court settled the true constitutional status of the Governor in a landmark five-judge Constitution Bench decision in ***Hargovind Pant v. Dr. Raghukul Tilak* (1979)**. The case arose when Dr. Raghukul Tilak, a former member of the Rajasthan Public Service Commission (RPSC), was appointed Governor of Rajasthan; his appointment was challenged under `Article 319(d)`, which bars former SPSC members from accepting any *"employment either under the Government of India or under the Government of a State."* Rejecting the challenge, Justice P.N. Bhagwati delivered a foundational exposition of gubernatorial independence: the Governor holds an **independent constitutional office**, **NOT** an employment under the Government of India. The Governor is neither subordinate nor subservient to the Central Government, does not take executive orders from Union ministries in the discharge of constitutional duties, and owes primary allegiance to the Constitution and the people of the State under the oath prescribed by `Article 159`.

> [!case]
> ***Hargovind Pant v. Dr. Raghukul Tilak* (1979) — 5-Judge Constitution Bench**
> - **Issue:** Whether a former member of a State Public Service Commission is barred by `Article 319(d)` from being appointed Governor of a State on the ground that the governorship is an "employment under the Government of India".
> - **Ratio Decidendi:** Unanimously held that the office of Governor is a **high, independent constitutional office**, not an employment under the Central Government. Mere appointment by the President does not create a master–servant relationship; the Governor is **not subordinate or subservient** to the Union Government.
> - **Exam Value:** Crucial both for **Prelims** (distinguishing constitutional offices from `Art. 319` employment bars) and for **Mains** (anchoring the argument that Governors must act as constitutional sentinels rather than political agents of New Delhi).

### Qualifications, Constitutional Conventions and Conditions of Office

The Constitution draws a sharp distinction between **mandatory legal qualifications** (`Article 157`), **unwritten healthy conventions**, and **conditions of office** (`Article 158`)—a boundary repeatedly tested in UPSC Prelims.

#### Only Two Constitutional Qualifications (Article 157)

**`Article 157`** prescribes **only two qualifications** for appointment as Governor:
1. The person must be a **citizen of India** (whether by birth or naturalisation—unlike the United States presidency, naturalised Indian citizens are eligible).
2. The person must have **completed the age of 35 years**.

Crucially, `Article 157` does **not** require a gubernatorial appointee to be qualified for election as a member of the State Legislative Assembly, Legislative Council, or Parliament. This creates a subtle contrast with the President (`Article 58`, who must be qualified for election to the Lok Sabha) and the Vice-President (`Article 66`, who must be qualified for election to the Rajya Sabha). Nor does the Constitution prescribe any educational qualification, administrative experience, or cooling-off period from active politics.

#### Two Unwritten Conventions and Their Routine Breach

During the Constituent Assembly Debates (notably Jawaharlal Nehru's speech on 31 May 1949) and early post-Independence practice, **two healthy constitutional conventions** developed to reinforce the Governor's impartiality:
- **The Outsider Convention:** The appointee should be an **outsider**—that is, not belonging to the State where appointed—so that the Governor remains insulated from local caste alignments, regional factionalism, and parochial party politics.
- **Consultation with the Chief Minister:** While appointing a Governor, the President (effectively the Union Government) is expected to **consult the Chief Minister** of the concerned State to foster smooth constitutional functioning and mutual trust between Raj Bhavan and the State Secretariat.

Because neither convention is encoded in `Article 155` or `Article 157`, both have been breached repeatedly across party regimes—especially whenever different political formations govern the Union and the State. In many instances, Union Governments have merely *"intimated"* the Chief Minister minutes before issuing the press communiqué rather than holding meaningful prior consultation, prompting sharp criticism from the Sarkaria (1988), Venkatachaliah (2002), and Punchhi (2010) Commissions.

#### Conditions of Office, Emoluments and Official Residence (Article 158)

**`Article 158`** lays down four explicit constitutional conditions governing the Governor's office:
1. **Bar on Legislative Membership (`Article 158(1)`):** The Governor shall **not** be a member of either House of Parliament or of a House of the Legislature of any State. If a sitting MP, MLA, or MLC is appointed Governor, they are deemed to have **vacated their seat** in that House on the exact date they enter upon office as Governor.
2. **Bar on Office of Profit (`Article 158(2)`):** The Governor shall **not** hold any other **office of profit**.
3. **Rent-Free Residence and Parliament-Fixed Emoluments (`Article 158(3)`):** The Governor is entitled, without payment of rent, to the use of official residences (**Raj Bhavan**), and to such emoluments, allowances, and privileges as may be determined by **Parliament by law** (and until Parliament so legislated, as specified in the Second Schedule). Under Section 3 of the ***Governors (Emoluments, Allowances and Privileges) Act, 1982***, as amended by **Section 161 of the Finance Act, 2018** (with retrospective effect from 1 January 2016), a Governor draws a salary of **₹3,50,000 per month** (revised upward from ₹1,10,000 fixed in 2008), alongside separate tour, hospitality, entertainment, and Raj Bhavan maintenance allowances notified by the Union Ministry of Home Affairs. Note a fine statutory nuance: if an appointee already draws a pension from prior government service, the monthly gubernatorial emoluments are reduced by the amount of that pension.
4. **Shared Allocation for Common Governor (`Article 158(3A)`):** Inserted by the **`7th Amendment Act, 1956`**, whenever the same person serves as Governor of two or more States, emoluments and allowances are shared among those States in proportions fixed by an **order of the President** (not Parliament, and not equal splits by compulsion).
5. **Financial Independence (`Article 158(4)` & `Article 202(3)(a)`):** The emoluments and allowances of the Governor **shall not be diminished during their term of office**, and all expenditure relating to the emoluments, allowances, and office of the Governor is **charged upon the Consolidated Fund of the State** (`Article 202(3)(a)`), meaning the State Legislative Assembly may discuss it but **cannot vote** to cut it.

#### Oath or Affirmation (Article 159) and Contingencies (Article 160)

Before entering upon office, every Governor (and every person discharging the functions of a Governor) must make and subscribe an oath or affirmation under **`Article 159`** in the presence of the **Chief Justice of the High Court** exercising jurisdiction in relation to the State, or, in the Chief Justice's absence, the **senior-most Judge of that High Court** available.

In the oath—whose exact text appears **directly in `Article 159` and NOT in the Third Schedule** (just as the oaths of the President under `Article 60` and the Vice-President under `Article 69` stand outside the Third Schedule)—the Governor solemnly swears or affirms:
- to **faithfully execute** the office of Governor (or discharge the functions of the Governor);
- to the best of ability to **preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law**; and
- to **devote himself/herself to the service and well-being of the people** of the State.

Notice the exact symmetry with `Article 60`: the President and the Governor are the **only two constitutional functionaries** in India who swear to **"preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law"**—whereas Ministers, Judges, CAG, and legislators swear under the Third Schedule to *"bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution"* and *"uphold the Constitution and the laws."*

Under **`Article 160`**, the President may make such provision as deemed fit for the discharge of the functions of the Governor of a State in any **contingency** not provided for in Chapter II of Part VI—such as the sudden death, sudden resignation, or incapacitating illness of an incumbent Governor. In practice, the President routinely assigns temporary additional charge either to the Governor of a neighbouring State or to the Chief Justice of the State High Court.

#### Constitutional Immunities (Article 361) and Their Limits

To safeguard the dignity and unimpeded functioning of the constitutional heads of the Union and States, **`Article 361`** grants identical personal immunities to both the President and the Governors across **four distinct clauses**:
1. **Non-Answerability for Official Acts (`Article 361(1)`):** The Governor is **not answerable to any court** for the exercise and performance of the powers and duties of office, or for any act done or purporting to be done in that capacity. However, the second proviso expressly preserves the right of any person to bring appropriate proceedings against the **Government of a State**.
2. **Absolute Bar on Criminal Proceedings (`Article 361(2)`):** **No criminal proceedings whatsoever** shall be instituted or continued against a Governor in any court during their term of office—extending even to **personal acts** committed before or during tenure.
3. **Immunity from Arrest or Imprisonment (`Article 361(3)`):** No process for the **arrest or imprisonment** of a Governor shall issue from any court during their term of office.
4. **Two Months' Notice for Personal Civil Proceedings (`Article 361(4)`):** Civil proceedings claiming relief against a Governor in respect of any act done in a **personal capacity** (whether before or after entering office) cannot be instituted during the term of office **until the expiration of two months next after written notice** has been delivered to the Governor (or left at their office) specifying the nature of the proceedings, the cause of action, the name, description, and place of residence of the claimant, and the relief claimed.

Crucially, constitutional courts distinguish **personal immunity of the individual** from **judicial review of official constitutional action**. In ***Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India* (2006)**, a five-judge Bench held that while `Article 361(1)` bars issuing personal notice to the Governor or compelling a personal affidavit, it **does not bar judicial scrutiny of gubernatorial acts** on grounds of *mala fides* or *ultra vires* illegality, with the Government defending the record.

Sharpening this boundary on **20 November 2025** in ***In Re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills* (`Special Reference No. 1 of 2025`, `2025 INSC 1333`, para 165.4)**, a five-judge Constitution Bench ruled that while `Article 361` grants absolute personal immunity to the individual, **"the constitutional office of the Governor is subject to the jurisdiction of this Court."** Accordingly, `Article 361` cannot shield prolonged, unexplained inaction under `Article 200` from a limited writ of mandamus.

> [!prelims]
> **Five High-Yield UPSC Prelims Traps on Articles 153–162 & 361**
> 1. **Oath Location (`Art. 159`):** The Governor's oath is **NOT** in the Third Schedule; it sits directly in `Article 159` (mirroring President `Art. 60` and VP `Art. 69`) and is administered by the **High Court Chief Justice**, NOT the President.
> 2. **Qualifications (`Art. 157`):** Only **two** qualifications (Indian citizen + 35 years). Legislative eligibility is **NOT** required; "outsider" and "CM consultation" are **conventions**, not constitutional mandates.
> 3. **Criminal vs Civil Immunity (`Art. 361` — 2018 PYQ):** Criminal proceedings are **completely barred** during term (even for personal acts); personal **civil proceedings** are **allowed** after **two months' written notice**.
> 4. **Salary Revision & Charging (`Art. 158(3)–(4)`):** Governor's salary (**₹3.5 lakh/month**) is fixed by **Parliament** (NOT the State Legislature!), yet **charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State** (`Art. 202(3)(a)`).
> 5. **Federal Amendment Entrenchment (`Art. 368(2)` Proviso — 2025 PYQ):** Amending **`Article 162`** (extent of executive power of a State) requires **special majority + ratification by at least half the States**, whereas amending **`Article 158`** (conditions of Governor's office) does **NOT** require State ratification!

### Tenure, Doctrine of Pleasure and Judicial Review of Removal

Nowhere is the structural vulnerability of the Governor's office more stark than in **`Article 156`**, which governs tenure and removal.

#### Article 156: Five-Year Term, Resignation, Transfer, Reappointment and Pleasure

`Article 156` establishes a three-clause framework:
- **Doctrine of Pleasure (`Article 156(1)`):** *"The Governor shall hold office during the pleasure of the President."*
- **Resignation (`Article 156(2)`):** A Governor may resign at any time by writing under their hand addressed to the **President**.
- **Normal Five-Year Term and Holdover Proviso (`Article 156(3)`):** Subject to the preceding clauses, a Governor holds office for a term of **five years** from the date on which they enter upon office. Crucially, the **proviso to `Article 156(3)`** stipulates that *"a Governor shall, notwithstanding the expiration of his term, continue to hold office until his successor enters upon his office."* This holdover clause prevents any constitutional vacuum or interregnum in the State Executive.

Because `Article 156(3)` opens with the subordinating words **"Subject to the foregoing provisions of this Article,"** the five-year term is completely subordinate to the **pleasure of the President** under `Article 156(1)`. Unlike the President (`Article 61`), Supreme Court and High Court Judges (`Articles 124(4), 217`), the Comptroller and Auditor General (`Article 148`), or the Chief Election Commissioner (`Article 324`), the Constitution prescribes **NO impeachment procedure**, **NO security of tenure**, and **NO grounds whatsoever** for the removal of a Governor (**UPSC Prelims 2013 PYQ**).

The Constitution is equally silent on **transfer** and **reappointment**. By established executive practice under `Articles 155–156`, the President may transfer a Governor appointed to one State to another State for the remainder of the term, and may reappoint an outgoing Governor in the same State or another State without any constitutional cap on the number of terms.

In practice, the absence of tenure security turned gubernatorial postings into a casualty of national electoral turnovers. Wholesale removals or forced resignations of Governors appointed by predecessor regimes occurred after changes of Union Government in **1977** (Janata Party), **1980** (Indira Gandhi's return), **1989** (V.P. Singh), **1991** (P.V. Narasimha Rao), **2004** (UPA-I), and **2014** (NDA). An empirical study by **Professor Ashok Pankaj** tracking gubernatorial tenures between **1950 and 2015** revealed the startling instability produced by `Article 156(1)`: **only 18 per cent** of Governors completed a full five-year term, **37 per cent** served less than one year, and another **15 per cent** served between one and two years. As the Sarkaria Commission observed in 1988, Governors were frequently transferred or removed *"as if they were civil servants,"* fatally eroding their capacity to act as fearless, non-partisan constitutional sentinels.

#### B.P. Singhal v. Union of India (2010): Disciplining the Doctrine of Pleasure

This unchecked political practice finally came before a five-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court—comprising **CJI K.G. Balakrishnan** and Justices S.H. Kapadia, R.V. Raveendran, B. Sudershan Reddy, and P. Sathasivam—in ***B.P. Singhal v. Union of India* (7 May 2010)**. The Public Interest Litigation challenged the July 2004 removal of four Governors—Vishnu Kant Shastri (Uttar Pradesh), Babu Parmanand (Haryana), Kailashpati Mishra (Gujarat), and Kidar Nath Sahani (Goa)—immediately after the UPA Government took office at the Centre, purely on the perceived ground that they did not share the ideology of the new ruling coalition.

Writing for a unanimous Bench, Justice R.V. Raveendran laid down **six path-breaking constitutional propositions** that govern `Article 156(1)` today:
1. **Executive Power on Aid and Advice:** The President's power under `Article 156(1)` is exercised on the aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers under `Article 74(1)`. Because the framers deliberately rejected a fixed, impeachable tenure, a Governor has **no guaranteed security of tenure** and no statutory right to a prior show-cause hearing or natural justice inquiry before removal.
2. **Constitutional Pleasure Is NOT Royal Prerogative:** In a constitutional republic governed by the Rule of Law, the **doctrine of pleasure is NOT arbitrary, unfettered, capricious, or whimsical**. The Court drew a sharp distinction between the unlimited common-law pleasure of the British Crown and the disciplined constitutional pleasure under India's Constitution (also contrasting `Article 156` with `Article 310` for civil servants and `Articles 75(2)/164(1)` for Ministers, who serve as political colleagues of the PM/CM).
3. **Valid and Compelling Reasons Required:** Withdrawal of Presidential pleasure under `Article 156(1)` should occur **only in rare and exceptional circumstances for valid and compelling reasons**—such as physical or mental incapacity, corruption, behaviour unbecoming of a Governor, violation of the Constitution, or a complete breakdown of constitutional propriety.
4. **Change of Central Government or Ideological Mismatch Is Invalid:** Crucially, **mere change of the political party in power at the Centre**, or the fact that a Governor is *"out of sync"* with the policies, philosophy, or political ideology of the new Union Government, **cannot be a valid ground for removal**. A Governor is a constitutional sentinel, not an employee or party functionary of the Union Cabinet.
5. **No Mandatory Disclosure at Removal, Yet Limited Judicial Review Applies:** The Union Government is not obliged to communicate reasons to the Governor at the time of removal, and courts begin with a presumption that the President acted bona fide on valid material. **However**, if the removed Governor (or aggrieved party) establishes a **strong *prima facie* case** that the removal was **arbitrary, *mala fide*, capricious, or whimsical**, the court can call upon the Union Government to produce the underlying material before it.
6. **Narrow Scope of Judicial Scrutiny:** Once the Union demonstrates the existence of *some* relevant, bona fide material, the court will **not** sit in appeal over the sufficiency or adequacy of those reasons; judicial review intervenes only where no reasons exist at all, or where the stated reasons are patently irrelevant, arbitrary, or *mala fide*.

While *B.P. Singhal* stopped short of creating an impeachment requirement or invalidating the 2004 removals (since the petitioners had no direct personal challenge left after six years), it permanently demolished the claim that `Article 156(1)` is a licence for partisan spoils-system purges.

#### Extent of Executive Power of the State (Article 162)

Rounding out the foundational provisions of the State Executive, **`Article 162`** defines the territorial and functional reach of State executive authority. Subject to the provisions of the Constitution, the **executive power of a State is co-extensive with the legislative power of the State Legislature**—meaning the State Government can take executive action on every subject in **List II (State List)** even in the absence of a specific prior statute (provided it does not infringe Fundamental Rights or existing law). In matters belonging to **List III (Concurrent List)**, however, the proviso to `Article 162` clarifies that the State's executive power is **subject to, and limited by**, any executive power expressly conferred by the Constitution or by a Parliamentary law upon the Union or its authorities. Because `Article 162` sits at the core of India's federal division of powers, it is expressly protected under the **proviso to `Article 368(2)`**—any constitutional amendment altering `Article 162` requires ratification by at least half the State Legislatures.

## 2. Powers of the Governor and Comparison with the President

Across the constitutional text, the Governor possesses broad powers mirroring those of the President of India in **four spheres**: **Executive**, **Legislative** (including assent to Bills and ordinance-making), **Financial**, and **Judicial**. Yet the parallel is far from exact. Unlike the President, the Governor possesses **no diplomatic powers** (cannot negotiate treaties or accredit ambassadors), **no military powers** (not a commander of the armed forces), and **no emergency powers** (cannot proclaim a National, State, or Financial Emergency—and can only send a report recommending President's Rule under `Article 356`). Conversely, as `Section 3` explores in detail, the Governor enjoys **wider express constitutional discretion** under `Article 163` than the President enjoys under `Article 74`, and exercises a uniquely federal gatekeeping function over State Bills under `Articles 200–201`.

### Executive Powers

Under **`Article 154(1)`**, the executive power of the State is vested in the Governor and is exercised either directly or through officers subordinate to the Governor in accordance with the Constitution. **`Article 166`** translates this vesting into daily governance through three procedural rules:
1. All executive actions of the Government of a State are **formally expressed to be taken in the name of the Governor** (`Article 166(1)`).
2. Orders and instruments made and executed in the name of the Governor are authenticated in such manner as specified in rules made by the Governor, and the validity of a duly authenticated order cannot be questioned on the ground that it was not executed by the Governor personally (`Article 166(2)`).
3. The Governor makes **Rules of Business** for the more convenient transaction of the business of the State Government and for the **allocation of business among Ministers**, insofar as it is not business in which the Governor is required to act in discretion (`Article 166(3)`).

Within this executive domain, the Governor performs an extensive suite of appointment, supervisory, and statutory functions:

- **Chief Minister and Council of Ministers (`Article 164(1)`):** Appoints the Chief Minister and, on the Chief Minister's advice, the other Ministers, who hold office during the Governor's pleasure (exercised strictly on the Chief Minister's advice).
- **Mandatory Tribal Welfare Minister (`Proviso to Article 164(1)`):** Four States—**Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, and Odisha**—must have a Minister in charge of Tribal Welfare (who may also hold SC/BC welfare or other portfolios). Originally covering **Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, and Orissa**, the **`94th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2006`** **removed Bihar** (after its tribal south became Jharkhand in 2000) and **added Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand**.
- **Advocate General (`Article 165`):** Appoints a High Court Judge–qualified jurist as Advocate General, fixes their remuneration, and removes them at pleasure.
- **State Election Commissioner (`Articles 243K & 243ZA`):** Appoints the SEC and fixes tenure/service conditions (non-variable to disadvantage). **Crucial safeguard:** The Governor **CANNOT remove the SEC**—removal follows the exact **High Court Judge** procedure (by President after Parliamentary special-majority address!).
- **State Public Service Commission (`Articles 316–317`):** Appoints the **SPSC Chairman and Members**, but **CANNOT remove them**! Under `Article 317(1)` and `(3)`, **ONLY the President** can remove SPSC members (for misbehaviour after a Supreme Court inquiry under `Article 145`, or for insolvency, outside employment, or infirmity). Under **`Article 317(2)`**, the Governor may only **suspend** a member pending the President's order after a Supreme Court reference.
- **Information & Cabinet Review (`Article 167`):** Seeks administrative and legislative information from the Chief Minister and may require an individual minister's decision to be placed before the full Council of Ministers.
- **President's Rule (`Article 356`):** Recommends President's Rule on constitutional breakdown and administers the State as the President's agent once imposed.
- **Ex-Officio Chancellor of State Universities (Statutory Role):** Under State University Acts, the Governor serves as **ex-officio Chancellor** of State public universities and appoints **Vice-Chancellors**. Because this role is **purely statutory, NOT constitutional**—and historically exercised outside `Article 163` ministerial advice (*Bhuri Nath*, 1997)—Vice-Chancellor appointments sparked chronic rows in **Tamil Nadu, Kerala, West Bengal, and Punjab (2022–2026)**. Several States passed Bills replacing the Governor as Chancellor; Rashtrapati Bhavan returned Punjab's Bill (**July 2024**) and withheld assent to three West Bengal Bills (**December 2025**), while Tamil Nadu's ten university Bills triggered the landmark 2025 Supreme Court verdicts.

> [!prelims]
> **The Four Classic Appoint-vs-Remove Traps Under the Governor's Executive Powers**
> 1. **SPSC Chairman & Members (`Arts. 316–317`):** Appointed by **Governor** → Removed **ONLY by President** (Governor can only **suspend** pending SC inquiry!).
> 2. **State Election Commissioner (`Art. 243K`):** Appointed by **Governor** → Removed **ONLY like a High Court Judge** (by President after Parliamentary special-majority address — NOT at Governor's pleasure!).
> 3. **Advocate General (`Art. 165`):** Appointed by **Governor** → Removed by **Governor** (holds office at Governor's pleasure, conventionally resigning with the State Cabinet).
> 4. **University Chancellor:** Conferred by **State Statutes**, **NOT** by the Constitution! (And the **Punchhi Commission, 2010** explicitly recommended ending this statutory burden.)

### Legislative Powers

Under **`Article 168`**, the Governor is an **integral part of the State Legislature** (alongside the Legislative Assembly, and the Legislative Council in bicameral States), even though the Governor is not a member of either House. The Constitution vests the Governor with wide-ranging legislative powers:

- **Summoning, Proroguing and Dissolving (`Article 174`):** Summons each House of the State Legislature to meet at such time and place as thought fit (ensuring **not more than six months** elapse between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next), prorogues either House, and dissolves the **Legislative Assembly** (the Legislative Council is a permanent body not subject to dissolution). As settled in ***Nabam Rebia* (2016)**, while a Council of Ministers enjoys the confidence of the Assembly, these `Article 174` powers must be exercised strictly on ministerial **aid and advice**.
- **Right to Address and Send Messages (`Article 175`):** May address either House separately or both Houses assembled together, and may send messages to the House or Houses with respect to a pending Bill or otherwise, which the House must consider with all convenient dispatch.
- **Special Address (`Article 176` — UPSC Prelims 2019 PYQ):** Must address the Legislative Assembly (or both Houses together in bicameral States) at the commencement of the **first session after each general election** to the Assembly and at the commencement of the **first session of each calendar year**, informing the Legislature of the causes of its summons. Because this Special Address articulates the elected government's annual policy blueprint, it is drafted by the State Cabinet and followed by a **Motion of Thanks**; recent rows in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and West Bengal (2023–2026)—where Governors skipped, edited, or walked out during Cabinet-prepared paragraphs—drew sharp constitutional critique for undermining `Article 163` ministerial responsibility.
- **Presiding Arrangements in Double Vacancies (`Articles 180(1) & 184(1)`):** Appoints any member of the Legislative Assembly to preside over its proceedings when the offices of **both Speaker and Deputy Speaker** fall vacant (`Article 180(1)`), and similarly appoints any member of the Legislative Council to preside when **both Chairman and Deputy Chairman** offices are vacant (`Article 184(1)`). *(Note: When presiding officers are merely **absent**—not vacant—the Panel of Chairmen presides, not a Governor nominee!)*
- **Nomination to the Legislative Council (`Article 171(5)`):** Nominates **one-sixth (1/6th)** of the members of the State Legislative Council from persons having special knowledge or practical experience in **five specific fields**: **Literature, Science, Art, Cooperative Movement, and Social Service**. *(Classic Prelims distinction: Under `Article 80(3)`, the President nominates 12 Rajya Sabha MPs from only **four** fields—**Cooperative Movement** is unique to the Governor's State Legislative Council nominations!)*
- **End of Anglo-Indian Nomination (`Article 333`):** Previously, the Governor could nominate **one member** of the Anglo-Indian community to the State Legislative Assembly if inadequately represented. The **`104th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019`** (effective **25 January 2020**) discontinued this Anglo-Indian nomination provision for both the Lok Sabha (`Article 331`) and State Assemblies (`Article 333`).
- **Deciding Disqualifications of Legislators (`Article 192`):** Decides any question as to whether a member of the State Legislature has become subject to any of the constitutional or statutory disqualifications under **`Article 191(1)`** (office of profit, unsound mind, undischarged insolvency, foreign citizenship, or RPA 1951 statutory disqualifications). Before deciding, the Governor **must obtain the opinion of the Election Commission of India** and **shall act according to such opinion** (`Article 192(2)` — ECI's opinion is constitutionally **binding**!). By contrast, disqualifications on the ground of **defection under the Tenth Schedule** (`Article 191(2)`) are decided by the **Speaker** (Assembly) or **Chairman** (Council), NOT the Governor.
- **Laying Reports of Constitutional Authorities:** Causes to be laid before the State Legislature the annual reports of the **State Finance Commission** (`Article 243-I(2)`), the **State Public Service Commission** (`Article 323(2)`, along with an explanatory memorandum wherever SPSC advice was not accepted), and the reports of the **Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG)** relating to the accounts of the State (`Article 151(2)`).

### Assent and Reservation of Bills: Articles 200 and 201

No bill passed by a State Legislature can become an Act without receiving assent under **`Article 200`** (or **`Article 201`** if reserved for the President). Over the past five years, `Articles 200 and 201` have evolved from quiet textbook clauses into the single most fiercely litigated arena of Indian constitutional federalism—culminating in two historic Supreme Court pronouncements in **April 2025** and **November 2025** that every UPSC aspirant must master with surgical precision.

#### Textual Anatomy of Article 200 and Article 201

When a Bill has been passed by the Legislative Assembly (or by both Houses in a bicameral State) and presented to the Governor, **`Article 200`** lays down the Governor's substantive powers alongside two pivotal provisos:
- **Substantive Clause:** The Governor shall declare either—
  1. that they **assent** to the Bill (it immediately becomes an Act); OR
  2. that they **withhold assent** therefrom; OR
  3. that they **reserve the Bill for the consideration of the President**.
- **First Proviso (Suspensive Return — Non-Money Bills ONLY):** The Governor may, **as soon as possible** after the presentation of a Bill (provided it is **not a Money Bill**), **return the Bill** to the House or Houses with a message requesting reconsideration of the Bill or of any specified provisions or suggested amendments. If the House or Houses pass the Bill again—**with or without amendment**—and present it once more to the Governor, **"the Governor shall not withhold assent therefrom."**
- **Second Proviso (Mandatory Reservation for High Court Protection):** The Governor **shall NOT assent to**, but **SHALL reserve for the consideration of the President**, any Bill which in the Governor's opinion would, if it became law, **so derogate from the powers of the High Court as to endanger the position which that Court is by the Constitution designed to fill**.

Beyond the `Article 200` second proviso, the Constitution specifies several other circumstances in which Presidential reservation and assent are **mandatory** or constitutionally required for full legal validity:
1. **`Article 31A(1)` Proviso:** State laws providing for compulsory acquisition of estates or taking over management of property (to secure immunity from `Articles 14 and 19`).
2. **`Article 31C` Proviso:** State laws giving effect to Directive Principles (`Article 39(b)–(c)`).
3. **`Article 254(2)`:** State laws on a **Concurrent List** subject containing provisions repugnant to an existing Parliamentary law (Presidential assent enables the State law to prevail within that State, though Parliament may subsequently override it).
4. **`Article 288(2)`:** State laws imposing taxes on water or electricity stored, generated, consumed, distributed, or sold by any authority established by Parliament for regulating inter-state rivers or river valleys.
5. **`Article 304(b)` read with `Article 255`:** Bills imposing reasonable restrictions on freedom of inter-state trade require **previous Presidential sanction** before introduction; if introduced without sanction, subsequent Presidential assent under `Article 255` cures the defect.
6. **`Article 360(4)(a)(ii)`:** During a **Financial Emergency**, the President may direct a State to reserve all **Money Bills or other Financial Bills** for Presidential consideration after they are passed by the State Legislature. *(Note the 2025 Prelims PYQ nuance: even here, the President cannot independently pluck a Bill out of a State Assembly on their own; it is the Governor who reserves Bills under `Article 200`!)*

In addition to these express constitutional triggers, M. Laxmikanth notes that Governors have historically reserved Bills where the legislation appears *ultra vires* the Constitution, directly opposed to Directive Principles, against the larger interest of the country, of grave national importance, or affecting compulsory acquisition of property under `Article 31A`.

Once a Governor reserves a Bill for the President, **`Article 201`** takes over—and the **Governor's constitutional role in that Bill terminates completely**. Under `Article 201`, the President declares either that they **assent** to the Bill or that they **withhold assent** therefrom. Where the reserved Bill is **not a Money Bill**, the President may direct the Governor to **return the Bill** to the State Legislature with a message for reconsideration. When so returned, the State Legislature **must reconsider it within a period of six months** from receipt. Crucially, even if the State Legislature passes the Bill again (with or without amendment) and presents it again to the President, **the President is NOT bound to give assent**! Unlike `Article 111` (where the President *must* assent to a re-passed Parliamentary Bill) or the first proviso to `Article 200` (where the Governor *shall not withhold* assent to a re-passed State Bill), **`Article 201` imposes no obligation on the President to assent even after re-passage**, nor does it fix a deadline for the President's decision.

```flow
# Legislative Pathway Under Articles 200 and 201 (Post-November 2025 Clarification)
Bill Passed by State Legislature | Presented to the Governor under Article 200 (Constitutional Discretion — Nov 2025 Opinion)
Option 1: Grant Assent | Bill immediately becomes a State Act || Option 2: Withhold & Return (Non-Money Bills ONLY) | First Proviso restricts withholding; return with message 'as soon as possible' || Option 3: Reserve for President | Discretionary OR Mandatory (e.g., High Court powers under 2nd Proviso, Arts. 31A, 254(2), 288(2))
If Returned: Legislature Re-passes (with/without amendments) | Governor CANNOT withhold assent; may Assent OR Reserve for President (Nov 2025 Opinion) || If Reserved: President Acts Under Article 201 | Governor's role ends; President may Assent, Withhold, or Return non-Money Bill (6-month Legislature reconsideration)
State Act Enacted OR Presidential Veto Final | Under Art. 201, President is NOT bound to assent even if State re-passes! No rigid judicial timelines or Art. 142 'deemed assent' — only limited mandamus for prolonged, unexplained inaction (20 Nov 2025)
```

#### The Judicial Evolution (2023–2025): From Punjab to Tamil Nadu to the Presidential Reference

Because neither `Article 200` nor `Article 201` specifies a numerical timeframe, several Governors between 2020 and 2024—notably in **Telangana, Punjab, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and West Bengal**—sat indefinitely on dozens of Bills passed by elected State Assemblies without assenting, returning, or reserving them, effectively inventing an unconstitutional **"pocket veto."** This deadlock generated a monumental three-stage Supreme Court jurisprudence between November 2023 and November 2025:

**Stage 1 — *State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor* (10 November 2023, 3-Judge Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud):** After Punjab Governor Banwarilal Purohit sat on four Bills passed in a reconvened June 2023 Assembly sitting, the Supreme Court upheld the Speaker's power to reconvene a House adjourned *sine die*. Ruling that **"as soon as possible"** carries real constitutional urgency and **bars any pocket veto**, the Bench read "withhold assent" harmoniously with the **first proviso**: withholding assent to a non-Money Bill requires **returning it with a message** rather than silently killing it.

> "The Governor, as an unelected Head of the State, is entrusted with certain constitutional powers. However, this power cannot be used to thwart the normal course of lawmaking by the State Legislatures." — Supreme Court of India, *State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor of Punjab*, 10 November 2023

**Stage 2 — *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu* (8 April 2025, `2025 INSC 481`, 2-Judge Bench of Justices J.B. Pardiwala and R. Mahadevan):** Between January 2020 and April 2023, Tamil Nadu's Assembly passed 12 Bills (chiefly reforming State university governance). Governor R.N. Ravi took no action until October 2023; two days after SC notice (10 November 2023), he summarily withheld assent to 10 Bills without returning them and reserved 2. After the Assembly re-enacted all 10 Bills unchanged on **18 November 2023**, the Governor on **28 November 2023** reserved those 10 re-passed Bills for the President (who withheld assent to 7, assented to 1, and left 2 pending).

Striking down the reservation of the 10 re-passed Bills (and consequential Presidential actions) as **illegal and *non est***, the two-judge Bench ruled: (1) there is **no "absolute" or "pocket" veto** under `Article 200`, and a re-passed Bill without material change must receive assent rather than second-stage reservation; (2) the Governor generally acts on **ministerial aid and advice** under `Article 200` (save express exceptions like the second proviso); (3) fixed **judicial timelines** apply—**one month** on ministerial advice or after re-passage, **three months** when acting contrary to advice, and **three months** for the President under `Article 201`; and (4) invoking **`Article 142`**, all **10 re-passed Bills were "deemed assented"** as of 18 November 2023.

**Stage 3 — *In Re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills by the Governor and the President of India* (`Special Reference No. 1 of 2025`, `2025 INSC 1333`, `(2026) 1 SCC 1`, 20 November 2025, 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** On **13 May 2025**, President Droupadi Murmu invoked **`Article 143(1)`** to refer **14 questions** on `Articles 200–201` and separation of powers. Answering the reference unanimously (without disturbing the final relief already granted to Tamil Nadu), **CJI B.R. Gavai and Justices Surya Kant, Vikram Nath, P.S. Narasimha, and A.S. Chandurkar** recalibrated the April 2025 ruling in **Paragraph 165**:

1. **Three Options (`165.1`):** Under `Article 200`, the Governor has **three options**—assent, reserve for the President, or withhold and return with comments (**non-Money Bills only**); the first proviso restricts withholding rather than creating a fourth option. Even after re-passage, while withholding is barred, the Governor may **assent or reserve** the Bill for the President where warranted.
2. **Independent Discretion (`165.2`):** Overturning the April 2025 view, the Governor exercises **independent constitutional discretion** under `Article 200` and is **NOT bound by ministerial aid and advice**.
3. **No Merits Review, YET Limited Mandamus (`165.3` & `165.6`):** The **merits** of decisions under `Articles 200 and 201` are **non-justiciable**. However, in glaring cases of **"prolonged, unexplained, and indefinite" inaction**, courts may issue a **limited mandamus** directing the Governor to act within a reasonable period without dictating the outcome.
4. **Article 361 Clarified (`165.4`):** `Article 361` confers **personal immunity**, not immunity for constitutional paralysis: the **office of Governor** remains amenable to limited judicial review for prolonged inaction.
5. **No Judicial Timelines (`165.5` & `165.7`):** Since `Articles 200 and 201` deliberately omit deadlines, courts **cannot impose rigid timelines** on either the Governor or the President.
6. **Optional `Art. 143` & No Pre-Enactment Review (`165.8` & `165.9`):** The President need **not** seek an `Article 143` reference on every reserved Bill, and courts **cannot review a Bill's contents before enactment** (save via `Article 143`).
7. **No `Article 142` "Deemed Assent" (`165.10` & `165.11`):** **`Article 142` cannot create "deemed assent"** or allow a State Bill to become law without gubernatorial/presidential assent, which violates **separation of powers**.
8. **Questions Returned (`165.12–165.14`):** Questions **12** (`Art. 145(3)` bench composition) and **14** (`Art. 131` Union–State suits) were returned unanswered as irrelevant, and Question **13** (general scope of `Art. 142`) was deemed overly broad beyond Question 10.

> [!news]
> **Reconciling April 2025 vs November 2025 for Prelims & Mains (2025–2026 Most Important Polity Shift!)**
> - ***State of Tamil Nadu* (8 Apr 2025, 2-Judge Bench, `2025 INSC 481`):** Bound Governor largely to ministerial advice; imposed **1-month / 3-month timelines** under Arts. 200–201; barred reserving re-passed Bills; granted **`Art. 142` "deemed assent"** to 10 TN Bills.
> - ***Special Reference No. 1 of 2025* (20 Nov 2025, 5-Judge Constitution Bench, `2025 INSC 1333`):** Restored **independent gubernatorial discretion** under Art. 200; **rejected rigid judicial timelines** and **`Art. 142` "deemed assent"** as violating separation of powers; held Art. 200/201 merits non-justiciable; **YET preserved constitutional accountability** by confirming that there is **no absolute pocket veto** and courts may issue a **limited mandamus** for *"prolonged, unexplained, and indefinite" inaction*, unimpeded by `Article 361` personal immunity!

### Financial Powers

In the financial domain, the Governor acts as the formal constitutional gatekeeper ensuring executive accountability over State public finances:
- **State Budget (`Article 202`):** Sees that the **Annual Financial Statement** (State Budget)—showing estimated receipts and expenditure of the State for the financial year—is laid before the State Legislature (the word "Budget" appears nowhere in the Constitution).
- **Prior Recommendation for Money Bills (`Article 207`):** No **Money Bill** (as defined in `Article 199`) can be introduced in the State Legislative Assembly **except on the prior recommendation of the Governor** (the same prior recommendation applies to Financial Bills Category I under `Article 207(1)` and consideration of Financial Bills Category II under `Article 207(3)`).
- **Demands for Grants (`Article 203(3)`):** No **Demand for a Grant** can be made in the Legislative Assembly except on the recommendation of the Governor (likewise for supplementary, additional, or excess grants under `Article 205`).
- **Contingency Fund of the State (`Article 267(2)`):** The **Contingency Fund of the State** is placed at the disposal of the Governor to make advances for meeting unforeseen expenditure pending authorisation by the State Legislature.
- **State Finance Commission (`Articles 243-I & 243-Y`):** Constitutes a **State Finance Commission every five years** to review the financial position of Panchayats (`Article 243-I`) and Municipalities (`Article 243-Y`) and recommend principles for tax sharing, grants-in-aid, and revenue augmentation between the State Government and local bodies.

### Judicial and Pardoning Powers (Article 161)

The Governor exercises both **clemency (pardoning) powers** and **judicial-appointment functions** in the State sphere.

#### Scope and Modes of Clemency Under Article 161

Under **`Article 161`**, the Governor possesses the power to grant **pardons, reprieves, respites, or remissions** of punishment, or to **suspend, remit, or commute** the sentence of any person convicted of any offence against any law relating to a matter to which the **executive power of the State extends**. Each of the five classical clemency terms carries a precise legal meaning:
1. **Pardon:** Completely absolves the convict of both the sentence and the conviction, removing all punishments, penalties, and constitutional disqualifications as though the offence had never been committed.
2. **Commutation:** Substitutes one form of punishment for a **lighter form** (for example, commuting a death sentence to rigorous imprisonment, or rigorous imprisonment to simple imprisonment).
3. **Remission:** Reduces the **duration/period** of a sentence without changing its character (for example, reducing a ten-year rigorous imprisonment term to five years of rigorous imprisonment).
4. **Respite:** Awards a **lesser sentence** in place of the originally awarded sentence owing to a **special mitigating circumstance**—such as physical disability of a convict or the pregnancy of a woman offender.
5. **Reprieve:** Grants a **temporary stay of execution** of a sentence (especially a death sentence) to allow the convict time to seek a pardon or commutation from the Governor or the President.

#### Two Critical Limits — and a Famous UPSC Prelims Trap

Comparing `Article 161` with the President's pardoning power under `Article 72` uncovers two decisive jurisdictional limitations alongside one classic UPSC Prelims trap:
- **No Jurisdiction Over Court Martial:** The Governor has **zero authority** to pardon, reprieve, respite, suspend, remit, or commute any punishment or sentence inflicted by a **Court Martial** (military court). That power belongs exclusively to the President under `Article 72(1)(a)`.
- **No Power to PARDON a Death Sentence — Yet CAN Suspend, Remit, or Commute It!** Even when a death sentence is awarded under a law falling within the State's executive competence (such as murder under `Section 302 IPC` / `Section 103 BNS`), the Governor **CANNOT grant a full pardon** of a death sentence; **only the President** can pardon a death sentence (`Article 72(1)(c)`). **HOWEVER**, **`Article 72(3)`** expressly saves the Governor's power under any law (and `Article 161`) to **suspend, remit, or commute a death sentence**! In ***State of Haryana v. Jagdish* (2010)** and ***State of Haryana v. Raj Kumar @ Bittu* (August 2021)**, the Supreme Court reaffirmed that the Governor's constitutional power under `Article 161` to remit or commute sentences—including death sentences—**overrides statutory restrictions** such as the 14-year minimum imprisonment bar under `Section 433A CrPC` (now `Section 475 BNSS`), even before a prisoner completes 14 years of actual incarceration.

#### Binding Cabinet Advice and Judicial Review of Article 161

Like the President under `Article 72`, the Governor does **not** exercise `Article 161` in personal discretion; the Governor is **constitutionally bound by the aid and advice of the State Council of Ministers** (***Maru Ram v. Union of India*, 1980**, five-judge Bench). In ***Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh* (2006)**, the Supreme Court clarified that clemency orders under `Articles 72 and 161` are subject to **limited judicial review** where the decision is passed without application of mind, is *mala fide*, rests on extraneous or wholly irrelevant considerations (such as caste, religion, or political loyalty), keeps relevant material out of sight, or suffers from arbitrariness.

Most decisively, in ***A.G. Perarivalan v. State of Tamil Nadu* (18 May 2022, 3-Judge Bench)**—concerning the 2018 Tamil Nadu Cabinet recommendation to remit the life sentence of a Rajiv Gandhi assassination convict whose death penalty had earlier been commuted—the Supreme Court ruled that the Governor's **over two-year delay** and subsequent **unilateral reference of the State Cabinet's `Article 161` recommendation to the President** was **contrary to the Constitution**. Where an offence falls within the State's executive competence, the State Cabinet's advice under `Article 161` is **binding on the Governor**, who has **no authority to refer a remission petition to the President** as a delaying tactic.

```compare
# Pardoning Power: President (Article 72) vs Governor (Article 161)
President of India (Article 72) | Governor of a State (Article 161)
Subject-Matter Jurisdiction :: Offences against **Union laws** (Union List & Union-executed Concurrent laws) | Offences against **State laws** (State executive competence under `Art. 162`)
Death Sentence (Full Pardon) :: **CAN grant full pardon** (exclusive power under `Art. 72(1)(c)`) | **CANNOT pardon** a death sentence (even under a State law!)
Death Sentence (Suspend / Remit / Commute) :: **CAN** suspend, remit, or commute | **CAN** suspend, remit, or commute (saved by `Art. 72(3)`; overrides 14-yr `Sec. 433A CrPC` / `Sec. 475 BNSS` floor)

```

#### Judicial Appointments Role

Beyond clemency, the Governor plays three formal roles in the State judicial apparatus:
1. **High Court Judges (`Article 217(1)`):** The President consults the Governor of the concerned State (alongside the Chief Justice of India and the Chief Justice of that High Court) when appointing Judges of the State High Court. *(Prelims trap from 2013 PYQ: High Court judges are **appointed by the President**, NEVER by the Governor!)*
2. **District Judges (`Article 233`):** Appointments, postings, and promotions of **District Judges** in any State are made by the Governor in **consultation with the High Court** exercising jurisdiction in the State.
3. **Subordinate Judicial Service (`Article 234`):** Appointments of persons other than District Judges to the **Judicial Service of the State** are made by the Governor in accordance with rules framed after **consultation with the State Public Service Commission and the High Court**.

### Ordinance-Making Power (Article 213)

Modelled closely on `Section 88` of the Government of India Act, 1935 and running parallel to the President's `Article 123`, **`Article 213`** vests the Governor with an extraordinary legislative power to **promulgate Ordinances during the recess of the State Legislature**. Its legal parameters are tightly structured:

- **Triggering Conditions (`Article 213(1)`):** An Ordinance can be promulgated only when the **Legislative Assembly** is not in session (or, in a **bicameral State**, when **both Houses OR even one of the two Houses** is not in session—since a Bill requires passage by both Houses, an Ordinance can validly be issued when only one House is sitting!), AND the Governor is **satisfied that circumstances exist which render it necessary to take immediate action**.
- **Not a Discretionary Power:** Just as the President acts on Union Cabinet advice under `Article 123`, the Governor exercises ordinance-making power strictly on the **aid and advice of the State Council of Ministers** (***Shamsher Singh*, 1974**).
- **Co-extensive Legislative Reach (`Article 213(3)`):** An Ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of the State Legislature, and is subject to the exact same constitutional limits: it can cover only subjects in **List II (State List)** and **List III (Concurrent List)**, cannot violate Fundamental Rights, may be retrospective or modify/repeal existing State Acts or even a tax law, and **cannot amend the Constitution**.
- **Lifespan and Withdrawal (`Article 213(2)`):** Every Ordinance **must be laid before the Legislative Assembly** (or both Houses in bicameral States) upon reassembly. It ceases to operate at the expiration of **six weeks from the reassembly** of the Legislature (in a bicameral State, six weeks are reckoned from the **later** of the two reassembly dates!). It ceases earlier if a resolution disapproving it is passed by the Assembly (and agreed to by the Council, if any). Since the maximum permissible gap between two sessions is six months (`Article 174`), the **maximum possible life** of an unapproved Ordinance is **six months and six weeks**. The Governor may also **withdraw** an Ordinance at any time on ministerial advice.

#### Three Specific Situations Requiring Prior Presidential Instructions (Proviso to Article 213(1))

This is one of the highest-yield UPSC Prelims distinctions between `Article 123` and `Article 213`. While the President needs no external permission to issue an Ordinance, the **proviso to `Article 213(1)`** strictly prohibits the Governor from promulgating an Ordinance **without instructions from the President** in **three specific circumstances**:
1. **Previous Presidential Sanction Required:** If a Bill containing the same provisions would under the Constitution have required the **previous sanction of the President** for its introduction into the State Legislature (for example, a Bill restricting freedom of inter-state trade under **`Article 304(b)`**).
2. **Bill Would Have Been Reserved:** If the Governor would have **deemed it necessary to reserve** a Bill containing the same provisions for the consideration of the President (for instance, a measure derogating from High Court powers under the second proviso to `Article 200`).
3. **Act Would Be Invalid Without Presidential Assent:** If an Act of the State Legislature containing the same provisions would under the Constitution have been **invalid unless, having been reserved for the President, it had received the President's assent** (for example, a State law on a Concurrent List entry repugnant to an existing Central law under **`Article 254(2)`**, or laws requiring Presidential assent under `Articles 31A, 31C, or 288(2)`).

#### Judicial Curbs on "Ordinance Raj"

Because State Governments routinely succumbed to governing by executive decree rather than facing Assembly debate—exemplified by Bihar promulgating **256 Ordinances between 1967 and 1981** (some kept alive for up to 14 years through serial re-promulgation)—the Supreme Court erected formidable constitutional guardrails:
- ***R.C. Cooper v. Union of India* (1970)** and ***A.K. Roy v. Union of India* (1982):** Confirmed that the President's/Governor's satisfaction regarding "immediate action" is not entirely immune from judicial scrutiny if shown to be *mala fide* (after the **`44th Amendment Act, 1978`** deleted the `38th Amendment`'s ouster clause that had made satisfaction non-justiciable!).
- ***D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar* (1987, 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** Struck down Bihar's practice of repeatedly re-promulgating Ordinances without placing them before the Legislature, condemning colourable re-promulgation as a **"fraud on the Constitution"** and a subversion of democratic lawmaking.
- ***Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar* (2 January 2017, 7-Judge Constitution Bench):** Significantly hardened *D.C. Wadhwa* by holding: (i) **laying an Ordinance before the Legislature is mandatory**, and deliberate failure to lay it constitutes a serious constitutional infraction; (ii) **re-promulgation of Ordinances is constitutionally impermissible** and a fraud on the Constitution; (iii) the Governor's satisfaction is **open to judicial review** if tainted by *mala fides* or oblique motive; and (iv) **rights, liabilities, or entitlements created by an Ordinance do NOT automatically survive after the Ordinance lapses**, unless reversing them would be impossible or contrary to overwhelming public interest (overruling *T. Venkata Reddy*, 1985!). Despite *Krishna Kumar Singh*, PRS Legislative Research's *Annual Review of State Laws 2025* (May 2026) documented **127 State Ordinances promulgated in 2025** (up 27 per cent from 100 in 2024), underscoring persistent executive shortcuts at the State level.

## 3. Discretion of the Governor, Constitutional Flashpoints and Reform Commissions

Few phrases in Indian constitutional law have generated more political heat, federal friction, and Constitution Bench litigation than **"in his discretion"** under **`Article 163`**. To evaluate gubernatorial conduct objectively for both Prelims and Mains, one must begin with the deliberate textual divergence the Constituent Assembly built between **`Article 74`** (governing the President) and **`Article 163`** (governing the Governor).

### Constitutional Position and Article 163: Governor vs President

The constitutional position of the Governor rests on three interlocking provisions—`Article 154` (vesting State executive power in the Governor), `Article 163` (creating the State Council of Ministers to aid and advise the Governor), and `Article 164(2)` (making the Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the State Legislative Assembly). Together, these clauses firmly establish a **parliamentary form of government** in the States where real executive power belongs to the elected Cabinet.

Yet the Constitution intentionally draws a sharper distinction in `Article 163` than in `Article 74`:
- **Express Constitutional Discretion (`Article 163(1)`):** *"There shall be a Council of Ministers with the Chief Minister at the head to aid and advise the Governor in the exercise of his functions, **except in so far as he is by or under this Constitution required to exercise his functions or any of them in his discretion**."* By contrast, **`Article 74(1)` contains NO such discretionary exception** for the President of India (**UPSC Prelims 2025 PYQ**).
- **The Finality Clause (`Article 163(2)`):** *"If any question arises whether any matter is or is not a matter as respects which the Governor is by or under this Constitution required to act in his discretion, **the decision of the Governor in his discretion shall be final**, and the validity of anything done by the Governor shall not be called in question on the ground that he ought or ought not to have acted in his discretion."* No comparable finality clause exists for the President.
- **Asymmetry of the 42nd and 44th Amendments:** While the **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`** amended `Article 74(1)` to make Union ministerial advice **explicitly binding** on the President—and the **`44th Amendment Act, 1978`** inserted the proviso permitting a single return for reconsideration—**neither Amendment altered `Article 163`**! In practice, however, judicial doctrine (*Shamsher Singh*, 1974) confirms that outside the narrow constitutional pockets of express or situational discretion, the Governor is just as constitutionally bound by ministerial advice as the President.
- **No Unchecked Prerogative:** Crucially, the Supreme Court in ***Nabam Rebia* (2016)** demolished the colonial misconception that `Article 163(2)` grants the Governor an all-encompassing, unreviewable discretionary zone. The finality of `Article 163(2)` applies **only** to domains genuinely falling within express or necessary constitutional discretion; if a Governor usurps discretion where none exists—or exercises it arbitrarily or *mala fide*—the action remains squarely open to **judicial review**.

### Constitutional Discretion vs Situational Discretion

Constitutional scholars and the Supreme Court divide gubernatorial discretion into two distinct analytical categories: **Constitutional (Express) Discretion**, arising from specific textual mandates in the Constitution, and **Situational (Circumstantial) Discretion**, arising from unforeseen political deadlocks or exceptional constitutional breakdown (**UPSC Prelims 2014 PYQ**).

#### Express Constitutional Discretion (Six Core Domains)

1. **Assent and Reservation of State Bills (`Article 200`):** Deciding whether to assent to a Bill, withhold and return a non-Money Bill, or reserve it for the consideration of the President (including mandatory reservation under the second proviso where High Court powers are endangered). As authoritatively reaffirmed by the five-judge Constitution Bench on **20 November 2025 (`Special Reference No. 1 of 2025`)**, choosing among the three `Article 200` options is an exercise of **independent constitutional discretion** not bound by ministerial advice.
2. **Recommending President's Rule (`Article 356`):** Assessing whether a breakdown of constitutional machinery has occurred in the State and sending a report to the President recommending the imposition of President's Rule—an assessment that obviously cannot depend on the advice of the very State Cabinet facing dismissal, yet remains subject to judicial review (*S.R. Bommai*, 1994; *Rameshwar Prasad*, 2006).
3. **Administering an Adjoining Union Territory (`Article 239(2)`):** Whenever the President appoints the Governor of a State as the Administrator of an adjoining Union Territory, the Governor exercises those UT functions **independently of the State Council of Ministers**.
4. **Sixth Schedule Mineral Royalties (`Paragraph 9(2), Sixth Schedule`):** In the four Sixth Schedule States of **Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram**, if a dispute arises over the share of royalties payable by the State Government to an Autonomous District Council (ADC) from licences or leases for mineral extraction, the amount determined by the Governor **in their discretion** is final.
5. **Seeking Administrative and Legislative Information (`Article 167`):** Calling for information from the Chief Minister regarding the administration of State affairs and legislative proposals, or requiring an individual minister's decision to be placed before the full Council of Ministers.
6. **Special Responsibilities Under Part XXI (`Articles 371–371J`) and Presidential Directions:** Under several asymmetric federal provisions, the Governor discharges **special responsibilities**—**after consulting the State Council of Ministers, yet ultimately acting in individual judgment** (meaning ministerial advice is not binding):
   - **Maharashtra and Gujarat (`Article 371(2)`):** Establishment of separate development boards for Vidarbha, Marathwada, and the rest of Maharashtra, and for Saurashtra, Kutch, and the rest of Gujarat.
   - **Nagaland (`Article 371A(1)(b)`):** Special responsibility with respect to **law and order** in Nagaland so long as internal disturbances continue.
   - **Assam (`Article 371B`):** Administration of tribal areas and committees of the Legislative Assembly from tribal regions.
   - **Manipur (`Article 371C`):** Securing the proper functioning of the **Hill Areas Committee** of the Legislative Assembly and submitting annual reports to the President.
   - **Sikkim (`Article 371F(g)`):** Special responsibility for **peace** and for an **equitable arrangement** for ensuring the social and economic advancement of different sections of the population.
   - **Arunachal Pradesh (`Article 371H(a)`):** Special responsibility with respect to **law and order** in Arunachal Pradesh.
   - **Karnataka (`Article 371J`, inserted by `98th Amendment Act, 2012`):** Establishment of a separate development board for the **Hyderabad-Karnataka (now Kalyana-Karnataka)** region and equitable allocation of funds, education, and public employment.

#### Situational (Circumstantial) Discretion

Even where the constitutional text mentions no discretion, the Governor—just like the President—must exercise calibrated situational discretion in five extraordinary political contingencies:
1. **Appointment of Chief Minister in a Hung Assembly (or Sudden Death of Incumbent CM):** When no single party or pre-poll alliance commands an absolute majority after elections, or when a sitting Chief Minister dies suddenly without an immediately recognised successor.
2. **Dismissal of a Council of Ministers Losing Confidence:** When a Ministry demonstrably loses the confidence of the Legislative Assembly—by losing a confidence/no-confidence vote or refusing to face a floor test—and still refuses to resign.
3. **Dissolution of the Legislative Assembly:** Deciding whether to accept or reject a request for premature dissolution tendered by a Chief Minister who has already **lost majority support** in the Assembly (whereas the advice of a majority Chief Minister to dissolve is normally binding).
4. **Calling a Floor Test on Objective Material:** Directing a sitting government to prove its majority on the floor of the House when credible, objective *prima facie* evidence indicates a loss of legislative confidence (*Shivraj Singh Chouhan*, 2020)—but **NEVER** merely to adjudicate an internal party faction fight (*Subhash Desai*, 2023).
5. **Sanctioning Prosecution of Ministers Against Biased Cabinet Advice:** In ***M.P. Special Police Establishment v. State of Madhya Pradesh* (2004, 5-Judge Constitution Bench)**, the Supreme Court carved out a vital accountability exception: where the State Council of Ministers refuses sanction to prosecute serving Ministers despite compelling *prima facie* corruption evidence gathered by the Lokayukta—rendering ministerial advice palpably irrational, biased, or tainted by conflict of interest—the Governor may exercise discretion to **grant prosecution sanction contrary to Cabinet advice**.

> [!example]
> **Hung Assemblies and the Sarkaria 4-Step Preference Order**
> To curb arbitrary gubernatorial invitations—which sparked intense litigation in **Goa (2017)**, **Manipur (2017)**, **Karnataka (2018 — B.S. Yediyurappa given 15 days, trimmed by SC to 24 hours)**, and **Maharashtra (2019 — dawn swearing-in undone by SC's 24-hour floor test)**—the **Sarkaria (1988)** and **Punchhi (2010)** Commissions prescribed a strict four-tier order of invitation:
> 1. **Largest Pre-Poll Alliance** (treated as a single political formation);
> 2. **Single Largest Party** claiming majority with the support of others (including independents);
> 3. **Post-Poll Coalition** where all partner parties join the government;
> 4. **Post-Poll Coalition** with some partners joining government and others offering outside support.
> In **every** case, majority must be verified exclusively via a **time-bound, televised Floor Test in the Assembly**—never by head-counts in Raj Bhavan (*S.R. Bommai*, 1994).

### Landmark Supreme Court Jurisprudence on Gubernatorial Authority

Over half a century, a formidable chain of Constitution Bench verdicts has progressively fenced in gubernatorial overreach and reconciled the colonial-origin office of Governor with India's democratic federalism:

1. ***Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab* (1974, 7-Judge Constitution Bench):** Settled the foundational rule of Indian parliamentary executive law. Chief Justice A.N. Ray and Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer declared that both the President and the Governor are **formal constitutional heads** whose "satisfaction" in law means the **satisfaction of the Council of Ministers**—except in the narrow pockets where the Constitution expressly requires the Governor to act in discretion. Justice Krishna Iyer memorably cautioned that treating the Governor as a rival executive would *"introduce a dangerous constitutional aberration."*
2. ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994, 9-Judge Constitution Bench):** Transformed Centre–State federalism by declaring **federalism part of the Basic Structure**, making `Article 356` proclamations (and the Governor's underlying report) open to **judicial review** for *mala fides* or irrelevant material, barring Assembly dissolution prior to Parliamentary approval, and laying down the cardinal parliamentary rule that a Ministry's majority must **invariably be tested on the floor of the Legislative Assembly, NEVER in the private chambers of Raj Bhavan**.
3. ***Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India* (2006, 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** Struck down the May 2005 dissolution of the newly elected Bihar Legislative Assembly (which had never even met!) based on Governor Buta Singh's unverified reports alleging horse-trading to prevent a rival alliance from staking a claim. Holding the Governor's report to be **personal *ipse dixit* tainted by extraneous political motives**, the Bench clarified that **`Article 361` immunity does not bar judicial review** of *mala fide* gubernatorial actions.
4. ***Nabam Rebia and Bamang Felix v. Deputy Speaker, Arunachal Pradesh Legislative Assembly* (13 July 2016, 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** In one of the sharpest rebukes to Raj Bhavan activism, the Court restored the dismissed Arunachal Pradesh government after Governor J.P. Rajkhowa unilaterally **advanced an Assembly session** and **dictated its legislative agenda** (prioritising a resolution to remove the Speaker) without ministerial advice. The Bench held unequivocally:
   - While a Council of Ministers enjoys Assembly confidence, the Governor **CANNOT unilaterally summon, advance, or prorogue the Assembly (`Article 174`) or send messages fixing House business (`Article 175`)** against Cabinet advice.
   - The Governor's discretion under `Article 163(1)–(2)` is **strictly circumscribed**, NOT all-pervasive, and any arbitrary usurpation of discretion is subject to **judicial review**.
   - The Governor has **no role whatsoever** in Tenth Schedule disqualification disputes or internal proceedings for removing the Speaker.
5. ***Shivraj Singh Chouhan v. Speaker, Madhya Pradesh Legislative Assembly* (13 April 2020, 2-Judge Bench):** Following the resignation of 22 Congress MLAs in Madhya Pradesh, the Supreme Court upheld the Governor's authority to **direct a floor test even in a running Assembly** whenever objective *prima facie* material indicates the incumbent government may have lost its majority—emphasising, however, that the Governor's satisfaction must rest on **objective, relevant material** and remains subject to judicial review so that it is never weaponised to destabilise an elected government.
6. ***Subhash Desai v. Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra* (11 May 2023, 5-Judge Constitution Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud):** Reviewing the June 2022 Maharashtra political crisis, the Bench held that Governor Bhagat Singh Koshyari acted **illegally** in summoning a floor test merely because a faction of Shiv Sena MLAs (led by Eknath Shinde) expressed internal dissatisfaction with Chief Minister Uddhav Thackeray. The Court laid down a bright constitutional prohibition: **a Governor CANNOT enter intra-party disputes or use a floor test to resolve factional splits within a political party** without objective material showing withdrawal of legislative support to the government! However, the Court declined to restore the Maha Vikas Aghadi government solely because Chief Minister Thackeray had **voluntarily resigned** before facing the floor test.

### Contemporary Governor–State Friction Points (2016–2026)

In the decade between 2016 and 2026, constitutional friction between Raj Bhavans and Opposition-ruled State Governments—most acutely in **Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Punjab, West Bengal, and Telangana**—shifted away from traditional `Article 356` dismissals (now tightly deterred by *Bommai*) toward subtler "everyday governance" choke points:
- **Legislative Stalling (`Article 200`):** Keeping duly passed State Bills pending for months or years without assent, return, or reservation—directly precipitating the 2023 *Punjab*, April 2025 *Tamil Nadu*, and November 2025 *Special Reference* verdicts.
- **University Chancellor Battles:** Using statutory Chancellor powers outside `Article 163` advice to block State-recommended Vice-Chancellors, appoint interim heads, or stall State Bills transferring Chancellorship to Chief Ministers or academicians.
- **Disputes Over the `Article 176` Special Address:** Publicly editing, omitting, or walking out of Cabinet-approved policy paragraphs during the opening address to the Assembly, eroding the settled convention that the Governor speaks as the constitutional voice of the elected Ministry.
- **Ministerial Swearing-In and Unilateral Dismissal Attempts:** Attempting to withhold oath from a minister recommended by the Chief Minister or purporting to unilaterally "dismiss" a serving minister (as briefly attempted in Tamil Nadu in June 2023 before being kept in abeyance), directly clashing with `Article 164(1)` under which the Governor's pleasure over ministers is exercised exclusively on the Chief Minister's advice.
- **Shortened Tenures and Partisan Transfers:** With Professor Ashok Pankaj's 1950–2015 dataset showing only **18 per cent** of Governors completing a full five-year term—and active political leaders routinely appointed without prior Chief Minister consultation—Raj Bhavans increasingly struggle to project the neutral constitutional stature envisioned by Dr. Ambedkar and Jawaharlal Nehru.

### Reform Commissions and Institutional Blueprint

Over nearly six decades, **five authoritative national and State commissions**—supplemented in February 2026 by Tamil Nadu's **Justice Kurian Joseph Committee**—have produced a remarkably convergent blueprint to depoliticise the office of Governor while preserving India's federal unity:

1. **First ARC (1966–69; Report on Centre–State Relationships, 1969):** Assisted by the **M.C. Setalvad Study Team** and chaired successively by Morarji Desai and K. Hanumanthaiah, it urged appointing eminent public figures **in consultation with the Chief Minister**, limiting Governors to a **single non-renewable term** (eliminating reappointment incentives), empowering the **Inter-State Council** to frame guidelines codifying discretion, and ensuring objective `Article 356` reporting.
2. **Rajamannar Committee (Tamil Nadu, 1969–71; Dr. P.V. Rajamannar):** Advocated **mandatory State Cabinet consultation** (or a high-powered impartial body); secure tenure removable **only for proved misbehaviour or incapacity after Supreme Court inquiry**; post-term disqualification from government office; an **Instrument of Instructions**; **deleting "during the pleasure of the Governor" from `Article 164(1)`**; and **abolishing or drastically curtailing `Articles 356, 357, and 365`**.
3. **Sarkaria Commission (1983–88; Justice R.S. Sarkaria):** Set the celebrated **four-point appointee criteria (UPSC Prelims 2019 PYQ)**—**(i) eminent person in some walk of life**, **(ii) outsider to the State**, **(iii) detached from local politics**, and **(iv) not recently active in politics**. It urged **amending `Article 155`** for mandatory CM consultation (plus consulting the VP and LS Speaker), protecting the **five-year term** unless compelling grounds arise (with an explanatory statement to Parliament), barring return to active partisan politics (except VP/President), codifying the **four-step hung-Assembly order** with a **30-day floor test** (60 days max), using `Article 356` as a **last resort** with a "speaking" report, and recording Presidential reasons when vetoing State Bills.
4. **Venkatachaliah Commission / NCRWC (2000–02):** Recommended a **four-member selection panel** (**PM, Union Home Minister, LS Speaker, State CM**); a protected **five-year term** with prior CM consultation before premature removal/transfer; a **six-month cap** for the Governor (`Article 200`) and **three-month cap** for the President (`Article 201`); and mandatory House floor tests.
5. **Punchhi Commission (2007–10; former CJI M.M. Punchhi):** Recommended **deleting "during the pleasure of the President" from `Article 156`** for a **fixed five-year tenure** removable only via **impeachment by the State Legislature** (mirroring `Article 61`); a formal selection **collegium** (PM, Home Minister, LS Speaker, State CM, plus VP); a **two-year cooling-off period** from active politics; a **six-month deadline** on Bills (`Article 200`); **abolishing the statutory University Chancellor role**; and codified hung-Assembly and prosecution-sanction norms.
6. **Justice Kurian Joseph Committee (Tamil Nadu; Part I Report tabled 18 February 2026):** Proposed amending `Article 155` so the **President must pick one of three eminent outsiders endorsed by a majority of the State Assembly's total membership**; a **single non-renewable five-year term** removable only by total-membership Assembly resolution binding the President within 14 days (with the **HC Chief Justice officiating** upon expiry); a **five-year cooling-off period** (`Article 157`); and a **Thirteenth Schedule (Instrument of Instructions)**.

## 4. The Chief Minister and State Council of Ministers

In the parliamentary scheme designed by `Part VI` of the Constitution, the **Governor** is the nominal (*de jure*) executive authority (Head of the State), whereas the **Chief Minister** is the real (*de facto*) executive authority (Head of the Government). Positioned at the exact pivot of State governance under **`Articles 163, 164, 166, 167, and 177`**, the Chief Minister mirrors the Prime Minister at the Centre—anchoring the formation, collective discipline, policy direction, and survival of the State Council of Ministers.

### The Chief Minister: Appointment, Qualifications, Oath and Tenure

#### Appointment and Legislative Membership Rules (Article 164)

Just as `Article 75` contains no elaborate procedure for selecting the Prime Minister, **`Article 164(1)`** states simply that **"the Chief Minister shall be appointed by the Governor"** and the other Ministers shall be appointed by the Governor on the advice of the Chief Minister. By binding parliamentary convention, the Governor is obliged to appoint the leader of the **majority party** (or majority pre-poll alliance) in the **State Legislative Assembly**. Situational discretion arises only in a **hung Assembly** (governed by the Sarkaria four-step preference sequence and a time-bound floor test—including a **composite floor test** between rival claimants as ordered by the Supreme Court in ***Jagdambika Pal v. Union of India*, 1998**) or upon the sudden death of an incumbent Chief Minister when the ruling party has no immediate successor.

A crucial Prelims distinction separates States from Union Territories with legislatures (**UPSC Prelims 2013 PYQ**): while the **Governor** appoints the Chief Minister of every State under `Article 164(1)`, the Chief Minister of the **National Capital Territory of Delhi** (`Article 239AA(5)`) and the Chief Minister of **Puducherry** (`Section 45` of the *Government of Union Territories Act, 1963*) are appointed by the **President of India**, **NOT** by the Lieutenant Governor!

Unlike the British convention confining the Prime Minister to the lower House, a Chief Minister in a **bicameral State** may belong to **either the Legislative Assembly or the Legislative Council**—as demonstrated by C. Rajagopalachari (Madras, 1952), Morarji Desai (Bombay, 1952), B.P. Mandal (Bihar, 1968), Prithviraj Chavan (Maharashtra, 2010), Nitish Kumar (Bihar), Yogi Adityanath (Uttar Pradesh, first term 2017–22), and Uddhav Thackeray (Maharashtra, 2019–22). Even more remarkably, under **`Article 164(4)`**, a **non-member of the State Legislature** may be appointed Chief Minister or Minister for a maximum period of **six consecutive months**, failing which they automatically cease to hold ministerial office upon expiry of that six-month window unless elected or nominated to either House in the interim.

#### Judicial Safeguards Against Abuse of Article 164(4): S.R. Chaudhuri (2001) and B.R. Kapur (2001)

Because political parties repeatedly tried to exploit `Article 164(4)` to bypass electoral verdicts or criminal disqualifications, the Supreme Court closed both loopholes in two landmark 2001 judgments:
1. ***S.R. Chaudhuri v. State of Punjab* (17 August 2001, 3-Judge Bench):** While reaffirming (*Har Sharan Verma*, 1971) that a genuinely qualified non-legislator may be appointed once for six months, the Court held that a non-member who **fails to get elected within six months CANNOT be re-appointed** as Chief Minister or Minister after a brief interval during the life of the **same Legislative Assembly**. Serial back-to-back six-month appointments without facing voters subvert representative democracy and make a mockery of `Article 164(4)`.
2. ***B.R. Kapur v. State of Tamil Nadu* (21 September 2001, 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** After AIADMK leader J. Jayalalithaa—who stood convicted and sentenced to more than two years' imprisonment in the TANSI land deal case, disqualifying her from contesting elections under `Section 8(3)` of the *Representation of the People Act, 1951* read with `Article 191(1)(e)`—was nevertheless sworn in as Chief Minister by Governor Fathima Beevi following her party's sweeping May 2001 Assembly victory, a five-judge Bench led by Justice S.P. Bharucha **quashed her appointment via a writ of *quo warranto***. The Court held unequivocally that a non-legislator appointed under `Article 164(4)` **must satisfy all qualifications for legislative membership under `Article 173` and must NOT suffer any disqualification under `Article 191` on the date of appointment**. Even an overwhelming legislative majority cannot override the Constitution's disqualification bar; the Governor is constitutionally barred from swearing in a disqualified person as Chief Minister.

> - **Constitutional Principle:** Popular or party majority operates *within*, never *above*, the written Constitution.

#### Oath, Tenure and Emoluments of the Chief Minister

Before entering upon office, the Governor administers to the Chief Minister (and every Minister) the **Oath of Office** and the **Oath of Secrecy** prescribed in the **`Third Schedule`** (`Article 164(3)`). Under the Oath of Office, the Chief Minister swears to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution, uphold India's sovereignty and integrity, faithfully and conscientiously discharge duties, and do right to all manner of people without fear or favour, affection or ill-will; under the Oath of Secrecy, the Chief Minister swears not to directly or indirectly communicate or reveal any matter brought under consideration except as required for due discharge of ministerial duties.

The term of the Chief Minister is **not fixed**; formally, the Chief Minister holds office **"during the pleasure of the Governor"** (`Article 164(1)`). In constitutional substance, however, the Governor **cannot dismiss a Chief Minister so long as they enjoy majority support in the Legislative Assembly**—a Governor may dismiss the Chief Minister only if the Ministry loses Assembly confidence and refuses to resign. The **salary and allowances** of the Chief Minister and Ministers are determined from time to time by the **State Legislature** (`Article 164(5)`), NOT by Parliament (contrast the Governor's salary fixed by Parliament!).

### Powers, Functions and Federal Role of the Chief Minister

As the undisputed engine of State administration, the Chief Minister exercises four interlocking clusters of powers and functions:

1. **In Relation to the State Council of Ministers:**
   - Recommends persons to be appointed as Ministers by the Governor (the Governor can appoint only those recommended by the CM).
   - Allocates and reshuffles ministerial portfolios; presides over meetings of the Council of Ministers and shapes all collective decisions.
   - Guides, directs, controls, and coordinates the activities of all departments; may ask any dissenting or non-performing Minister to resign or advise the Governor to dismiss them.
   - **Keystone Effect:** Because the Chief Minister heads the Council, **the resignation or death of the Chief Minister automatically dissolves the entire Council of Ministers**—whereas the resignation or death of any other Minister merely creates a single vacancy that the Chief Minister may or may not fill.
2. **In Relation to the Governor (`Article 167`):**
   - Functions as the principal constitutional channel of communication between the Governor and the Council of Ministers. Mirroring `Article 78` at the Union level, **`Article 167`** imposes **three explicit constitutional duties** on the Chief Minister:
     - **`Article 167(a)`:** To **communicate to the Governor all decisions** of the Council of Ministers relating to the administration of State affairs and proposals for legislation;
     - **`Article 167(b)`:** To **furnish such information** relating to State administration and legislative proposals as the Governor may call for; and
     - **`Article 167(c)`:** If the Governor so requires, to **submit for the consideration of the Council of Ministers** any matter on which a decision has been taken by a Minister individually but which has not been considered by the Council (reinforcing collective responsibility!).
   - Advises the Governor on major State constitutional appointments, including the **Advocate General**, the **Chairman and Members of the SPSC**, and the **State Election Commissioner**.
3. **In Relation to the State Legislature:**
   - Advises the Governor with regard to **summoning and proroguing** sessions of the State Legislature (`Article 174`) and may recommend the **dissolution of the Legislative Assembly** to the Governor at any time while commanding a majority.
   - Announces major government policies on the floor of the House and defends the administration's legislative and budgetary programme.
4. **Executive, Planning and Cooperative Federalism Roles:**
   - Serves as **Chairman of the State Planning Board**.
   - Acts as **Vice-Chairman of the concerned Zonal Council by rotation**, holding office for a period of **one year at a time** (with the Union Home Minister as Chairman under the *States Reorganisation Act, 1956*).
   - Represents the State as a full member of the **Inter-State Council** (`Article 263`) and the **Governing Council of NITI Aayog** (both chaired by the Prime Minister), as well as in the **National Development Council** (where convened).
   - Acts as the **chief spokesperson** of the State Government, the **crisis manager-in-chief** during natural disasters or law-and-order emergencies, and the **political head of the State civil services** (working closely with the Chief Secretary).

### State Council of Ministers: Constitutional Architecture (Articles 163, 164, 166, 167 and 177)

The institutional rules governing the **State Council of Ministers** are embedded across **five core Articles** of Part VI: **`Article 163`** (Council to aid and advise Governor + discretionary finality + non-inquirability of advice), **`Article 164`** (appointment, size cap, Tenth Schedule bar, collective responsibility, oath, six-month rule, salaries), **`Article 166`** (conduct of executive business in Governor's name), **`Article 167`** (Chief Minister's duties toward Governor), and **`Article 177`** (legislative participation rights of Ministers and Advocate General).

#### The 91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003: Size Caps, Minimum Floor and Anti-Defection Bar

Before 2003, coalition compulsions and defection bargaining spawned unwieldy "jumbo cabinets" across Indian States—draining exchequers and turning ministerial berths into political bribes. Acting on recommendations of the Law Commission and the Venkatachaliah Commission (NCRWC), Parliament enacted the **`91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003`**, inserting two game-changing clauses into `Article 164`:
- **Maximum 15% Ceiling AND Minimum 12-Minister Floor (`Article 164(1A)`):** The total number of Ministers, **including the Chief Minister**, in the Council of Ministers in a State **shall NOT exceed 15 per cent** of the total strength of the **State Legislative Assembly** *(note: Legislative Council strength is NEVER counted in calculating the 15% cap!)*. **Crucially**, the first proviso mandates that the number of Ministers, **including the Chief Minister**, in a State **shall NOT be less than 12**! This constitutional floor protects small States whose 15% figure would fall below 12—such as **Sikkim** (32 MLAs, where 15% is 4.8 yet up to 12 ministers are permitted), **Goa** (40 MLAs), and **Mizoram** (40 MLAs). Contrast this sharply with the Union: **`Article 75(1A)` imposes a 15% cap on the Union Council of Ministers with NO minimum floor!**
- **Special 10% Cap for NCT of Delhi (`Article 239AA(4)`):** Under **`Article 239AA(4)`** (inserted by the `69th Amendment Act, 1991`), the Council of Ministers for the **National Capital Territory of Delhi** is capped at **not more than 10 per cent** of the total members of the Legislative Assembly—yielding a hard maximum of **7 Ministers (including the Chief Minister)** in Delhi's 70-member Assembly!
- **Tenth Schedule Ministerial Bar (`Article 164(1B)`):** A member of either House of a State Legislature belonging to any political party who is **disqualified on the ground of defection under the Tenth Schedule** is simultaneously **disqualified from being appointed as a Minister** from the date of disqualification until the date on which their legislative term expires OR the date on which they contest an election to either House and are declared elected, **whichever is earlier**.
- **Mandatory Tribal Welfare Minister (`Proviso to Article 164(1)`):** As noted in Section 2, the **`94th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2006`** updated this proviso so that a Tribal Welfare Minister is constitutionally mandatory in **Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, and Odisha** (dropping **Bihar**).

#### Nature of Ministerial Advice and Caretaker Ministries

Under **`Article 163(3)`**, the question whether any, and if so what, advice was tendered by Ministers to the Governor **shall not be inquired into in any court**. This mirrors `Article 74(2)` and preserves the candid, confidential character of Cabinet deliberations. Extending the Supreme Court's ruling in ***U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi* (1971)** to States, **`Article 163(1)` is mandatory at all times**: even after the dissolution of the State Legislative Assembly, the outgoing Council of Ministers does **not** cease to hold office until a new ministry is sworn in, functioning as a **caretaker government** strictly confined to routine administration (avoiding major new policy or financial commitments during elections).

#### Collective Responsibility, Individual Responsibility and Absence of Legal Responsibility

Ministerial accountability in the States rests on three classical Westminster pillars:
1. **Collective Responsibility (`Article 164(2)`):** The Council of Ministers is **collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly of the State ONLY**—**NEVER to the Legislative Council**! This bedrock rule means all Ministers **"swim or sink together"** as a unified team. If the Legislative Assembly passes a **No-Confidence Motion** (or defeats a Confidence Motion or Money Bill), **all Ministers must resign together**, including Ministers who happen to be members of the Legislative Council. Equally, Cabinet decisions bind every Minister: a Minister who disagrees with a Cabinet decision in private must still defend it publicly and inside the Legislature, or else resign (as B.R. Ambedkar and C.D. Deshmukh did at the Union level).
2. **Individual Responsibility (`Article 164(1)`):** Ministers hold office **"during the pleasure of the Governor,"** which in parliamentary practice means **at the pleasure of the Chief Minister**. The Governor may remove an individual Minister **only on the advice of the Chief Minister**—enabling the Chief Minister to enforce administrative competence, integrity, and policy cohesion across departments. A Governor cannot unilaterally dismiss a serving Minister against the Chief Minister's wishes.
3. **No Legal Responsibility:** Unlike the United Kingdom—where every public act of the Monarch requires the **countersignature of a Minister**, rendering that Minister personally liable in a court of law if the act proves unlawful—the Indian Constitution contains **no provision for ministerial countersignature** of the Governor's orders, and **`Article 163(3)`** expressly bars courts from inquiring into the nature of advice tendered by Ministers.

#### Rights of Ministers and the Advocate General in the Legislature (Article 177)

Mirroring `Article 88` for Parliament, **`Article 177`** grants every Minister and the **Advocate General of the State** the right to **speak in, and otherwise take part in the proceedings of**, the Legislative Assembly—and, in a bicameral State, **both Houses**—as well as any committee of the State Legislature of which they may be named a member. However, `Article 177` expressly clarifies that this right **does NOT entitle them to vote** in a House of which they are not a member: thus a Minister who is an MLC can speak and answer questions in the Legislative Assembly but can vote **only in the Legislative Council**, while the **Advocate General** cannot vote in **either** House!

### Composition, Hierarchy, State Cabinet and Parliamentary Secretaries

Although the Constitution does not specify ranks within the State Council of Ministers (referring only to the Chief Minister and "other Ministers"), parliamentary practice mirrors the Union's **three-tier ministerial hierarchy**:
- **Cabinet Ministers:** Senior political leaders heading the most critical State departments (Home, Finance, Revenue, Agriculture, Health, Education, Energy, Public Works). They are full members of the **State Cabinet**, attend all Cabinet meetings, and formulate core State policy.
- **Ministers of State (MoS):** May either be given **independent charge** of smaller departments (enjoying functional autonomy comparable to Cabinet Ministers, though attending Cabinet meetings only when invited for matters concerning their portfolio) or be **attached to Cabinet Ministers** to assist with specific wings of large departments.
- **Deputy Ministers:** Junior ministers never given independent charge; attached to Cabinet Ministers or Ministers of State to assist in administrative, legislative, and constituency duties. They are not members of the Cabinet.

Two extra-constitutional offices frequently encountered in Indian State politics require clear conceptual grounding:
- **Deputy Chief Minister:** An **extra-constitutional post** created purely for political coalition management, caste/regional representation, or party balancing (some States, such as Andhra Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Bihar, have appointed one or even multiple Deputy Chief Ministers simultaneously). Constitutionally, a Deputy Chief Minister holds the exact rank, oath, and status of a **Cabinet Minister**—possessing no separate constitutional powers and counting fully within the `15%` cap of `Article 164(1A)`.
- **Parliamentary Secretaries and *Bimolangshu Roy v. State of Assam* (2017):** After the `91st Amendment (2003)` enforced the `15%` ministerial cap, several States—including Assam, Delhi, West Bengal, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, and Manipur—attempted a political subterfuge by enacting State statutes appointing ruling-party MLAs as **"Parliamentary Secretaries"** with the rank, perks, official-file access, and status of Ministers of State while claiming they did not count toward the `Article 164(1A)` ceiling. On **26 July 2017**, in ***Bimolangshu Roy v. State of Assam***, the Supreme Court struck down Assam's 2004 Act as unconstitutional, holding that State Legislatures **lack legislative competence** under `Article 194(3)` and Entry 39 of List II to invent new quasi-ministerial offices. Following *Bimolangshu Roy*, multiple High Courts—including the **Himachal Pradesh High Court in November 2024**—have similarly quashed State Parliamentary Secretary laws as a colourable fraud circumventing `Article 164(1A)`.

#### State Cabinet and Cabinet Committees

Within the broader Council of Ministers (which rarely meets as a whole), the **State Cabinet**—a compact inner nucleus of 15–25 senior Cabinet Ministers chaired by the Chief Minister—serves as the **highest policy-making, executive-coordinating, legislative-steering, and crisis-management body** in the State. Operating entirely through extra-constitutional convention and **Rules of Business (`Article 166(3)`)**, the State Cabinet is assisted by **Standing and Ad-hoc Cabinet Committees** (for example, on Economic Affairs, Political Affairs, Infrastructure, or Drought Relief) chaired predominantly by the Chief Minister or senior Cabinet Ministers to expedite inter-departmental coordination.

## 1. The Governor: Appointment, Tenure and Constitutional Position

- **Part VI (`Arts. 153–167`):** State Executive = **Governor, CM, State CoM & Advocate General (`Art. 165`)**. **NO Vice-Governor** (President arranges contingencies under **`Art. 160`**, e.g., HC Chief Justice officiating).
- **Dual Role & 7th Amend (1956):** Both **Constitutional Head of State** and **Centre's vital link**. **`7th Amend, 1956`** added proviso to **`Art. 153`** (common Governor for **2+ States**) & **`Art. 158(3A)`** (**President** fixes salary share).
- **Canadian vs US Model (30–31 May 1949):** Constituent Assembly rejected direct election (Patel's early draft) for **6 reasons**: incompatible with parliamentary rule; rival mandate/friction with CM; wasteful cost for nominal head; political/personal campaign; partisan winner loses neutrality; fosters **separatist provincial tendencies** (Nehru). Adopted **Canadian model** of Presidential appointment (`Art. 155`: warrant under hand & seal).
- ***Hargovind Pant* (1979, 5-Judge):** **Independent constitutional office**, **NOT employment under GoI** (not barred by `Art. 319(d)`); neither subordinate nor subservient to Centre.
- **Qualifications (`Art. 157`) & Conventions:** Only **2 qualifications**: **Indian citizen + 35 years** (NO legislative eligibility required, unlike President/VP!). **2 conventions** (often breached): **outsider** + **CM consultation**.
- **Conditions (`Art. 158`) & Oath (`Art. 159`):** No MP/MLA/MLC, no office of profit, rent-free Raj Bhavan; salary **₹3,50,000/month** (fixed by **Parliament** — *Governors Act 1982* / *Finance Act 2018*), **charged on State Consolidated Fund (`Art. 202(3)(a)`)**, **non-diminishable (`Art. 158(4)`)** (2018 PYQ). **Oath (`Art. 159`)** by **HC Chief Justice** (directly in `Art. 159`, **NOT 3rd Schedule!**; *"preserve, protect and defend"*).
- **Immunities (`Art. 361`):** Not answerable for official acts; **absolute bar on criminal proceedings & arrest** during term (even personal acts); **personal civil suits** need **2 months' written notice**. *Rameshwar Prasad* (2006) & *Special Ref. No. 1* (20 Nov 2025): immunity is **personal**, NOT a shield for unconstitutional acts or prolonged office inaction!
- **Tenure & *B.P. Singhal* (2010, 5-Judge):** `Art. 156`: normal **5 yrs** (+ holdover proviso), resigns to President, holds office at **President's pleasure** — **NO impeachment, NO grounds** (2013 PYQ; Ashok Pankaj 1950–2015: only **18%** completed 5 yrs). *Singhal*: pleasure is **NOT arbitrary/unfettered**; **Union regime change or ideological mismatch INVALID**; compelling grounds required; **limited judicial review** for *mala fides*.
- **State Executive Reach (`Art. 162`):** Co-extensive with State Legislature (List III subject to Union); entrenched under **`Art. 368(2)` proviso** (half-State ratification; `Art. 158` is NOT — 2025 PYQ!).

## 2. Powers of the Governor and Comparison with the President

- **4 Domains / 3 Missing:** Executive, Legislative, Financial, Judicial — **NO diplomatic, military, or emergency proclamation powers** (only recommends `Art. 356`).
- **Executive (`Arts. 154, 166, 167`):** Formal State acts in Governor's name; Rules of Business (`Art. 166(3)`). Appoints CM, Ministers, **Advocate General (`Art. 165` — Governor's pleasure)**, **SEC (`Art. 243K` — removed ONLY like HC Judge!)**, **SPSC Chairman/Members (`Art. 316` — removed ONLY by President `Art. 317`; Governor only suspends pending SC inquiry!)**. Mandatory **Tribal Welfare Minister** in **CG, JH, MP & Odisha** (**`94th Amend, 2006`** dropped Bihar, added CG/JH). **University Chancellor** is **STATUTORY**, NOT constitutional (2022–2026 rows in TN, Kerala, WB, Punjab).
- **Legislative:** Integral part (`Art. 168`); summons/prorogues/dissolves (`Art. 174`); **Special Address (`Art. 176` — 2019 PYQ)**; messages (`Art. 175`); double-vacancy presiding appointments (`Arts. 180(1), 184(1)`); nominates **1/6th MLCs (`Art. 171(5)`)** from **5 fields: Literature, Science, Art, Cooperative Movement & Social Service** (RS has 4 — **no Cooperative Movement!**); Anglo-Indian seat (`Art. 333`) ended by **`104th Amend, 2019`**; decides `Art. 191` disqualifications on **binding ECI opinion (`Art. 192`)** (Tenth Schedule = Speaker/Chairman); lays SFC, SPSC & State CAG reports.
- **Bills (`Arts. 200–201`) & 2023–2025 Trilogy:** `Art. 200`: assent, reserve (**mandatory** if **HC powers** endangered under 2nd Proviso; also `Arts. 31A, 31C, 254(2), 288(2), 304(b)/255, 360(4)`), or withhold & return non-Money Bill *"as soon as possible"* (1st Proviso). Under **`Art. 201`**, Governor's role ends; President may assent, withhold, or return (6-month reconsideration), and is **NOT bound even if re-passed!** *(2025 PYQ: President CANNOT suo motu reserve State Bills!)*
  - ***Punjab* (10 Nov 2023):** No pocket veto; withholding must pair with return.
  - ***Tamil Nadu* (8 Apr 2025, 2-Judge, `2025 INSC 481`):** Voided reservation of 10 re-passed Bills; set **1–3 month timelines**; granted **`Art. 142` "deemed assent"**.
  - ***Special Reference No. 1 of 2025* (20 Nov 2025, 5-Judge Bench, `2025 INSC 1333`):** Answered 14 `Art. 143(1)` questions (Q12/14 unanswered; Q13 broad): **(1)** 3 `Art. 200` options (post-re-passage may assent OR reserve); **(2)** **independent constitutional discretion** (NOT bound by Cabinet advice); **(3)** `Arts. 200/201` merits **non-justiciable**; **(4)** **NO rigid judicial timelines** & **NO `Art. 142` "deemed assent"**; **(5)** no pre-enactment Bill review; `Art. 143` optional; **(6) limited mandamus for "prolonged, unexplained, and indefinite" inaction**, unimpeded by `Art. 361`!
- **Financial & Judicial (`Art. 161`):** Budget (`Art. 202`), Money Bills (`Art. 207`), Grants (`Art. 203(3)`), Contingency Fund (`Art. 267(2)`), SFC every 5 yrs (`Arts. 243-I/Y`). **`Art. 161` Pardon:** 5 modes + suspend; State laws only; **NO death-sentence pardon** (President ONLY) & **NO Court Martial** — **YET CAN suspend/remit/commute death sentence** (`Art. 72(3)`; overrides 14-yr `Sec. 433A CrPC`/`Sec. 475 BNSS` — *Raj Kumar* 2021)! Bound by State Cabinet (*Maru Ram*; ***Perarivalan* 2022**: cannot refer to President). Consulted on HC Judges (`Art. 217`); appoints District (`Art. 233`) & subordinate judges (`Art. 234`).
- **Ordinances (`Art. 213`):** Recess of Assembly (or either House); 6 weeks post-reassembly (max **6 months + 6 weeks**); Cabinet advice; mandatory laying & no re-promulgation (*D.C. Wadhwa* 1987; ***Krishna Kumar Singh* 2017**, 7-Judge). **3 cases needing Presidential instructions (`Art. 213(1)` proviso):** prior Presidential sanction (`Art. 304(b)`), Bill would have been reserved, or State Act invalid without Presidential assent (`Art. 254(2)`).

## 3. Discretion of the Governor, Constitutional Flashpoints and Reform Commissions

- **`Art. 163` vs `Art. 74`:** `Art. 163(1)–(2)` expressly confers discretion & finality (2025 PYQ; **untouched by 42nd/44th Amendments**!), whereas `Art. 74` gives President NO express discretion. Yet *Nabam Rebia* (2016) held `Art. 163(2)` is **NOT absolute** and judicially reviewable.
- **Constitutional Discretion (6):** `Art. 200` Bill options (Nov 2025); `Art. 356` report; adjoining UT (`Art. 239(2)`); **6th Schedule mineral royalties** in **AMTM (Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram)**; `Art. 167` info; **Special Responsibilities (`Arts. 371–371J`)** (individual judgment after consulting CoM: MH/GJ `371`, **Nagaland `371A` & Arunachal `371H` law & order**, Assam `371B`, Manipur `371C`, Sikkim `371F`, Karnataka `371J`). *(2014 PYQ: Appointing Ministers & `Art. 166` Rules are NOT discretionary!)*
- **Situational Discretion (5):** Hung Assembly CM (**Sarkaria 4-step order**: Pre-poll alliance → Single largest party → Post-poll coalition all in govt → Post-poll with outside support + Floor Test); dismissing CoM losing confidence; dissolution on minority CM advice; floor test on objective evidence; **prosecution sanction against biased Cabinet** (***M.P. SPE*, 2004**, 5-Judge).
- **Landmark Cases:** ***Shamsher Singh* (1974)** (binding advice); ***S.R. Bommai* (1994)** (Floor Test in Assembly only; `Art. 356` reviewable); ***Rameshwar Prasad* (2006)** (Bihar dissolution void; `Art. 361` limits); ***Nabam Rebia* (2016)** (cannot unilaterally summon/advance Assembly or set agenda against majority CoM); ***Shivraj Singh Chouhan* (2020)** (floor test in running Assembly on objective evidence); ***Subhash Desai* (2023)** (no floor test for intra-party splits; voluntary resignation bars restoration).
- **5 Commissions + 2026 Panel:**
  - **1st ARC (1969):** Eminent public figure, CM consultation, **single non-renewable term**, ISC guidelines.
  - **Rajamannar (1971, TN):** Mandatory State consultation, SC-inquiry removal, **delete pleasure from `Art. 164(1)`**, abolish/curtail `Arts. 356/357/365`.
  - **Sarkaria (1988):** **4 criteria (2019 PYQ)** — eminent, outsider, detached, no recent active politics; amend `Art. 155` for CM consult (+ VP/LS Speaker); normal 5-yr term; 4-step hung-Assembly rule & 30-day floor test; `Art. 356` last resort.
  - **NCRWC / Venkatachaliah (2002):** Panel (PM, Home Minister, LS Speaker, CM); fixed 5-yr term; **6-month Governor / 3-month President Bill caps**.
  - **Punchhi (2010):** **Delete pleasure (`Art. 156`)**, fixed 5-yr term, **State Legislature impeachment**, collegium (+ VP), 2-yr cooling-off, **6-month Bill cap**, **end University Chancellor role**.
  - **Kurian Joseph Panel (TN, Feb 2026):** Assembly endorses 3 outsiders (President picks 1); single 5-yr term; Assembly removal resolution binds in 14 days; 5-yr cooling-off; 13th Schedule Instrument of Instructions.

## 4. The Chief Minister and State Council of Ministers

- **Appointment & Membership (`Art. 164`):** Governor appoints Assembly majority leader (`Art. 164(1)`). *(UT Trap — 2013 PYQ: CMs of **Delhi [`Art. 239AA(5)`]** & **Puducherry** appointed by **President**, NOT LG!)* CM may belong to **Assembly OR Council**, or be a **non-legislator max 6 months (`Art. 164(4)`)** — curbed by ***S.R. Chaudhuri* (2001)** (no repeat stints in same Assembly) and ***B.R. Kapur* (2001, 5-Judge — Jayalalithaa)** (person **disqualified under `Art. 191` / RPA `Sec. 8(3)`** CANNOT be appointed CM!).
- **Oath, Term, Salary & Duties:** Governor gives **3rd Schedule** Oaths of Office & Secrecy; Assembly confidence; **salary fixed by State Legislature (`Art. 164(5)`)**. CM death/resignation **dissolves entire CoM**. **`Art. 167`** mirrors `Art. 78`. **Federal roles:** **State Planning Board Chair**, rotating **Zonal Council Vice-Chair (1 yr)**, **ISC (`Art. 263`)** & **NITI Governing Council** member.
- **130th Amendment Bill, 2025:** Introduced **20 Aug 2025** (`Arts. 75, 164, 239AA`) for automatic removal after **30 consecutive days' detention** on 5+ yr offences; in **July 2026**, JPC (Aparajita Sarangi) deferred draft report (floating *"suspension"* + acquittal reversal) to Winter Session 2026.
- **CoM Size & Defection (`91st Amend, 2003`):** **`Art. 164(1A)`**: **max 15%** of Assembly (incl. CM) **AND min 12 ministers** (Union `Art. 75(1A)` has **NO minimum**!). **Delhi (`Art. 239AA(4)`)**: **10% = max 7 ministers**. **`Art. 164(1B)`**: Tenth Schedule defectors barred. **Parliamentary Secretaries** struck down in ***Bimolangshu Roy* (2017)**.
- **Responsibility, `Art. 177` & Cabinet:** **Collective responsibility (`Art. 164(2)`) to Legislative Assembly ONLY** (never Council!); **Individual responsibility** to Governor on CM's advice; **No legal responsibility** (`Art. 163(3)` non-inquirable; caretaker CoM post-dissolution). **`Art. 177`** (mirrors `Art. 88`): Ministers & **Advocate General** speak in either House without non-member vote. Three tiers (Cabinet, MoS, Deputy); **Deputy CM** extra-constitutional; word **"Cabinet"** absent from Part VI (only `Art. 352(3)` for Union!).
