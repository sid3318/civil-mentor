## 1. Doctrine of separation of powers and checks and balances

Every constitutional democracy faces a core dilemma: how to empower the State to govern while preventing concentrated authority from extinguishing liberty. The **doctrine of separation of powers** answers this by dividing authority into three functions—**legislative** (lawmaking), **executive** (implementation), and **judicial** (interpretation and adjudication)—assigned to separate organs.

Its lineage runs from **Aristotle's *Politics*** and **John Locke's *Two Treatises of Government* (1689)** to **Baron de Montesquieu**, whose ***The Spirit of the Laws* (1748)** supplied the classic warning:

> "When the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person, or in the same body of Magistrates, there can be no liberty; because apprehensions may arise, lest the same monarch or senate should enact tyrannical laws, to execute them in a tyrannical manner. Again, there is no liberty, if the judicial power be not separated from the legislative and executive." — Montesquieu, *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748), Book XI, Chapter VI

Watertight separation—zero overlap in personnel, functions, or control—is unworkable in modern welfare states where complex regulation requires delegated rules and legislative–executive gridlock paralyses governance. Democracies therefore pair **functional differentiation** with **checks and balances** so each branch restrains excesses by the others.

| Dimension | United States | United Kingdom | India |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Model** | Rigid separation + strong mutual checks | Fusion of legislature and executive | **Functional differentiation** + overlaps & checks |
| **Executive–Legislature** | Separate presidential election; Cabinet outside Congress | Parliamentary fusion; ministers sit in Parliament | Parliamentary fusion; Cabinet responsible to Lok Sabha (**Art. 75(3)**) |
| **Ultimate supremacy** | Written Constitution + strong **judicial supremacy** | **Parliamentary sovereignty** (Dicey) | **Constitutional supremacy** (amendment power + judicial review) |
| **Judicial separation** | Independent Art. III courts; Senate confirms judges | **Constitutional Reform Act, 2005** created **UK Supreme Court** (2009), ending Lord Chancellor overlap | Independent integrated judiciary; **Art. 50** separation; collegium appointments |
| **Key checks** | Veto, Senate approvals, impeachment, judicial review | Ministerial accountability; HRA 1998 incompatibility declarations | Judicial review, Basic Structure, judge removal, oversight, ordinance approval |

> **Prelims pointer:** Britain's **Constitutional Reform Act, 2005** separated the apex judiciary from the House of Lords by establishing the **UK Supreme Court** (2009) and stripping the Lord Chancellor's judicial headship, without ending Diceyan parliamentary sovereignty.

## 2. Separation of powers under the Indian Constitution

India's Constitution has **no express clause** mandating rigid separation. In the Constituent Assembly, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** opposed **K.T. Shah's** separation amendment (draft Article 40-A) because a parliamentary executive drawn from the legislature cannot sit in airtight compartments. Instead, Parts V and VI vest executive (**Arts. 53(1), 154(1)**), legislative (**Arts. 245–246**), and judicial (**Arts. 124–147, 214–237**) authority distinctly, fortified by four specific buffers:

- **Article 50 (DPSP):** Directs separating the judiciary from the executive in public services—operationalised via the **CrPC, 1973** (carried forward in the **BNSS, 2023**, effective 1 July 2024), dividing **Judicial Magistrates** (under High Courts) from **Executive Magistrates** (DM/SDMs).
- **Articles 121 and 211:** Bar Parliament and State Legislatures from discussing Supreme Court or High Court judges' conduct, **except** on a removal motion.
- **Articles 122 and 212:** Bar courts from questioning legislative proceedings on **procedural irregularity**—though substantive illegality or constitutional violation remains reviewable (***Raja Ram Pal*, 2007**).
- **Article 361:** Grants the President and Governors personal immunity from court answerability for official acts and criminal arrest in office, without shielding unconstitutional State action.

### Deliberate functional overlaps

To keep parliamentary governance agile, the Constitution builds functional intersections across all three organs:
- **Executive performing legislative/quasi-judicial roles:** Ordinances (**Arts. 123, 213**—re-promulgation void as a "fraud on the Constitution", ***D.C. Wadhwa*, 1987**; ***Krishna Kumar Singh*, 2017**); delegated legislation; President/Governor as integral parts of legislatures (**Arts. 79, 168**); pardons (**Arts. 72, 161**—narrowly reviewable, ***Epuru Sudhakar*, 2006**); MP/MLA disqualifications on binding ECI advice (**Arts. 103, 192**); and statutory tribunals.
- **Legislature performing judicial/executive-linked roles:** Presidential impeachment (**Art. 61**); judge removal (**Arts. 124(4), 217**); parliamentary privileges (**Arts. 105, 194**); Tenth Schedule defection adjudication by presiding officers (reviewable as tribunals, ***Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992**); and Cabinet membership inside the House (**Art. 75(3)**).
- **Judiciary performing rule-making/administrative roles:** Procedural rule-making (**Art. 145**); control over court staff and subordinate courts (**Arts. 146, 229, 235**); binding precedent (**Art. 141**); and **Article 142** decrees for "complete justice".

### Landmark jurisprudence

Indian courts enforce one clear test: **incidental functional overlap is valid, but no organ may assume an essential function of another or erode checks and balances.**

In ***Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab* (1955)**, Chief Justice B.K. Mukherjea gave the doctrine its classic Indian formulation:

> "The Indian Constitution has not indeed recognized the doctrine of separation of powers in its absolute rigidity but the functions of the different parts or branches of the Government have been sufficiently differentiated and consequently it can very well be said that our Constitution does not contemplate assumption, by one organ or part of the State, of functions that essentially belong to another." — Chief Justice B.K. Mukherjea, *Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab* (1955)

Earlier, ***In Re Delhi Laws Act* (1951)** allowed ancillary delegated legislation while barring Parliament from abdicating its **"essential legislative function"** (policy determination). After ***Golaknath* (1967)** stressed demarcated spheres, ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)** and ***Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975)** elevated separation of powers to the **Basic Structure**—*Raj Narain* voiding the 39th Amendment's Article 329A(4) because Parliament usurped **judicial power** to validate an election directly without applying law. Conversely, ***Bhim Singh v. Union of India* (2010, five judges)** upheld **MPLADS** since MPs merely *recommend* works while district authorities execute them, keeping checks intact; whereas ***NJAC* (2015, 4:1)** voided the 99th Amendment to preserve judicial primacy and separation of powers.

| Landmark Case | Provision / Issue | Core Separation-of-Powers Ruling |
|---|---|---|
| ***In Re Delhi Laws Act* (1951)** | Delegated legislation | Ancillary delegation allowed; **essential legislative function** non-delegable |
| ***Ram Jawaya Kapur* (1955)** | Executive trade without Act | **Sufficient differentiation**, not rigid separation; no usurpation of core roles |
| ***Raj Narain* (1975)** | 39th Amdt (Art. 329A(4)) | **Separation of powers = Basic Structure**; legislature cannot adjudicate elections |
| ***Bhim Singh* (2010, 5 JJ)** | MPLADS scheme | Functional overlap permissible while **checks and balances** survive |
| ***NJAC* (2015, 4:1)** | 99th Amdt & NJAC Act | Protected **judicial primacy** in appointments as part of separation & independence |
| ***Madras Bar Assn* (19 Nov 2025)** | Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 | Voided re-enacted tenure/age/panel clauses; barred **legislative override** |
| ***Special Reference No. 1 of 2025* (20 Nov 2025, 5 JJ)** | Bill-assent timelines & "deemed assent" (Arts. 200–201) | Fixed judicial timelines & Art. 142 **"deemed assent"** breach separation; only **limited mandamus** for indefinite inaction |

> **In the news:** Back-to-back verdicts on **19–20 November 2025** policed *both* sides of separation of powers. On **19 November 2025** (***Madras Bar Association***), CJI B.R. Gavai and Justice K. Vinod Chandran checked the **legislature/executive**, striking down re-enacted provisions of the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 as an unconstitutional "legislative override" (leading to the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026** and National Tribunals Commission). Next day (**Special Reference No. 1 of 2025**, answering President Murmu's 14 Article 143 questions of 13 May 2025), a five-judge bench under CJI Gavai checked the **judiciary**, reversing the 8 April 2025 holding (*State of Tamil Nadu*) that imposed 1–3 month timelines under Articles 200–201 and **"deemed assent"** via Article 142—leaving only a **limited mandamus** for "prolonged, unexplained, and indefinite" inaction.

## 3. Parliamentary sovereignty vs judicial supremacy: India's constitutional synthesis

Constitutional systems traditionally lean toward two poles. Under **British parliamentary sovereignty** (A.V. Dicey), Parliament can make or unmake any law and no court can strike down an Act (even under the Human Rights Act, 1998, courts issue only non-binding declarations of incompatibility). Under **American judicial supremacy**, a brief written Constitution and broad **"due process of law"** clauses (Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments) empowered courts to strike down even socio-economic legislation deemed substantively unreasonable.

India's framers rejected both extremes, building a synthesis of **constitutional supremacy**: neither Parliament nor the Supreme Court is sovereign; the Constitution governs both.

Parliament enjoys wide lawmaking power, amends the Constitution under **Article 368**, and may enact retrospective validating statutes that genuinely remove the statutory defect identified by a court—the valid **doctrine of legislative abrogation** affirmed in ***NHPC Ltd. v. State of Himachal Pradesh* (6 September 2023)**. Yet Parliament **cannot directly reverse a judgment by bare declaration** or re-enact struck-down provisions without curing their constitutional flaw (***Madras Bar Association*, 2021 & 19 November 2025**), nor alter the **Basic Structure** (detailed in the basic structure note). In tandem, courts enforce Fundamental Rights (**Arts. 13, 32, 226**), federal boundaries (**Art. 246**), and constitutional identity.

### From "procedure established by law" to "due process of law"

Advised by US Justice **Felix Frankfurter** that American "due process" had blocked welfare laws, **B.N. Rau** and the Constituent Assembly adopted Japan's narrower **"procedure established by law"** in **Article 21**. In ***A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* (1950)**, the Court read this literally: any validly enacted statutory procedure sufficed. After the Emergency, a seven-judge bench in ***Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978)** linked the **Golden Triangle (Articles 14, 19, 21)**: procedure depriving life or liberty must be **"fair, just and reasonable"**, not arbitrary or oppressive. *Maneka Gandhi*—refined through ***K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017)**—effectively embedded **substantive due process** in India without reviving *Lochner*-era economic vetoes.

> **Prelims pointer:** **Article 21** textually reads **"procedure established by law"** (borrowed from **Japan**), NOT "due process of law". Substantive **due process** entered Indian law judicially via ***Maneka Gandhi* (1978)**.

## 4. Judicial review: constitutional foundation, scope and limits

**Judicial review** empowers courts to test Union and State statutes, constitutional amendments, and executive acts against the Constitution and void them to the extent of inconsistency. Conceptually inspired by the US (***Marbury v. Madison*, 1803**, Chief Justice John Marshall), it stands on explicit textual foundations in India.

> **Prelims pointer:** The exact phrase **"judicial review" does NOT appear anywhere in the Constitution**. It flows from **Articles 13, 32, 131–136, 143, 226, 227, 245–246, 251, 254, and 372**, and is **Basic Structure** (***Minerva Mills*, 1980**; ***L. Chandra Kumar*, 1997**).

### Three domains and evolving tests

| Domain | Constitutional Anchor | Governing Judicial Tests | Landmark Cases |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Constitutional Amendments** | Art. 368 + implied limits | **Basic Structure**; **width** & **identity tests** (*M. Nagaraj*) | *Kesavananda* (1973), *Minerva Mills* (1980), *M. Nagaraj* (2006), *NJAC* (2015) |
| **Union & State Statutes** | Arts. 13, 245–246, 251, 254 | **Legislative competence**, **Part III / express text**, **manifest arbitrariness** (Art. 14). *Not* abstract basic structure alone (*Anjum Kadari*, Nov 2024) | *Kuldip Nayar* (2006), *Shayara Bano* (2017), *Electoral Bonds* (Feb 2024), *Anjum Kadari* (Nov 2024) |
| **Executive Action** | Arts. 14, 32, 226, 227 | **Illegality, *Wednesbury* irrationality, procedural impropriety** + **4-step proportionality** | *Tata Cellular* (1994), *Modern Dental College* (2016), *Puttaswamy* (2017), *Anuradha Bhasin* (2020) |

Two modern doctrines sharpen scrutiny: **manifest arbitrariness** (***Shayara Bano*, 2017**; ***Navtej Singh Johar*, 2018**; ***Electoral Bonds*, Feb 2024**), invalidating capricious or unprincipled statutes under Article 14; and **four-step proportionality** (***Modern Dental College*, 2016**; ***Puttaswamy*, 2017**; ***Anuradha Bhasin*, 2020**; ***Madhyamam Broadcasting*, 2023**), requiring rights-restricting action to show **(i) legitimate goal**, **(ii) rational nexus**, **(iii) least restrictive necessity**, and **(iv) fair balancing**.

### Ninth Schedule and *I.R. Coelho* (2007)

Inserted by the **1st Amendment (1951)** via **Article 31B** to protect 13 agrarian-reform laws, the **Ninth Schedule** grew to **284 statutes**, sweeping in non-agrarian laws like Tamil Nadu's 69 per cent quota. Building on ***Waman Rao* (1981)**, a unanimous **nine-judge bench** in ***I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu* (2007)** ended blanket immunity: **all laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 remain open to judicial review** under the **rights test** and **essence of rights test** anchored in the **Golden Triangle (Articles 14, 19, 21)**, Article 15, and Basic Structure.

**Limits:** Courts presume statutes constitutional, avoid judging economic policy wisdom absent manifest arbitrariness, respect legislative procedural immunity (**Arts. 122, 212**), and—per **Special Reference No. 1 of 2025**—decline merits review of Articles 200–201 Bill decisions or pre-enacted Bills (save under Article 143).

## 5. Judicial activism, Public Interest Litigation (PIL) and judicial overreach

**Judicial activism** is a proactive interpretive philosophy expanding constitutional rights and addressing legislative or executive defaults. Surging after the Emergency (*ADM Jabalpur*, 1976), Indian activism transformed **Article 21** into a charter of dignified life encompassing livelihood (*Olga Tellis*, 1985), clean environment (*Subhash Kumar*, 1991), education (*Unni Krishnan*, 1993), legal aid, health, and privacy (*Puttaswamy*, 2017).

### Public Interest Litigation (PIL)

Pioneered in the late 1970s–early 1980s by **Justices V.R. Krishna Iyer** and **P.N. Bhagwati** (termed **Social Action Litigation** by Upendra Baxi), PIL democratised access via four tools: relaxed ***locus standi*** permitting representative standing for vulnerable groups (***S.P. Gupta*, 1981**); **epistolary jurisdiction** (treating letters/postcards as writs); court-appointed **fact-finding commissions**; and **continuing mandamus** monitoring compliance.

Key milestones include ***Mumbai Kamgar Sabha* (1976)** (procedural seed); ***Hussainara Khatoon* (1979)** (advocate **Kapila Hingorani**, "Mother of PIL", freeing Bihar undertrials and declaring **right to speedy trial**); ***Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (1984)** (bonded labour, epistolary relief); ***M.C. Mehta* environmental series** (1986 onwards—**absolute liability**, Taj Mahal, Ganga clean-up, Delhi CNG); ***Vishaka* (1997)** (binding workplace sexual-harassment guidelines under Arts. 141–142 until the **POSH Act, 2013**); ***D.K. Basu* (1997)** (11 arrest safeguards); and ***Prakash Singh* (2006)** (seven **police-reform** directives).

> **Example:** To curb frivolous "publicity, private, political, or paisa interest litigation", the Supreme Court issued **PIL Guidelines (1988, updated 2003)** and in ***State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal* (2010)** mandated verifying petitioner **credentials**, testing *prima facie* merit and substantial public interest, rejecting oblique motives, and levying **exemplary costs** on abuse.

### Activism vs overreach vs restraint

When rights enforcement turns into routine policy-making or administrative micro-management without democratic accountability or technical capacity, **activism becomes judicial overreach**.

| Dimension | Judicial Activism | Judicial Overreach | Judicial Restraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nature** | Purposive rights enforcement & filling genuine voids | Intrusion into core policy, budgets, or administration | Policy deference; intervention only for clear constitutional breach |
| **Legitimacy** | Grounded in Arts. 13, 21, 32, 141–142 | Strains **separation of powers** & democratic accountability | Preserves institutional comity & elected mandate |
| **Examples** | *Hussainara Khatoon* (1979), *Vishaka* (1997), *D.K. Basu* (1997) | 2016 highway liquor ban; 2016 cinema Anthem order; April 2025 deemed assent | *Tata Cellular* (1994), *Aravali Golf Club* (2007), Nov 2025 Reference |

In ***Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass* (6 December 2007)**, setting aside orders directing post creation, Justice Markandey Katju issued the classic admonition:

> "Judges must know their limits and must not try to run the Government. They must have modesty and humility, and not behave like Emperors. There is broad separation of powers under the Constitution and each organ of the State—the legislature, the executive and the judiciary—must have respect for the others and must not encroach into each other's domains." — Justice Markandey Katju, *Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass* (2007)

**Classic Mains examples of overreach:** (1) the **December 2016 highway liquor-vend ban** (500-metre ban under Art. 142 disrupting state excise revenues and prompting municipal road denotifications before dilution); (2) the **November 2016 cinema National Anthem mandate** (*Shyam Narayan Chouksey*, made optional in January 2018); (3) administrative micro-management (BCCI–Lodha reforms, granular urban/air-quality orders); and (4) the **April 2025 "deemed assent" timelines** reversed in November 2025.

**The opposite danger—judicial abdication:** Restraint must never become **excessive executive deference** ("executive courts") on liberty or delayed constitutional hearings. In ***Madhyamam Broadcasting Ltd. v. Union of India* (5 April 2023)** (MediaOne case), CJI D.Y. Chandrachud and Justice Hima Kohli curtailed **"sealed cover jurisprudence"** as violating natural and open justice, prescribing a less restrictive **public interest immunity (PII)** redaction procedure instead.

> **Remember:** Warn against *both* extremes in Mains: **judicial overreach** usurps elected governance, while **judicial abdication** (uncritical sealed-cover deference) leaves citizens defenceless against executive excess.

## 6. Maintaining constitutional equilibrium: way forward

India's constitutional equilibrium rests on **institutional comity** and reciprocal restraint:

- **Proactive governance:** Timely legislative and executive action removes the vacuums that invite judicial gap-filling.
- **Honouring the *Lakshman Rekha*:** Parliament must avoid **legislative overrides** (*Madras Bar Association*, Nov 2025), while courts must refrain from rewriting constitutional silences via **Article 142** (*Special Reference No. 1 of 2025*, Nov 2025).
- **Disciplined doctrinal standards:** Consistent use of **four-step proportionality**, **manifest arbitrariness**, and textual discipline (*Anjum Kadari*, Nov 2024) curbs subjectivity.
- **PIL gatekeeping & open justice:** Enforcing ***Balwant Singh Chaufal* (2010)** credential checks and exemplary costs protects genuine PILs, while replacing sealed covers with **PII** scrutiny (*Madhyamam Broadcasting*, 2023) and ensuring timely Constitution Bench hearings sustain judicial credibility.

> **Mains angle:** Structure GS-2 answers on four axes: (1) **Functional differentiation + checks** (*Ram Jawaya Kapur*, *Raj Narain*) versus US rigidity and UK parliamentary sovereignty; (2) **Doctrinal arc** (*Gopalan* → *Maneka Gandhi*, *I.R. Coelho*, proportionality); (3) **Balanced critique** contrasting rights-advancing activism (*Hussainara Khatoon*, *Vishaka*) with both overreach (*Aravali Golf Club*, highway liquor ban) and abdication (sealed covers, *Madhyamam Broadcasting*); and (4) **Twin November 2025 verdicts** proving constitutional restraint binds all three branches.
