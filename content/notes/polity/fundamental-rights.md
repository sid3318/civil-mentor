## 1. Why Fundamental Rights

Part III of the Constitution, Articles 12 to 35, sets out the Fundamental Rights. They are often called the **Magna Carta of India**. They are "fundamental" in two senses: they are essential to the all-round development of the individual, and they are protected by the Constitution itself, which no ordinary law can override. Their purpose is to establish a government of laws rather than of men, and to protect the individual, and minorities, against the tyranny of the majority. They promote what the framers called political democracy.

The idea grew with the national movement. The **Nehru Report** of 1928 asked for a declaration of rights, and the Congress's **Karachi session** of 1931 adopted a resolution on Fundamental Rights and Economic Policy. In the Constituent Assembly, a sub-committee on Fundamental Rights chaired by J.B. Kripalani drafted them. The framers drew inspiration from the American Bill of Rights, but wrote far more detailed rights, with their limits stated alongside.

Originally the Constitution guaranteed seven groups of rights. The **right to property** (Article 31) was removed from Part III by the **44th Amendment Act, 1978**. It is now a legal right under Article 300A, protected against executive action but not against laws made by the legislature. Six groups remain:

| Right | Articles |
|---|---|
| Right to equality | 14–18 |
| Right to freedom | 19–22 |
| Right against exploitation | 23–24 |
| Right to freedom of religion | 25–28 |
| Cultural and educational rights | 29–30 |
| Right to constitutional remedies | 32 |

This note covers the general features, Articles 12 and 13, and the right to equality. The other groups have notes of their own.

## 2. What sets these rights apart

The Fundamental Rights share a set of features, and Prelims questions often turn on them.

- **Some are for citizens only, others for every person.** Articles 15, 16, 19, 29 and 30 are available only to citizens. The rest, including Articles 14, 20, 21 and 22, protect citizens and foreigners alike, and some, such as Article 14, protect legal persons like companies too.
- **They are not absolute.** Each can be restricted by the State on stated grounds, and the courts decide whether a restriction is reasonable.
- **Most protect against the State; a few, against private persons too.** Most limit what the State may do. Articles 15(2), 17, 23 and 24 also bind private individuals.
- **Some are negative, some positive.** Most restrain the State (the State "shall not" deny equality). Others require it to act, such as providing free education under Article 21A.
- **They are justiciable.** A person whose right is violated can go straight to the Supreme Court under Article 32, and to a High Court under Article 226.
- **Parliament can amend them**, but not so as to destroy the basic structure.
- **They can be suspended during a national emergency**, except the rights under **Articles 20 and 21**. The 44th Amendment added this exception. Article 19 is suspended automatically only when an emergency is declared on the ground of war or external aggression, not armed rebellion (Article 358).
- **They can be restricted** for the armed forces (Article 33) and in areas under martial law (Article 34), and they are qualified by the savings in Articles 31A, 31B and 31C.
- **Most are self-executing.** A few need a law to give them effect, such as the law punishing untouchability.

> **Prelims pointer:** Rights available only to citizens: Articles 15, 16, 19, 29 and 30. Rights that cannot be suspended even in an emergency: Articles 20 and 21. Rights that apply against private individuals as well as the State: Articles 15(2), 17, 23 and 24.

## 3. Article 12: who is "the State"

Since most Fundamental Rights are guarantees against the State, the definition of "the State" decides who can be held to them. Article 12 defines it to include:

1. the Government and Parliament of India;
2. the government and legislature of each state;
3. all local authorities, such as municipalities, panchayats, district boards and improvement trusts;
4. all other authorities, statutory or non-statutory, such as the LIC, ONGC or SAIL.

The phrase "other authorities" has been widened by the Supreme Court over time. In ***R.D. Shetty* (1979)** and ***Ajay Hasia* (1981)** the Court held that a private body or corporation acting as an **instrumentality or agency of the State** falls within Article 12. Its tests include whether the government holds the share capital, funds it, exercises deep and pervasive control over it, or has given it functions of a public nature. Applying them, the Court held in ***Zee Telefilms* (2005)** that the **BCCI is not "the State"** under Article 12, although its public functions make it answerable to the High Courts under Article 226.

## 4. Article 13: laws that violate rights are void

Article 13 gives teeth to the Fundamental Rights. It declares that all laws inconsistent with them, or in derogation of them, are **void** to the extent of the inconsistency. It applies to laws made before the Constitution ("pre-constitutional laws") and to laws made after it. Article 13 thus expressly provides for **judicial review**, which the Supreme Court exercises under Article 32 and the High Courts under Article 226.

Article 13 defines "law" broadly. It includes:

- permanent laws enacted by Parliament or the state legislatures;
- temporary laws such as ordinances;
- statutory instruments such as orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations and notifications;
- non-legislative sources of law, such as custom or usage having the force of law.

The courts have built several doctrines around Article 13:

- **Severability.** Only the offending part of a law is struck down, if it can be separated from the rest.
- **Eclipse.** A pre-constitutional law that violates a Fundamental Right is not dead, only overshadowed. It revives if the right is later amended away (*Bhikaji Narain Dhakras*, 1955).
- **No waiver.** A citizen cannot waive a Fundamental Right, even voluntarily, because the rights serve public policy as well as the individual (*Basheshar Nath*, 1959).

> **Remember:** The **24th Amendment (1971)** declared that a constitutional amendment is not a "law" within the meaning of Article 13. But in ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)** the Supreme Court held that an amendment can still be struck down if it violates the **basic structure** of the Constitution, which includes core Fundamental Rights.

## 5. Article 14: equality before law and equal protection of laws

Article 14 says that the State shall not deny to any person **equality before the law** or the **equal protection of the laws** within the territory of India. It applies to all persons, citizens or not, natural or legal.

The two phrases come from different traditions and mean different things.

| Equality before the law | Equal protection of the laws |
|---|---|
| A British concept | An American concept |
| A negative idea: no special privileges in favour of any person | A positive idea: equal treatment in equal circumstances |
| Everyone, high or low, is subject to the ordinary law | The State must treat alike those who are alike, and may treat differently those who are different |

Equality before the law is part of A.V. Dicey's **rule of law**, which has three elements: the absence of arbitrary power, equality before the law, and the primacy of individual rights (the constitution is the result of rights, not their source). The Supreme Court has held that the rule of law, in the first two senses, is a basic feature of the Indian Constitution.

### Reasonable classification

Equal protection does not mean that every law must apply to everyone. The State may classify people for legitimate purposes, but the classification must be **reasonable**. In *State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar* (1952) and later cases, the Supreme Court laid down a two-part test:

```text
 Does the law treat a group differently?
                 │
                 ▼
 1. Intelligible differentia: is the group
    clearly marked off from those left out?
                 │ yes
                 ▼
 2. Rational nexus: is the difference linked
    to the law's purpose?
                 │ yes
                 ▼
 Reasonable classification → valid
```

### Arbitrariness

From the 1970s the Court added a second test. In ***E.P. Royappa* (1974)** it held that **equality is antithetic to arbitrariness**: an arbitrary act is unequal by its nature. ***Maneka Gandhi* (1978)** carried this further, holding that Article 14 strikes at arbitrariness in State action because a fair and reasonable procedure is part of equality. In ***Shayara Bano* (2017)** the Court used the doctrine of **manifest arbitrariness** to strike down instant triple talaq. In ***Navtej Singh Johar* (2018)** it read down Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code to decriminalise consensual same-sex relations between adults, and in ***Joseph Shine* (2018)** it struck down the offence of adultery, both partly on Article 14 grounds.

### Exceptions to Article 14

Equality before the law is not absolute. The Constitution and international law recognise exceptions:

- **The President and the Governors** are not answerable to any court for the exercise of their powers, and no criminal proceedings can be instituted against them during their term of office (Article 361).
- **No one can be sued for publishing** a substantially true report of proceedings in Parliament or a state legislature (Article 361A).
- **Members of Parliament and the state legislatures** cannot be proceeded against for anything said or any vote given in the House (Articles 105 and 194).
- **Laws giving effect to Article 39(b) and (c)** cannot be challenged under Article 14 (Article 31C).
- **Foreign sovereigns, ambassadors and diplomats** enjoy immunity under international law, as do the UN and its agencies.

## 6. Article 15: no discrimination

Article 15 forbids the State to discriminate against any citizen on grounds **only** of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. The word "only" matters: discrimination on other grounds, such as residence, is not forbidden by Article 15.

Clause (2) goes further. It forbids anyone, the State or private persons, to restrict citizens' access to shops, public restaurants, hotels and places of public entertainment, or their use of wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and places of public resort maintained by the State or dedicated to public use, on these grounds.

The exceptions are where Article 15 does its most important work:

| Clause | Allows special provisions for | Added by |
|---|---|---|
| 15(3) | Women and children | Original |
| 15(4) | Socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes | 1st Amendment (1951) |
| 15(5) | Admission of these groups to educational institutions, including private ones (aided or unaided), except minority institutions | 93rd Amendment (2005) |
| 15(6) | Economically weaker sections, up to 10% of seats in educational institutions | 103rd Amendment (2019) |

Clause (4) was added after the Supreme Court, in *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* (1951), struck down a communal government order allotting medical college seats by caste and religion. The 93rd Amendment's clause (5) was upheld in *Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust* (2014). The EWS quota of clause (6) was upheld by a 3–2 majority in ***Janhit Abhiyan* (November 2022)**, which held that reservation based on economic criteria alone does not violate the basic structure.

## 7. Article 16: equality of opportunity in public employment

Article 16 guarantees every citizen equality of opportunity in matters of employment or appointment to any office under the State. No citizen may be discriminated against, or held ineligible, on grounds only of **religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth or residence**. Note that "descent" and "residence" appear here but not in Article 15.

The exceptions:

| Clause | Allows | Added by |
|---|---|---|
| 16(3) | Parliament to prescribe residence as a condition for certain jobs in a state or Union territory | Original |
| 16(4) | Reservation of posts for backward classes not adequately represented in State services | Original |
| 16(4A) | Reservation in promotion for SCs and STs, with consequential seniority | 77th (1995) and 85th (2001) Amendments |
| 16(4B) | Carrying forward unfilled reserved vacancies, outside the 50% ceiling for that year | 81st Amendment (2000) |
| 16(5) | Offices of religious institutions to be held by members of that religion | Original |
| 16(6) | Reservation for economically weaker sections, up to 10% | 103rd Amendment (2019) |

The leading case is ***Indra Sawhney* (1992)**, the Mandal case. It upheld 27% reservation for Other Backward Classes in central government jobs. It excluded the **creamy layer**, the advanced among the backward. It held that reservations should not normally exceed **50%**, except in extraordinary situations, and it barred reservation in promotions, a bar later removed by the 77th Amendment. In ***M. Nagaraj* (2006)** the Court upheld reservation in promotions, but required the State to show backwardness, inadequate representation and administrative efficiency. ***Jarnail Singh* (2018)** dropped the need for quantifiable data on backwardness for SCs and STs. The note on reservation covers this case law in full.

> **In the news:** On 1 August 2024, in ***State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh***, a seven-judge bench held by 6 to 1 that states may **sub-classify Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes** to give the more backward among them a larger share of the reservation. This overruled *E.V. Chinnaiah* (2004), which had treated the SCs as one homogeneous group. Several judges also suggested applying a creamy layer test to SCs and STs.

## 8. Article 17: untouchability abolished

Article 17 abolishes untouchability and forbids its practice in any form. Enforcing any disability arising out of untouchability is an offence punishable by law.

The Constitution does not define "untouchability", and neither does the law. The courts have held that it refers not to untouchability in its literal sense but to the social practice that excluded certain castes because of their birth. The **Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955**, was amended in 1976 and renamed the **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955**. It was followed by the **Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989**. The right in Article 17 is available against private individuals as well as the State, and the State has a constitutional duty to see that it is respected.

## 9. Article 18: titles abolished

Article 18 abolishes titles and makes four provisions:

1. The State shall not confer any title, except a military or academic distinction, on anyone, citizen or foreigner.
2. A citizen of India shall not accept any title from a foreign state.
3. A foreigner holding an office of profit or trust under the State shall not accept a title from a foreign state without the President's consent.
4. No citizen or foreigner holding an office of profit or trust under the State shall accept any present, emolument or office from a foreign state without the President's consent.

The aim was to end the hereditary titles of the colonial era, such as *Rai Bahadur* and *Khan Bahadur*, which created artificial distinctions among citizens. In ***Balaji Raghavan* (1996)** the Supreme Court upheld the national awards, the Bharat Ratna and the Padma awards, holding that they are not "titles" within the meaning of Article 18. It added that they should not be used as prefixes or suffixes to the recipients' names.

> **Mains angle:** The right to equality is best discussed as moving from **formal** to **substantive** equality. Formal equality treats everyone alike. Substantive equality recognises that treating unequals alike perpetuates inequality, and so permits protective discrimination: reservations, special provisions for women and children, and the abolition of untouchability. A good answer can trace this through the case law: the classification test, the doctrine against arbitrariness (*Royappa*, *Maneka Gandhi*, *Shayara Bano*), and the reservation cases (*Indra Sawhney*, *Janhit Abhiyan*, *Davinder Singh*). It should also discuss the tensions: merit and representation, the 50% ceiling, and benefits captured by the better-off within groups. Conclude that the Constitution sees equality as something the State must actively build, not merely declare.

- **Part III:** Articles 12–35, the "Magna Carta of India". Roots in the Nehru Report (1928) and the Karachi resolution (1931). Drafted by the sub-committee chaired by J.B. Kripalani. Inspired by the US Bill of Rights.
- **Seven groups became six:** property was removed by the 44th Amendment (1978) and is now Article 300A, a legal right.
- **Citizens only:** Articles 15, 16, 19, 29, 30. **Never suspended:** 20 and 21. **Against private persons too:** 15(2), 17, 23, 24. **Article 19** is suspended automatically only in a war or external-aggression emergency (Article 358).
- **Article 12, "the State":** Union and state governments and legislatures, local authorities, other authorities. Instrumentality test: *R.D. Shetty* (1979), *Ajay Hasia* (1981). The BCCI is not the State (*Zee Telefilms*, 2005).
- **Article 13:** inconsistent laws are void, which is the basis of judicial review. "Law" includes ordinances, rules, notifications, and custom with the force of law. Doctrines of severability, eclipse (*Bhikaji*, 1955) and no waiver (*Basheshar Nath*, 1959). The 24th Amendment (1971) says an amendment is not "law"; *Kesavananda* (1973) adds the basic structure test.
- **Article 14:** equality before the law (British, negative) and equal protection (American, positive). Dicey's rule of law. Reasonable classification needs intelligible differentia and a rational nexus. Arbitrariness: *Royappa* (1974), *Maneka Gandhi* (1978), *Shayara Bano* (2017, triple talaq), *Navtej Johar* (2018), *Joseph Shine* (2018).
- **Exceptions to Article 14:** Articles 361, 361A, 105, 194 and 31C; foreign diplomats.
- **Article 15:** religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth ("only"). 15(3) women and children; 15(4) backward classes, SCs and STs (1st Amendment, after *Champakam Dorairajan*, 1951); 15(5) admissions including private institutions, except minority ones (93rd, 2005); 15(6) EWS 10% (103rd, 2019; upheld 3–2 in *Janhit Abhiyan*, November 2022).
- **Article 16:** adds descent and residence. 16(4) reservation; 16(4A) promotion (77th, 1995; seniority by the 85th, 2001); 16(4B) carry-forward (81st, 2000); 16(6) EWS.
- **Reservation cases:** *Indra Sawhney* (1992): 27% for OBCs, creamy layer, 50% ceiling. *M. Nagaraj* (2006). *Jarnail Singh* (2018). *Davinder Singh* (1 August 2024): sub-classification of SCs and STs allowed, overruling *E.V. Chinnaiah* (2004).
- **Article 17:** untouchability abolished but not defined. Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 (renamed 1976); SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989.
- **Article 18:** no titles except military and academic. Foreign titles need the President's consent for foreigners in office. National awards are not titles (*Balaji Raghavan*, 1996).
