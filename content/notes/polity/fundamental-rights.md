## 1. Nature, Origins & Salient Features of Fundamental Rights

Part III of the Constitution of India (`Articles 12` to `35`) enshrines the **Fundamental Rights**, justly celebrated as the **Magna Carta of India**. Just as the original Magna Carta of 1215 compelled King John of England to accept legal limits on royal authority, Part III places binding constitutional fetters on executive discretion and legislative majorities alike. Nowhere else in the world does a written constitution contain a more elaborate, carefully calibrated, and judicially enforceable charter of individual and group liberties.

These rights are designated "fundamental" in two distinct yet reinforcing senses. First, they are indispensable to the full intellectual, moral, spiritual, and material development of every human personality; without them, citizenship shrinks into mere subjecthood. Second, they are guaranteed and protected by the fundamental law of the land—the Constitution itself—so that no ordinary statute, executive notification, or transient parliamentary majority can abridge or extinguish them.

### Concept, Constitutional Philosophy & Historical Origins

The overarching purpose of Fundamental Rights is to establish "a government of laws and not of men" and to realise **political democracy** across the Indian republic. They operate primarily as shields against arbitrary State power, guarding both the lone dissenting individual and vulnerable religious, linguistic, or social minorities against the potential tyranny of an electoral majority. While Part IV (Directive Principles of State Policy) charts the roadmap toward social and economic democracy, Part III anchors the civil and political liberties without which democratic accountability cannot function.

#### Roots in the Indian Freedom Struggle

India's demand for a binding charter of rights did not arise overnight in the Constituent Assembly; it matured organically across five decades of anti-colonial struggle against arbitrary British rule, press censorship, preventive detention, and racial discrimination:

- **Constitution of India Bill (1895):** Often attributed to the circle of Bal Gangadhar Tilak, this early swaraj document envisioned free speech, inviolability of the home, right to property, and equality before law.
- **Commonwealth of India Bill (1925):** Drafted under Annie Besant's leadership, it proposed seven fundamental rights including liberty of person, freedom of conscience, free expression, free assembly, and non-discrimination on grounds of sex.
- **Nehru Report (1928):** Prepared by an all-parties committee chaired by Motilal Nehru, this landmark constitutional draft demanded nineteen specific Fundamental Rights—including universal adult suffrage, equal rights for women, freedom of conscience, protection of minority languages and cultures, and free elementary education—most of which found direct echoes in the 1950 Constitution.
- **Karachi Session of the Indian National Congress (1931):** Presided over by Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, the Congress adopted the historic **Resolution on Fundamental Rights and Economic Policy** (drafted largely by Jawaharlal Nehru and Mahatma Gandhi). It seamlessly fused civil liberties—free speech, free association, religious neutrality of the State, abolition of untouchability—with socio-economic commitments such as living wages and workers' protection.
- **Government of India Act, 1935 & Sapru Committee (1945):** The Simon Commission (1930) and the Joint Parliamentary Committee rejected demands for a bill of rights, resulting in the conspicuous omission of Fundamental Rights from the 1935 Act (save narrow provisions on non-discrimination in public employment under Section 298 and property compensation under Section 299). In 1945, the non-partisan **Sapru Committee** made a crucial conceptual breakthrough by dividing rights into two categories: **justiciable rights** (which became Part III) and **non-justiciable rights** (which became Part IV).

#### Drafting in the Constituent Assembly & Comparative Influences

When the Constituent Assembly convened, it established the **Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal and Excluded Areas** chaired by **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel**. Under it operated the **Sub-Committee on Fundamental Rights**, chaired by **Acharya J.B. Kripalani** (with members including B.R. Ambedkar, K.M. Munshi, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, Minoo Masani, Rajkumari Amrit Kaur, and Hansa Mehta). Drawing structural inspiration primarily from the **Bill of Rights in the United States Constitution** (the first ten amendments of 1791), the framers nonetheless departed sharply from the American model in one critical respect.

The US Bill of Rights is framed in brief, seemingly absolute language (for example, the First Amendment declares that Congress "shall make no law" abridging free speech), leaving the US Supreme Court to carve out exceptions through judicial doctrines like "police power" and "clear and present danger." India's framers—steered by Ambedkar and Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar amid the trauma of Partition, princely integration, and deep social inequality—chose instead to codify **detailed qualifications and reasonable restrictions** directly alongside each right in the text itself. The goal was to strike a transparent constitutional balance between individual liberty and collective social control rather than leave the boundaries entirely to unpredictable judicial discretion.

```keyfacts
# Part III Architecture at a Glance
Articles 12–35 | Magna Carta of India | Originally 7 groups; 6 remain after the 44th Amendment (1978) shifted Right to Property to Art. 300A
Articles 15, 16, 19, 29, 30 | Citizens-Only Rights | Exclusive to Indian citizens; foreigners and alien entities cannot claim them
Articles 20 & 21 | Non-Suspendable Rights | Protected even during a National Emergency under Article 359 (added by 44th Amendment, 1978)
Articles 15(2), 17, 23, 24 | Direct Horizontal Application | Enforceable against private individuals as well as the State (expanded to Arts. 19/21 in Kaushal Kishor, 2023)
```

### Classification of Fundamental Rights & Removal of Right to Property

Originally, the Constitution of 1950 classified Fundamental Rights into **seven distinct groups**, including the **Right to Property** guaranteed under `Article 19(1)(f)` (freedom to acquire, hold, and dispose of property) and `Article 31` (protection against compulsory deprivation of property without authority of law and compensation). Over the first three decades after Independence, the Right to Property proved to be the single most litigated provision in the entire Constitution, sparking repeated confrontations between Parliament's zamindari abolition and agrarian reform programmes and the Supreme Court's insistence on market-value compensation (*Bella Banerjee*, 1954; *R.C. Cooper*, 1970).

To end this constitutional friction once and for all, the Janata government enacted the **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`** (effective 20 June 1979). It deleted both `Article 19(1)(f)` and `Article 31` from Part III and simultaneously inserted a new `Article 300A` in Part XII under the heading "Right to Property," declaring simply that *"No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law."* This surgical shift transformed property from a Fundamental Right into a **constitutional (and legal) right** with three immediate consequences: first, a citizen can no longer move the Supreme Court directly under `Article 32` for property deprivation (remedies lie before a High Court under `Article 226` or a civil court); second, the right protects private property against **arbitrary executive action** but NOT against valid legislation enacted by Parliament or a State Legislature; third, there is no general constitutional guarantee of full compensation except in two narrow retained clauses—`Article 30(1A)` (acquisition of property of a minority educational institution) and the second proviso to `Article 31A(1)` (acquisition of land under personal cultivation within the statutory ceiling limit).

With the deletion of property from Part III, **six groups of Fundamental Rights** remain in force today:

| Group | Fundamental Right | Articles | Core Constitutional Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Right to Equality** | `14–18` | Rule of law, non-discrimination, substantive affirmative action, abolition of untouchability & titles |
| **2** | **Right to Freedom** | `19–22` | Six democratic freedoms, criminal procedural safeguards, life & dignity, elementary education, arrest/detention limits |
| **3** | **Right against Exploitation** | `23–24` | Abolition of human trafficking, *begar*, forced labour, and hazardous child labour |
| **4** | **Right to Freedom of Religion** | `25–28` | Individual conscience, collective denominational autonomy, secular taxation, neutral State education |
| **5** | **Cultural and Educational Rights** | `29–30` | Conservation of distinct language, script, and culture; minority autonomy in educational institutions |
| **6** | **Right to Constitutional Remedies** | `32` | Direct Supreme Court enforcement via five prerogative writs — "heart and soul" of the Constitution |

*(Note: `Article 12` defines "the State", `Article 13` establishes judicial review over inconsistent laws, `Articles 31A`, `31B`, and `31C` save specified socio-economic laws from Part III challenge, and `Articles 33`, `34`, and `35` govern armed forces, martial law, and exclusive Parliamentary legislation.)*

> [!remember]
> **Right to Property — Constitutional Right, NOT Mere Ordinary Statutory Right:** Although textbooks frequently summarise `Article 300A` as a "legal right," the Supreme Court (*Vidya Devi v. State of Himachal Pradesh*, 2020; *Kolkata Municipal Corporation v. Bimal Kumar Shah*, May 2024) has emphasised that `Article 300A` is a **constitutional and human right**. The State cannot forcibly dispossess a citizen without following fair statutory procedure, notice, hearing, and reasonable compensation—even though `Article 32` writ jurisdiction is unavailable.

### Salient Features & Complete Feature Matrix

Understanding the structural features of Part III is vital because UPSC Prelims routinely tests subtle boundaries between citizenship, horizontal enforceability, emergency suspension, and legislative competence. Eleven defining characteristics distinguish India's Fundamental Rights:

1. **Differential Entitlement — Citizens Only vs All Persons:** Five Articles (`15`, `16`, `19`, `29`, and `30`) are reserved exclusively for **Indian citizens** (and `Article 19` is further restricted to natural citizens, excluding juristic bodies like companies). All other Fundamental Rights (`14`, `20`, `21`, `21A`, `22`, `23`, `24`, `25`, `26`, `27`, and `28`) extend to **all persons**—citizens, friendly foreigners, and, where the nature of the right permits (such as `Article 14` and `Article 20`), legal persons like corporations and registered societies. Enemy aliens, however, lose the ordinary arrest safeguards of `Article 22(1)–(2)`.
2. **Qualified, Not Absolute:** Unlike absolute natural-law rhetoric, every Indian Fundamental Right (with narrow exceptions such as `Article 17` and `Article 24` in their core prohibitions) is subject to **reasonable restrictions** imposed by the State on constitutionally enumerated grounds—sovereignty and integrity, security of the State, public order, decency, morality, or general public interest. Crucially, the final arbiter of whether a restriction is "reasonable" is the **judiciary**, not the executive or legislature.
3. **Primarily Vertical, Selectively Horizontal:** Most Fundamental Rights protect individuals vertically against the **arbitrary action of the State** (and its instrumentalities under `Article 12`). However, four textual provisions—`Article 15(2)` (access to public shops, restaurants, wells, roads), `Article 17` (untouchability), `Article 23` (trafficking and forced labour), and `Article 24` (child labour)—operate **horizontally against private individuals** as well. Marking a historic doctrinal shift, a five-judge Constitution Bench in ***Kaushal Kishor v. State of Uttar Pradesh* (January 2023)** held by a 4–1 majority that rights under `Articles 19` and `21` can also be enforced against non-State private actors, imposing an affirmative duty on the State to safeguard liberty against private threats.
4. **Blend of Negative Restraints and Positive Mandates:** Most rights are worded negatively as prohibitions on State power ("The State shall not deny..." in `Article 14`, "No person shall be deprived..." in `Article 21`). Yet several confer **positive privileges or affirmative duties**, such as `Article 19` (positive freedoms), `Article 21A` (State duty to provide free and compulsory elementary education), `Article 25(1)`, `Article 29`, and `Article 30`.
5. **Justiciable and Directly Enforceable:** Fundamental Rights are legally enforceable in courts of law. Under `Article 32`, an aggrieved person can approach the **Supreme Court directly** without exhausting lower appellate tiers, and the right to move the Supreme Court is itself a Fundamental Right. High Courts hold concurrent (and wider) writ jurisdiction under `Article 226`.
6. **Constitutional Guarantee & Basic Structure Shield:** Because Fundamental Rights are guaranteed by the Supreme Court as their "protector and guarantor" (*Romesh Thappar*, 1950), they cannot be altered by ordinary laws. They are **not sacrosanct or permanent**—Parliament can amend them via a Constitutional Amendment Act under `Article 368`—but only so long as the amendment does not abrogate or damage the **Basic Structure** of the Constitution (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973).
7. **Suspension During National Emergency (Articles 358 & 359):** Fundamental Rights can be suspended during a Proclamation of National Emergency (`Article 352`), subject to two vital post-`44th Amendment (1978)` safeguards:
   - **`Article 358`:** The six freedoms under `Article 19` are **automatically suspended** only when an Emergency is proclaimed on grounds of **war or external aggression**—never on the ground of **armed rebellion**.
   - **`Article 359`:** For other rights, the President may by order suspend the right to move any court for their enforcement, **except `Articles 20` and `21`**, which can NEVER be suspended under any circumstances.
8. **Scope Curtailed by Saving Clauses (Articles 31A, 31B & 31C):** The operation of `Articles 14` and `19` (and broader Part III challenges) is qualified by the constitutional safe harbours in `Article 31A` (agrarian and estate acquisition laws), `Article 31B` (Ninth Schedule statutes, subject to Basic Structure review post-24 April 1973), and `Article 31C` (laws implementing Directive Principles in `Articles 39(b)` and `39(c)`).
9. **Armed Forces & Intelligence Modification (Article 33):** Parliament alone may restrict or abrogate the application of Fundamental Rights to members of the armed forces, paramilitary forces, police, intelligence agencies, and related communications staff to ensure discipline and duty performance.
10. **Martial Law Restriction (Article 34):** Fundamental Rights can be restricted in any area where **martial law** (military rule) is in force, and Parliament may pass an Act of Indemnity validating official acts done to restore order.
11. **Mostly Self-Executing, Selectively Requiring Parliamentary Statutes:** The vast majority of Fundamental Rights take effect directly without enabling legislation (`Articles 14`, `19`, `21`, `25`). However, a few provisions—especially those declaring punishable offences such as untouchability (`Article 17`) and human trafficking or forced labour (`Article 23`)—require enabling criminal statutes, which **`Article 35`** reserves exclusively to **Parliament** (not State Legislatures) to ensure uniform national standards.

| Entitlement Category | Applicable Fundamental Rights | Key Constitutional & Legal Nuances |
|---|---|---|
| **Citizens Only** | `Arts. 15, 16, 19, 29, 30` | Foreigners excluded. For `Art. 19`, legal persons (companies/corporations) are also excluded, though Indian citizen shareholders may sue if their own rights are hit (*Bennett Coleman*, 1972). |
| **All Persons (Citizens + Foreigners)** | `Arts. 14, 20, 21, 21A, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28` | Applies to citizens, friendly aliens, and (for `Arts. 14, 20`) legal persons. **Exception:** Enemy aliens are denied ordinary arrest protections under `Art. 22(1)–(2)`. |
| **Directly Binding on Private Individuals (Horizontal)** | `Arts. 15(2), 17, 23, 24` *(+ `Arts. 19, 21` post-Kaushal Kishor, 2023)* | Enforced both vertically against State authorities and horizontally against private citizens, landlords, employers, and non-State entities. |
| **Non-Suspendable in National Emergency** | `Arts. 20, 21` | Guaranteed permanently by the `44th Amendment (1978)` amending `Art. 359(1)`. Even during external war, the State cannot suspend life, liberty, or fair conviction safeguards. |

> [!prelims]
> **High-Yield Prelims Traps on Part III Features:**
> 1. **Mnemonic for Citizens-Only Articles:** **15, 16, 19, 29, 30** — remember **"15–16 (Equality of Opportunity/No Discrimination), 19 (Six Freedoms), 29–30 (Minority Culture & Education)"**. Everything else in `14–28` covers foreigners too!
> 2. **Companies & Article 19:** A company incorporated in India is a "legal person" (protected by `Art. 14`), **NOT a "citizen"** under the Citizenship Act, 1955—so a company itself cannot invoke `Article 19` (*State Trading Corporation v. CTO*, 1963).
> 3. **Armed Rebellion & Article 19:** Under `Article 358` (post-44th Amendment), a National Emergency declared on the ground of **armed rebellion** does NOT automatically suspend `Article 19`; only war or external aggression triggers automatic suspension.

## 2. Definition of 'State' (Article 12) & Judicial Review (Article 13)

Before Part III enumerates a single substantive liberty, `Articles 12` and `13` build its institutional and legal scaffolding. `Article 12` answers the foundational threshold question—*against whom* are Fundamental Rights enforceable? `Article 13` answers the enforcement question—*what happens* when a law, old or new, collides with a Fundamental Right? Together, these two Articles convert Part III from a pious manifesto into a living instrument of constitutional supremacy.

### Article 12: Meaning & Four Limbs of 'the State'

Because Fundamental Rights were conceived chiefly as restraints upon public authority, the reach of Part III expands or contracts directly with the breadth of the word **"State"**. `Article 12` supplies an inclusive (not exhaustive) definition expressly applicable to Part III (and extended by `Article 36` to Part IV, Directive Principles):

> Unless the context otherwise requires, "the State" includes:
> 1. **The Government and Parliament of India** — i.e., the Union Executive (President, Council of Ministers, central ministries, bureaucratic departments) and the Union Legislature (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha).
> 2. **The Government and the Legislature of each of the States** — i.e., the State Executives (Governor, State Cabinets, administrative agencies) and State Legislatures (Vidhan Sabha and Vidhan Parishad).
> 3. **All local authorities** within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India — municipalities, municipal corporations, panchayats, district boards, improvement trusts, mining settlement boards, and port trusts. Under Section 3(31) of the General Clauses Act, 1897, and *Union of India v. R.C. Jain* (1981), a "local authority" must have separate legal existence, function in a defined territorial area, enjoy substantial autonomy, be entrusted with governmental/municipal functions, and possess power to raise funds through local taxation or fees.
> 4. **All other authorities** within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India — covering statutory corporations (such as LIC, ONGC, SAIL, Airport Authority of India, SEBI, universities) as well as non-statutory bodies, government companies, and registered societies acting as agencies or instrumentalities of the government.

Notice the deliberate territorial phrase *"within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India."* Even an Indian authority or agency operating outside India's geographical borders—such as an Indian embassy or an overseas public-sector branch under Union control—remains bound by Fundamental Rights when dealing with citizens or persons subject to its acts.

### Judicial Expansion of 'Other Authorities' & the Judiciary Debate

The first three limbs of `Article 12` are straightforward; virtually all constitutional litigation has centred on the fourth limb—**"other authorities."** In the modern welfare and regulatory state, governments increasingly act through public-sector corporations, government-owned companies under the Companies Act, registered societies, autonomous boards, and public-private partnerships. If the State could escape Part III simply by incorporating a company or registering a society, Fundamental Rights would be hollowed out.

#### From *Ejusdem Generis* to the Instrumentality Test

The Supreme Court's journey across five decades reveals a steady expansion from formalistic restraint to substantive functional scrutiny:

- ***University of Madras v. Shanta Bai* (1954, Madras HC):** Early decisions applied the narrow statutory rule of *ejusdem generis* (of the same kind), holding that "other authorities" meant only bodies exercising strictly sovereign or governmental functions—thereby excluding state-aided universities.
- ***Rajasthan State Electricity Board v. Mohan Lal* (1967, Constitution Bench):** The Supreme Court emphatically rejected *ejusdem generis*. It held that "other authorities" includes **all authorities created by the Constitution or a statute** on whom powers are conferred by law—even if they perform commercial or non-sovereign activities (such as supplying electricity).
- ***Sukhdev Singh v. Bhagatram* (1975, Constitution Bench):** Applying this principle, the Court held that statutory corporations—the Oil and Natural Gas Commission (ONGC), Life Insurance Corporation (LIC), and Industrial Finance Corporation (IFC)—are "State" under `Article 12`. Crucially, Justice K.K. Mathew's concurring opinion planted the seed of the **instrumentality or agency test** for non-statutory bodies.
- ***Ramana Dayaram Shetty v. International Airport Authority of India* (1979):** Justice P.N. Bhagwati elevated the instrumentality test into binding doctrine, holding that even a body not created by statute is "State" if it functions as an **agency or instrumentality of the government**.
- ***Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib Sehravardi* (1981, Constitution Bench):** Dealing with the Regional Engineering College, Srinagar—a non-statutory **registered society**—the Court held that the *form* of incorporation (statute, company, or society) is immaterial; what matters is the *substance* of State control. It codified the famous **six-factor test** for identifying a State instrumentality:
  1. Entire or major share capital is held by the government.
  2. Financial assistance from the State is so extensive as to meet almost the entire expenditure of the body.
  3. The body enjoys a State-conferred or State-protected monopoly status.
  4. Existence of **deep and pervasive State control** over administration and policy.
  5. Functions of the body are of public importance and closely related to governmental functions.
  6. A pre-existing department of the government has been transferred to the corporation or society.
- ***Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology* (2002, 7-Judge Bench, 5–2):** Overruling *Sabhajit Tewary* (1975), a seven-judge bench held that the **Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR)** is "State" under `Article 12`. Clarifying *Ajay Hasia*, the Court emphasised that the six factors are not a rigid checklist; the cumulative core test is whether the body is **"financially, functionally and administratively dominated by or under the control of the Government,"** and whether that control is particular to the body and **pervasive**—not merely regulatory control applicable to every private entity under general statutes.

```flow
# Testing Whether a Body Is 'State' Under Article 12
Is the body Union/State Govt, Legislature, or Local Authority? | Directly covered under Limbs 1–3 of Article 12
Created by Constitution or Statute with legal powers? | Yes → 'Other Authority' (Rajasthan Electricity Board, 1967) || Non-Statutory (Company / Society / Trust)? | Apply cumulative Pradeep Kumar Biswas (2002) & Ajay Hasia (1981) test
Financially, functionally & administratively under deep, pervasive State control? | Yes → 'State' under Art. 12 (CSIR, LIC, SAIL, ONGC) || No pervasive State control, but performs public duties? | Not 'State' under Art. 12, yet amenable to High Court writ under Art. 226 (BCCI — Zee Telefilms, 2005)
```

#### Private Bodies & the BCCI Ruling (*Zee Telefilms*, 2005)

Applying the tightened *Pradeep Kumar Biswas* threshold, a five-judge Constitution Bench in ***Zee Telefilms Ltd. v. Union of India* (2005, 3–2 majority)** ruled that the **Board of Control for Cricket in India (BCCI)** is **NOT "State" under `Article 12`**. The BCCI is an autonomous society registered under the Tamil Nadu Societies Registration Act; it receives no financial assistance from the government, its share capital is not State-held, its monopoly over cricket is *de facto* rather than State-conferred by statute, and the Union Government exercises no deep or pervasive administrative control over its selections or office-bearers. However, the majority simultaneously held that because the BCCI discharges vital **public functions** (selecting the national Indian team and regulating cricket nationwide), an aggrieved party can seek writ relief against it before a **High Court under `Article 226`**—though not directly before the Supreme Court under `Article 32`. Similarly, NCERT (*Chander Mohan Khanna*, 1991) and private unaided schools or cooperative societies without deep State domination fall outside `Article 12`.

#### Is the Judiciary 'State' Under Article 12?

A classic UPSC Prelims and Mains subtlety concerns whether courts themselves are included within `Article 12`. Unlike the Union and State Executives and Legislatures, `Article 12` deliberately omits the judiciary from its text. Constitutional jurisprudence draws a sharp functional boundary:

- **Administrative & Rule-Making Capacity — YES, 'State':** When the Supreme Court or a High Court performs non-judicial administrative functions—such as recruiting court staff, making service rules (`Article 146` or `Article 229`), framing court procedures (`Article 145`), or conducting disciplinary proceedings—it acts as **"State"** under `Article 12`, and its actions can be challenged under `Articles 14`, `16`, or `32` (*Prem Chand Garg v. Excise Commissioner*, 1963).
- **Judicial Capacity — NO, Not 'State':** In ***Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar v. State of Maharashtra* (1966, 9-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court held that a judicial decision or order passed by a court of competent jurisdiction in adjudication between parties cannot be said to violate Fundamental Rights so as to be challenged by a writ petition under `Article 32`. An erroneous judicial order is corrected via **appeal, revision, review, or curative petition** (*Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra*, 2002), not by treating the deciding judge as "the State." In ***A.R. Antulay v. R.S. Nayak* (1988, 7-Judge Bench)**, the Court nonetheless clarified that even the Supreme Court cannot pass a judicial direction contrary to statutory mandates or Fundamental Rights—and if it inadvertently does so, it has an inherent duty to recall the order *ex debito justitiae* (as a matter of justice).

> [!prelims]
> **Article 12 High-Yield Distinctions:**
> - **Included as 'State':** LIC, ONGC, SAIL, FCI, CSIR (*Pradeep Kumar Biswas*, 2002), Airport Authority of India, Indian Statistical Institute, Nationalised Banks, Bharat Petroleum, State Universities, Gram Panchayats, Municipalities.
> - **NOT 'State' under Art. 12:** BCCI (*Zee Telefilms*, 2005 — but amenable to High Court writs under `Art. 226`), NCERT (*Chander Mohan Khanna*, 1991), Judiciary in its *judicial* adjudicatory capacity (*Mirajkar*, 1966), purely private companies receiving no pervasive State control.

### Article 13: Laws Inconsistent with Fundamental Rights & Judicial Review

If `Article 12` defines the target, **`Article 13`** supplies the constitutional sword. It explicitly anchors the doctrine of **Judicial Review** in the Indian Constitution—even though the exact phrase "judicial review" does not appear anywhere in the constitutional text—by empowering the Supreme Court (`Article 32`) and High Courts (`Article 226`) to declare any law void to the extent it contravenes Part III.

`Article 13` comprises four carefully structured clauses:

- **`Article 13(1)` — Pre-Constitutional Laws:** All "laws in force" in the territory of India immediately before the commencement of the Constitution (26 January 1950)—such as colonial-era Acts like the Indian Penal Code, 1860, or the Press (Emergency Powers) Act, 1931—are declared **void to the extent of their inconsistency** with Part III. Crucially, `Article 13(1)` is **prospective, not retrospective** (*Keshavan Madhava Menon v. State of Bombay*, 1951): actions completed or prosecutions lawfully initiated for acts committed *before* 26 January 1950 are not wiped out.
- **`Article 13(2)` — Post-Constitutional Laws:** Imposes an express constitutional injunction upon the State: *"The State shall not make any law which takes away or abridges the rights conferred by this Part and any law made in contravention of this clause shall, to the extent of the contravention, be void."* Unlike pre-constitutional statutes, a post-constitutional law violating Fundamental Rights is **void *ab initio*** (stillborn from inception) in respect of persons entitled to that right.
- **`Article 13(3)` — Expansive Definition of 'Law' and 'Laws in Force':** To prevent the executive from bypassing Part III through non-statutory instruments, `Article 13(3)(a)` defines "law" broadly to include:
  1. **Permanent legislation** enacted by Parliament or State Legislatures.
  2. **Temporary laws** such as Ordinances promulgated by the President (`Article 123`) or State Governors (`Article 213`).
  3. **Delegated / subordinate legislation** in the nature of statutory orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, and notifications issued by the executive or local authorities.
  4. **Non-legislative sources of law**, specifically any **custom or usage** having in the territory of India the force of law.
  *(Purely administrative directions or internal executive instructions lacking statutory force are normally outside `Article 13(3)(a)`, though arbitrary executive action remains directly challengeable under `Article 14`.)*
- **`Article 13(4)` — Constitutional Amendments Exempted:** Inserted by the **`24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971`**, declaring that nothing in `Article 13` shall apply to any amendment of the Constitution made under `Article 368`.

#### The Personal Law Debate: *Narasu Appa Mali* vs *Shayara Bano*

One of the fiercest unresolved debates under `Article 13(3)` asks whether **uncodified religious personal laws** (governing marriage, divorce, inheritance, maintenance, and guardianship) constitute "laws in force" testable against Fundamental Rights. In ***State of Bombay v. Narasu Appa Mali* (1952, Bombay HC)**, Chief Justice M.C. Chagla and Justice P.B. Gajendragadkar held that **personal laws are NOT "laws in force"** under `Article 13(1)`—reasoning that `Article 13(3)(a)` mentions "custom or usage" but omits personal law, and that `Article 44` (Uniform Civil Code) and `Entry 5, Concurrent List` show the framers intended legislatures, not courts, to reform personal laws.

Over seven decades later, *Narasu Appa Mali* has come under heavy constitutional fire. In ***Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017)**, the majority avoided directly overruling *Narasu* by holding that instant triple talaq (*talaq-e-biddat*) was statutorily recognised under Section 2 of the **Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937**, making it a pre-constitutional *statute* testable under `Article 13(1)` and void for manifest arbitrariness. Going further in the ***Sabarimala* judgment (*Indian Young Lawyers Association*, 2018)**, Justice D.Y. Chandrachud explicitly criticised *Narasu Appa Mali* as flawed, arguing that immunising customs and personal laws from Part III creates unconstitutional "islands of exclusion" contrary to transformative constitutionalism. The question remains formally pending before the **nine-judge Constitution Bench** constituted in *Kantaru Rajeevaru* (2019).

> [!debate]
> **Should Uncodified Personal Laws Be Tested Under Article 13?**
> - **Case for Judicial Review (Transformative Constitutionalism):** If mere "custom or usage" is explicitly subject to `Article 13(3)`, exempting entrenched patriarchal personal laws perpetuates gender inequality (`Arts. 14–15`) and treats women as second-class citizens in domestic matters.
> - **Case for Legislative Reform (Pluralism & Judicial Restraint):** Personal laws are interwoven with community identity and `Article 25` religious freedom; piecemeal judicial invalidation without comprehensive statutory replacement creates legal vacuums, making parliamentary reform under `Article 44` and consultative codification democratically preferable.

### Constitutional Doctrines Under Article 13

To apply `Article 13` without needlessly destabilising the entire legal system, the Supreme Court has crafted four celebrated constitutional doctrines—each a favourite in both Prelims MCQs and Mains case-law citations:

#### 1. Doctrine of Severability (Separability)

The words **"to the extent of the inconsistency/contravention"** in `Articles 13(1)` and `13(2)` mean that a statute does not automatically perish in its entirety merely because one clause violates a Fundamental Right. If the invalid portion can be cleanly severed without altering the core legislative scheme, courts strike down *only* the offending part and preserve the valid remainder (*State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara*, 1951—eight sections of the Bombay Prohibition Act severed; *Shreya Singhal*, 2015—only Section 66A of the IT Act, 2000 struck down, leaving the rest intact). In ***R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla v. Union of India* (1957)**, the Court laid down comprehensive guidelines: if valid and invalid provisions are so inextricably interwoven that the legislature would never have enacted the valid part alone, the entire Act fails (as happened with the NJAC Act in 2015).

#### 2. Doctrine of Eclipse

An inconsistent **pre-constitutional law** under `Article 13(1)` is not dead or erased from the statute book; it is merely **overshadowed (eclipsed)** by the Fundamental Right and remains dormant and unenforceable. If a subsequent constitutional amendment removes the conflict, the shadow lifts and the original statute automatically revives without requiring fresh legislative re-enactment! In ***Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh* (1955)**, a 1947 provincial motor-transport monopoly law clashed with `Article 19(1)(g)` in 1950, but automatically revived once the **1st Amendment (1951)** amended `Article 19(6)` to permit State trade monopolies.

What about **post-constitutional laws** under `Article 13(2)`? In ***Deep Chand v. State of Uttar Pradesh* (1959)** and ***Mahendra Lal Jaini* (1963)**, the Court held that a post-constitutional law contravening Part III is **stillborn (void *ab initio*)**, so the Doctrine of Eclipse cannot revive it later. However, in ***State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills* (1974)**, the Supreme Court introduced a vital nuance: if a post-constitutional law violates a citizens-only right (like `Article 19`), it is void *only* qua citizens, remaining valid and enforceable against **non-citizens and companies**—meaning it is not completely erased from the statute book and can be eclipsed rather than wholly stillborn!

#### 3. Doctrine of No Waiver

Can an individual voluntarily give up or waive their Fundamental Right—for instance, by signing a settlement accepting an unconstitutional tax? Unlike the United States, where citizens may generally waive constitutional rights, the Supreme Court in ***Basheshar Nath v. Commissioner of Income Tax* (1959)** held that **a citizen CANNOT waive a Fundamental Right** (especially `Article 14`, and by extension Part III generally). Fundamental Rights are not mere private privileges enacted for individual benefit; they embody high **public policy** and constitutional morality designed to restrain State power in a young republic where vast numbers of citizens are economically vulnerable and easily coerced into surrendering rights. The Court reaffirmed this principle in ***Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985)**, holding that pavement dwellers' earlier undertaking not to obstruct eviction could not estop them from asserting their `Article 21` right to livelihood.

#### 4. Doctrine of Prospective Overruling

Imported from American jurisprudence by Chief Justice K. Subba Rao in ***I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab* (1967)**, the **Doctrine of Prospective Overruling** allows a constitutional court to declare a law or precedent unconstitutional *only for the future*, leaving past transactions, land distributions, and settled rights executed under the earlier ruling undisturbed. In *Golaknath*, the Court restricted prospective overruling to: (a) constitutional matters only, (b) exercise by the **Supreme Court alone** (not High Courts), and (c) situations where retroactive invalidation would trigger grave administrative or socio-economic chaos.

### Article 13(4): Constitutional Amendments & Basic Structure

No provision in Part III ignited a greater institutional battle than the question: *Is a Constitutional Amendment Act passed under `Article 368` a "law" within the meaning of `Article 13(2)`?* If yes, Parliament could never amend any Fundamental Right; if no, Parliament's amending power would be unlimited.

The constitutional pendulum swung dramatically across four landmark milestones:

1. ***Shankari Prasad v. Union of India* (1951)** and ***Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan* (1965):** The Supreme Court upheld the 1st and 17th Amendments, holding that "law" in `Article 13(2)` covers only **ordinary legislative law**, not constituent power exercised under `Article 368`. Thus, Parliament could freely amend Fundamental Rights.
2. ***I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab* (1967, 11-Judge Bench, 6–5):** Reversing *Shankari Prasad* and *Sajjan Singh*, the majority held that Fundamental Rights occupy a "transcendental and immutable" position, a constitutional amendment *is* "law" under `Article 13(2)`, and Parliament therefore **cannot abridge or take away** any Fundamental Right.
3. **`24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971`:** Parliament responded swiftly by inserting **`Article 13(4)`** and **`Article 368(3)`**, declaring unequivocally that nothing in `Article 13` applies to any constitutional amendment made under `Article 368`, and making Presidential assent to amendment bills mandatory.
4. ***Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (24 April 1973, 13-Judge Bench, 7–6):** Overruling *Golaknath* and upholding the validity of the `24th Amendment` and `Article 13(4)`, the largest bench in Indian judicial history forged a brilliant synthesis. Parliament *can* amend any provision of the Constitution—including Fundamental Rights—because a constitutional amendment is not ordinary "law" under `Article 13`. Yet the power to "amend" does not include the power to destroy: no amendment may alter or damage the **Basic Structure** of the Constitution, which encompasses the core identity of equality, liberty, secularism, and judicial review.

## 3. Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)

The Right to Equality (`Articles 14` to `18`) forms the foundational pillar of India's transformative constitutionalism. Inheriting a society fractured by millennia of caste hierarchy, untouchability, gender subordination, feudal titles, and colonial racial privilege, the framers refused to settle for hollow legal neutrality. `Article 14` states the broad general principle of equality and rule of law; `Articles 15` and `16` translate it into specific bans on discrimination paired with positive affirmative action for historically disadvantaged groups; `Article 17` strikes directly at India's deepest social evil, untouchability; and `Article 18` dismantles hereditary and colonial titles to foster civic egalitarianism.

### Article 14: Equality Before Law & Equal Protection of Laws

`Article 14` declares with austere majesty: *"The State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India."* Notice three textual anchors immediately: the protection belongs to **"any person"** (citizens, foreigners, corporations, registered societies, and statutory bodies alike); it operates against **"the State"** (`Article 12`); and it pairs two distinct legal concepts drawn from two great constitutional traditions.

#### Equality Before Law (Dicey's Rule of Law) vs Equal Protection of Laws

```compare
# Equality Before Law vs Equal Protection of Laws (Article 14)
Equality Before Law | Equal Protection of Laws
Constitutional Origin :: British Common Law tradition (A.V. Dicey's Rule of Law) | American Constitution (14th Amendment, 1868)
Conceptual Nature :: Negative concept — absence of any special privileges in favour of any person | Positive concept — affirmative guarantee of equal treatment under equal circumstances
Core Operational Rule :: Every individual—rich or poor, Prime Minister or constable—is subject to the ordinary law administered by ordinary courts | "Like should be treated alike, and unlike must not be treated alike"; permits rational differentiation
Equality Paradigm :: Formal / procedural equality | Substantive / distributive equality (underpins progressive taxation & reservations)
```

**A.V. Dicey's Rule of Law:** British jurist A.V. Dicey articulated three classic pillars of the Rule of Law: (1) **Absence of arbitrary power** (supremacy of regular law—no one can be punished except for a distinct breach of law established in ordinary courts); (2) **Equality before the law** (equal subjection of all classes to ordinary law without separate administrative courts like France's *droit administratif*); and (3) **Primacy of individual rights** (in Britain's unwritten system, the constitution is the *result* of judge-declared individual rights, not their source). **Prelims Trap:** Only the **first and second** elements apply to India! In India, the third element does NOT apply because the **written Constitution is the source of individual rights**, not their consequence. The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed (*Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain*, 1975; *Minerva Mills*, 1980) that the Rule of Law embodied in `Article 14` is an indestructible part of the **Basic Structure**.

#### The Old Doctrine: Reasonable Classification (*Anwar Ali Sarkar*, 1952)

Human beings are not identical in wealth, capability, age, geography, or historical disadvantage; applying a uniform rule mechanically to unequal persons would itself generate grave injustice (for example, taxing a daily-wage labourer and a billionaire at the same flat rupee sum). Consequently, `Article 14` **forbids class legislation** (singling out a group arbitrarily for favour or burden), **but permits reasonable classification** for legitimate legislative purposes.

```flow
# The Two-Pronged Reasonable Classification Test (Article 14)
State law or policy differentiates between groups | Challenged under Equal Protection of Laws (Art. 14)
Prong 1: Intelligible Differentia | Clear, real, and rational basis distinguishing persons/things grouped together from those left out || Prong 2: Rational Nexus | Direct logical connection between the differentia and the legitimate statutory objective sought
Both Prongs Satisfied → Valid Classification | Upheld under Art. 14 (Anwar Ali Sarkar, 1952; R.K. Dalmia, 1958) || Either Prong Fails → Class Legislation | Struck down as unconstitutional under Art. 14
```

In ***State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar* (1952)** and ***Ram Krishna Dalmia v. Justice S.R. Tendolkar* (1958)**, the Supreme Court consolidated the classic parameters of reasonable classification:
- Classification must be founded on an **intelligible differentia** and bear a **rational nexus** to the object of the Act.
- Even a **single individual or corporation** may constitute a valid "class by itself" if unique circumstances justify special treatment (*Chiranjit Lal Chowdhuri v. Union of India*, 1950—Sholapur Spinning and Weaving Company takeover).
- Classification need not be scientifically perfect or mathematically precise; legislative wisdom enjoys an initial presumption of constitutionality, and the burden of proving hostile discrimination rests on the challenger.
- Classification may be based on geography, time, nature of occupation, or economic capacity—provided it is never arbitrary, artificial, or evasive (*Kathi Raning Rawat*, 1952; *Air India v. Nergesh Meerza*, 1981—striking down air-hostess retirement upon first pregnancy as "most unreasonable and arbitrary").

#### The New Doctrine: Non-Arbitrariness & Manifest Arbitrariness

By the 1970s, jurists recognised a dangerous flaw in relying solely on the reasonable-classification formula: a cruel or irrational law applied uniformly to *everyone* could technically pass the classification test! In a paradigm-shifting leap, Justice P.N. Bhagwati in ***E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1974)** liberated `Article 14` from "cribbed, cabined and confined" boundaries, declaring: *"Equality is a dynamic concept with many aspects and dimensions... From a positivistic point of view, equality is antithetic to arbitrariness. In fact equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies."* Where an executive or State act is arbitrary, it is inherently unequal under `Article 14`.

This new doctrine matured through four milestone rulings:
1. ***Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978)** and ***Ajay Hasia* (1981):** Cemented non-arbitrariness as a freestanding constitutional test—every State action must be fair, just, non-capricious, and reasoned.
2. ***Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017, 5-Judge Bench, 3–2):** Overruling earlier doubts (*McDowell*, 1996) about whether primary legislation could be invalidated merely for arbitrariness, Justices R.F. Nariman and U.U. Lalit authoritatively applied the doctrine of **manifest arbitrariness** to strike down instant triple talaq (*talaq-e-biddat*). A statute is manifestly arbitrary if it is enacted "capriciously, irrationally and/or without adequate determining principle," or is "excessive and disproportionate."
3. ***Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018, 5-Judge Unanimous)** and ***Joseph Shine v. Union of India* (2018, 5-Judge Unanimous):** Invoked manifest arbitrariness under `Article 14` (alongside `Articles 15`, `19`, and `21`) to read down **Section 377 IPC** (decriminalising consensual adult same-sex relations) and to strike down **Section 497 IPC** (the archaic adultery offence treating a wife as her husband's chattel).
4. ***Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR) v. Union of India* (15 February 2024, 5-Judge Unanimous):** Invalidated the Electoral Bonds Scheme and the Finance Act, 2017 amendments removing the cap on corporate political donations (Section 182, Companies Act) as **manifestly arbitrary** under `Article 14` because treating loss-making shell companies and profit-making companies identically facilitated unlimited quid pro quo corporate capture of politics.

#### Six Exceptions to Equality Before Law

Equality before law under `Article 14` is not absolute; the Constitution and customary international law carve out six explicit institutional immunities:

1. **President of India & State Governors (`Article 361`):** Not answerable to any court for the exercise and performance of official powers and duties; no criminal proceedings can be instituted or continued against them during their term; no arrest or imprisonment process can issue against them while in office; civil proceedings regarding personal acts require **two months' prior written notice**. *(Note: Does not bar judicial review of the validity of Presidential/Gubernatorial actions such as `Article 356` proclamations or mercy petitions.)*
2. **True Publication of Legislative Proceedings (`Article 361A`, added by 44th Amendment, 1978):** No person is liable to civil or criminal proceedings in any court for publishing in a newspaper (or broadcast) a substantially true report of proceedings of either House of Parliament or a State Legislature, unless made with malice (secret sittings excepted).
3. **Members of Parliament (`Article 105`) & State Legislators (`Article 194`):** Immune from court proceedings for anything said or any vote given inside Parliament, a State Legislature, or their committees. *(Note: In `Sita Soren v. Union of India`, March 2024, a 7-judge bench unanimously overruled `P.V. Narasimha Rao` [1998], clarifying that parliamentary immunity does NOT protect legislators who accept bribes to vote or speak in the House!)*
4. **Directive Principles Safe Harbour (`Article 31C`):** Laws enacted to give effect to the socialist Directive Principles in `Article 39(b)` (equitable distribution of material resources) and `Article 39(c)` (preventing concentration of wealth) cannot be challenged on the ground of violating `Article 14` (or `Article 19`). As the Supreme Court famously remarked in *Sanjeev Coke* (1982), *"Where Article 31C comes in, Article 14 goes out."*
5. **Foreign Sovereigns, Rulers, Ambassadors & Diplomats:** Enjoy immunity from Indian criminal and civil jurisdiction under international law and the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, 1961 (enacted via the Diplomatic Relations Act, 1972).
6. **United Nations & Its Specialised Agencies:** Enjoy diplomatic privileges and immunities under the UN (Privileges and Immunities) Act, 1947.

### Article 15: Prohibition of Discrimination on Certain Grounds

While `Article 14` protects all persons against general inequality, **`Article 15`** protects **citizens only** against specific forms of historical social discrimination. Its architecture combines two stringent prohibitions—`15(1)` vertical and `15(2)` horizontal—with four affirmative-action enabling clauses—`15(3)` through `15(6)`.

#### Clause 15(1) & the Significance of 'Only'

`Article 15(1)` commands that *"The State shall not discriminate against any citizen on grounds **only** of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth or any of them."* Two words carry immense Prelims weight:
- **"Discriminate"** means making an adverse distinction or unfavourable bias against someone.
- **"Only"** means that discrimination grounded *solely* on one or more of these five identities is unconstitutional; differentiation based on **other rational grounds**—such as **residence** (e.g., state domicile fee concessions in *D.P. Joshi v. State of Madhya Bharat*, 1955), language, academic merit, or physical fitness—is NOT prohibited by `Article 15(1)` (though it must still satisfy `Article 14` reasonableness). Crucially, in *Navtej Singh Johar* (2018), the Court read **"sex"** expansively to encompass **sexual orientation and gender identity**, barring discrimination against LGBTQ+ citizens.

#### Clause 15(2): Horizontal Ban on Civic Segregation

Targeting the everyday caste and communal exclusions deeply embedded in Indian villages and towns, `Article 15(2)` prohibits **both the State and private individuals** from subjecting any citizen, on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth, to any disability, liability, restriction, or condition regarding:
- **(a)** Access to **shops, public restaurants, hotels, and places of public entertainment** (even when wholly privately owned and receiving zero government funding!); or
- **(b)** Use of **wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads, and places of public resort** maintained wholly or partly out of State funds OR dedicated to the use of the general public.

#### Protective Discrimination: Clauses 15(3) to 15(6)

To transform formal equality into substantive justice, `Article 15` contains four vital enabling exceptions:

1. **`Article 15(3)` — Women and Children (Original):** Permits the State to make *any special provision* for women and children—such as reserved seats in local bodies (`Articles 243D/243T`), women-only educational institutions, maternity benefits, free education for children, and protective provisions in bail or workplace safety (e.g., POSH Act, 2013).
2. **`Article 15(4)` — Advancement of SEBCs, SCs & STs (`1st Amendment Act, 1951`):** Added immediately after the Supreme Court in ***State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* (1951)** struck down a Madras Communal Government Order reserving medical and engineering seats by caste and religion on the ground that `Article 15` contained no reservation exception (unlike `Article 16(4)`). Clause `15(4)` empowers the State to make special provisions (seat reservations, fee waivers, scholarships) for the advancement of any **Socially and Educationally Backward Classes (SEBCs)** of citizens, or for the **Scheduled Castes (SCs)** and **Scheduled Tribes (STs)**.
3. **`Article 15(5)` — Admission to Educational Institutions, Including Private Unaided Colleges (`93rd Amendment Act, 2005`):** Enacted to overcome *T.M.A. Pai* (2002) and *P.A. Inamdar* (2005) (which had barred State quotas in unaided private colleges), `Article 15(5)` empowers the State to make special admission provisions by law for SEBCs, SCs, and STs in educational institutions—including **private educational institutions, whether aided or unaided**—while expressly **exempting minority educational institutions** covered under `Article 30(1)`. Parliament operationalised this through the **Central Educational Institutions (Reservation in Admission) Act, 2006** (granting 27% OBC quota in IITs, IIMs, central universities). In ***Ashoka Kumar Thakur v. Union of India* (2008, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court upheld `Article 15(5)` for State-maintained and aided institutions while mandating **creamy layer exclusion** for OBCs; subsequently, in ***Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v. Union of India* (2014, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Court upheld `Article 15(5)` even as applied to **private unaided non-minority institutions** (while confirming the exemption of minority institutions from both `Article 15(5)` and the RTE Act).
4. **`Article 15(6)` — 10% EWS Reservation (`103rd Amendment Act, 2019`):** Empowers the State to make special provisions—including up to **10% reservation** in educational institutions (both public and private, aided or unaided, **except minority institutions**)—for **Economically Weaker Sections (EWS)** of citizens *other than* the classes already covered by `15(4)` and `15(5)` (i.e., unreserved/general category citizens meeting income and asset criteria, initially ₹8 lakh annual family income). In ***Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India* (7 November 2022, 5-Judge Bench, 3–2 majority)**, the Supreme Court upheld the `103rd Amendment`, holding that: (a) affirmative action based solely on **economic criteria** does not violate the Basic Structure; (b) excluding SCs, STs, and OBCs from the EWS quota is valid because they already enjoy separate vertical reservations; and (c) the 50% cap from *Indra Sawhney* is not inflexible and applies strictly to backward-class quotas under `15(4)`/`16(4)`, not to a distinct economic compartment. *(Chief Justice U.U. Lalit and Justice S. Ravindra Bhat dissented, arguing that excluding poor SCs, STs, and OBCs from an economic quota breaches the Equality Code.)*

### Article 16: Equality of Opportunity in Public Employment

`Article 16` operationalises equality specifically within the domain of **public employment or appointment to any office under the State**—again restricted exclusively to **citizens**.

- **`Article 16(1)`:** Guarantees equality of opportunity for all citizens in matters relating to employment or appointment to any office under the State (covering initial recruitment, salary, promotion, seniority, and termination).
- **`Article 16(2)`:** Prohibits ineligibility or discrimination in State employment on grounds **only of seven criteria**: religion, race, caste, sex, **descent**, place of birth, or **residence**. Notice the classic UPSC Prelims contrast: `Article 16(2)` adds **two extra grounds** (*descent* and *residence*) that do NOT appear in `Article 15(1)`.

#### Exceptions Under Clauses 16(3) to 16(6)

1. **`Article 16(3)` — Parliamentary Residence Requirement (Original):** Only **Parliament** (NOT a State Legislature, reinforced by `Article 35`) may prescribe **residence** within a State or Union Territory as a condition for certain public employments in that State/UT. Parliament enacted the *Public Employment (Requirement as to Residence) Act, 1957* for Andhra Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh, Manipur, and Tripura; it expired in 1974. Today, special regional domicile provisions survive only for Andhra Pradesh and Telangana under `Article 371D` (and select other `Article 371` clauses), which is why State-enacted local-jobs quota laws routinely face constitutional invalidation under `Article 16(2)–(3)`.
2. **`Article 16(4)` — Reservation for Backward Classes (Original):** Enables the State to reserve appointments or posts in favour of any **backward class of citizens** which, in the opinion of the State, is **not adequately represented** in the services under the State. Notice that `16(4)` is an *enabling* provision—not a mandatory Fundamental Right to demand reservation (*Mukesh Kumar v. State of Uttarakhand*, 2020).
3. **`Article 16(4A)` — Reservation in Promotion & Consequential Seniority (`77th Amendment, 1995` & `85th Amendment, 2001`):** After *Indra Sawhney* (1992) barred reservation in promotions, Parliament inserted `16(4A)` via the `77th Amendment` to allow reservation in **promotions for SCs and STs** (note: **NOT OBCs!**). Later, after *Ajit Singh* (1999) applied the "catch-up rule" to seniority, the `85th Amendment` (retrospective to 1995) added **consequential seniority** for promoted SC/ST government servants.
4. **`Article 16(4B)` — Carry-Forward / Backlog Vacancies Rule (`81st Amendment, 2000`):** Allows unfilled reserved vacancies from a previous year to be carried forward as a **separate class of backlog vacancies**, excluded from computing the **50% annual ceiling** on reservations in the succeeding year.
5. **`Article 16(5)` — Religious Denominational Offices (Original):** Permits a law requiring the incumbent of an office connected with a religious or denominational institution (or a member of its governing body) to profess that particular religion or belong to that denomination.
6. **`Article 16(6)` — 10% EWS Job Reservation (`103rd Amendment, 2019`):** Empowers the State to reserve up to **10% of appointments or posts** in favour of Economically Weaker Sections (in addition to existing quotas), upheld alongside `15(6)` in *Janhit Abhiyan* (2022).

|---|---|---|
| **Beneficiaries** | Citizens only | Citizens only |
| **Prohibited Grounds ("only")** | **5 grounds:** Religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth | **7 grounds:** Religion, race, caste, sex, **descent**, place of birth, **residence** |

#### Landmark Reservation Jurisprudence: From Mandal to *Davinder Singh* (2024)

Reservation law sits at the very intersection of `Articles 14`, `15`, `16`, and `335` (which directs that SC/ST claims in services be taken into consideration consistently with "maintenance of efficiency of administration"). Five landmark Constitution Bench rulings define the modern architecture:

1. ***Indra Sawhney v. Union of India* (1992, 9-Judge Bench, 6–3 — Mandal Case):** Following the Morarji Desai government's **Second Backward Classes Commission (B.P. Mandal Commission, 1979–80)**—which identified 3,743 socially and educationally backward castes constituting ~52% of India's population—the V.P. Singh government notified **27% OBC reservation** in central jobs (1990), and the Narasimha Rao government added 10% for economically backward general castes (1991). The nine-judge bench delivered seven foundational rulings:
   - Upheld **27% OBC reservation** under `Article 16(4)`, holding that caste can be a starting indicator of social backwardness in India, and clarified that `16(4)` is a *facet* of equality under `16(1)`, not an exception to it.
   - Struck down the 1991 executive **10% purely economic quota** as impermissible under `Article 16(4)` as it then stood (which later prompted the `103rd Constitutional Amendment`).
   - Mandated exclusion of the **Creamy Layer** (socially and economically advanced sections) from OBC benefits—subsequently operationalised via the **Ram Nandan Committee (1993)** and updated income ceilings.
   - Fixed a general **50% ceiling** on vertical reservations per year (barring extraordinary situations in far-flung/remote areas).
   - Distinguished **vertical reservations** (social groups: SC, ST, OBC under `16(4)`) from **horizontal reservations** (interlocking categories like women, persons with disabilities, ex-servicemen under `16(1)`/`15(3)`), which cut across vertical categories without breaching the 50% cap.
   - Held that `Article 16(4)` allows **sub-classification of OBCs** into "backward" and "more backward," barred **reservation in promotions** (later overridden for SCs/STs by `77th Amendment`), and directed creation of a permanent statutory body (**National Commission for Backward Classes**, later granted constitutional status as `Article 338B` by the `102nd Amendment, 2018`).
2. ***M. Nagaraj v. Union of India* (2006, 5-Judge Bench):** Upheld the constitutional validity of the `77th`, `81st`, `82nd` (relaxing evaluation marks under `Art. 335`), and `85th` Amendments (`Articles 16(4A)` and `16(4B)`), but subjected SC/ST promotion reservations to **three controlling conditions**: the State must collect **quantifiable data** showing (i) backwardness of the class, (ii) inadequacy of representation in the specific service/cadre, and (iii) compliance with overall administrative efficiency under `Article 335`, while respecting the 50% cap and creamy layer principle.
3. ***Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta* (2018, 5-Judge Bench):** Modified *M. Nagaraj* in two crucial ways. First, it **struck down the requirement to collect quantifiable data on the backwardness of SCs and STs** (since SCs/STs are presumed backward by constitutional notification under `Articles 341–342`), while retaining the requirement of quantifiable data on **inadequate representation** and efficiency (`Article 335`). Second, it affirmed that the **creamy layer exclusion principle** applies to SCs and STs in promotions as a facet of `Article 14` equality.
4. ***Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v. Chief Minister, Maharashtra* (May 2021, 5-Judge Bench — Maratha Quota Case):** Struck down Maharashtra's Maratha reservation law for breaching *Indra Sawhney*'s **50% ceiling** without demonstrating "extraordinary circumstances," and interpreted the `102nd Amendment` as divesting States of the power to identify SEBCs—prompting Parliament to pass the **`105th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2021`** restoring States' power to maintain their own State OBC lists.
5. ***State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* (1 August 2024, 7-Judge Bench, 6–1):** Overruling the 2004 five-judge precedent in ***E.V. Chinnaiah v. State of Andhra Pradesh*** (which had held SCs under `Article 341` formed one indivisible homogeneous group), the Constitution Bench declared that **States have full constitutional authority to sub-classify Scheduled Castes (and Scheduled Tribes)** to ensure more equitable distribution of quota benefits to the most marginalised sub-castes within the list.

### Article 17: Abolition of Untouchability

`Article 17` is unique in Part III for its uncompromising, absolute brevity: *"‘Untouchability’ is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden. The enforcement of any disability arising out of ‘Untouchability’ shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law."* Notice four defining traits: it has **no exceptions or qualifications**; it is enforceable **horizontally against private persons** as well as the State; in ***People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India* (1982)**, the Supreme Court held that whenever `Article 17` is violated by private citizens, it is the **constitutional duty of the State** to intervene and punish the perpetrators; and it is **not self-executing** for criminal penalties, requiring Parliament to legislate punishments under `Article 35`.

Neither the Constitution nor any statute formally defines **"untouchability."** In ***Devarajiah v. Padmanna* (1958, Mysore HC)**—consistently endorsed by the Supreme Court—the judiciary clarified that the quotation marks around *"Untouchability"* signify it does **not** cover literal or grammatical untouchability (such as quarantine during contagious disease or social boycott over personal disputes), but specifically the **historical caste-based social practice** imposing pollution, stigma, and civic disabilities upon certain classes solely by reason of birth. In the *Sabarimala* judgment (2018), Justice D.Y. Chandrachud read `Article 17` expansively to encompass menstrual exclusion practices rooted in notions of ritual "purity and pollution," though that broader reading is part of the pending nine-judge reference.

#### Parliamentary Enforcement Architecture

Exercising its exclusive punitive power under `Article 35`, Parliament enacted two cornerstone statutes:
- **Protection of Civil Rights (PCR) Act, 1955:** Originally enacted as the *Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955*, it was comprehensively strengthened and renamed in **1976**. It defines "civil right" as any right accruing to a person by reason of the abolition of untouchability, makes offences cognisable and non-compoundable, punishes denial of entry to places of worship, shops, hospitals, educational institutions, or preaching/justifying untouchability on historical or philosophical grounds, and disqualifies convicts from contesting Parliamentary or State Legislature elections under the Representation of the People Act, 1951.
- **Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989:** Enacted because the PCR Act proved insufficient against violent caste atrocities. It enumerates specific atrocities (land dispossession, bonded labour, social/economic boycott, humiliation), establishes Special Courts for speedy trial, mandates victim compensation and rehabilitation, and restricts anticipatory bail under Section 18. After a two-judge bench in *Subhash Kashinath Mahajan* (2018) diluted arrest safeguards by requiring preliminary inquiry and prior sanction, Parliament enacted the **SC/ST (PoA) Amendment Act, 2018** (inserting Section 18A), whose constitutional validity was upheld by a three-judge bench in ***Prathvi Raj Chauhan v. Union of India* (2020)**—clarifying that anticipatory bail remains barred unless *prima facie* no offence under the Act is made out.

### Article 18: Abolition of Titles

To cultivate an egalitarian republican ethos and extinguish the colonial hierarchy of hereditary titles—such as *Maharaja*, *Raj Bahadur*, *Rai Bahadur*, *Rai Saheb*, *Dewan Bahadur*, and *Khan Bahadur*—`Article 18` abolishes titles through four distinct clauses:

1. **`Article 18(1)`:** Prohibits the State from conferring any title—**except a military or academic distinction** (such as *Param Vir Chakra*, *Major General*, *Doctor*, or *Professor*)—on anybody, whether a **citizen or a foreigner**.
2. **`Article 18(2)`:** Prohibits a **citizen of India** from accepting any title from any foreign state.
3. **`Article 18(3)`:** Prohibits a **foreigner holding any office of profit or trust under the State** from accepting any title from any foreign state **without the consent of the President**.
4. **`Article 18(4)`:** Prohibits **any person (citizen or foreigner) holding an office of profit or trust under the State** from accepting any **present, emolument, or office** of any kind from or under any foreign state **without the consent of the President**.

Notice a subtle Prelims detail: unlike `Article 17` and `Article 23`, `Article 18` is **directory** in the sense that it does not itself prescribe a criminal penalty for breaching its prohibition (nor does `Article 35` mandate one), though Parliament may by law disqualify offenders or impose sanctions.

#### National Awards & *Balaji Raghavan* (1996)

In 1954, the Government of India instituted four civilian National Awards: **Bharat Ratna, Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan, and Padma Shri**. Discontinued briefly by the Janata government in 1977 on the ground that they offended `Article 18`, they were revived in 1980 and challenged before a five-judge Constitution Bench in ***Balaji Raghavan v. Union of India* (1996)**. The Supreme Court upheld their constitutional validity, drawing a sharp distinction between **hereditary/status titles** (forbidden by `Article 18`) and **National Awards recognising merit and distinguished public service** (encouraged by the Fundamental Duty to strive toward excellence under `Article 51A(j)`). However, the Court stipulated two strict conditions: (a) awardees **cannot use Bharat Ratna or Padma awards as prefixes or suffixes** to their names (on letterheads, visiting cards, or invitations), failing which they forfeit the award; and (b) selection committees must exercise rigorous scrutiny to prevent political patronage.

## 4. Right to Freedom: The Six Freedoms (Article 19)

If `Articles 14` to `18` secure the equality of citizens, `Articles 19` to `22` safeguard the personal and political liberties without which a constitutional democracy degenerates into authoritarian conformity. At the heart of this freedom cluster stands **`Article 19`**, guaranteeing six core democratic freedoms to every Indian citizen—freedoms of speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, and occupation. Because each freedom carries the capacity to clash with public order, national security, or the rights of fellow citizens, the framers engineered `Article 19` as a carefully weighted scale: clause `(1)` states the positive freedoms, while clauses `(2)` through `(6)` specify the exact, exhaustive grounds on which the State may enact "reasonable restrictions."

### Architecture of Article 19: Citizens Only & Reasonable Restrictions

Originally, `Article 19(1)` protected **seven freedoms**, including sub-clause `(f)`—the right to acquire, hold, and dispose of property—which was omitted by the **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`**. The six surviving freedoms share four foundational structural rules that UPSC Prelims tests repeatedly:

1. **Restricted Strictly to Natural Citizens:** Unlike `Articles 14` and `21`, `Article 19` belongs **exclusively to citizens of India**. Foreigners, enemy aliens, and **juristic/legal persons** such as companies, statutory corporations, or municipal bodies cannot directly invoke `Article 19` (*State Trading Corporation of India v. CTO*, 1963). However, in ***Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India* (1972)** (the Newsprint Policy case), the Supreme Court held that Indian citizen **shareholders, editors, or directors** of a newspaper company can maintain a petition under `Article 19(1)(a)` when State action directly abridges their personal right to free expression through the corporate vehicle.
2. **Vertical Protection & the Horizontal Shift (*Kaushal Kishor*, 2023):** Traditionally, `Article 19` operated solely against **State action**, not private interference (remedies against private parties lay in tort or criminal law). However, on **3 January 2023**, a five-judge Constitution Bench in ***Kaushal Kishor v. State of Uttar Pradesh*** held by a **4–1 majority** (Justice B.V. Nagarathna dissenting on this point) that Fundamental Rights under **`Articles 19` and `21` can be enforced even against private individuals and non-State entities**, and that the State bears an affirmative obligation to protect citizens' liberty against private threats.
3. **Exhaustive Grounds & Statutory Mandate:** A restriction on any `Article 19(1)` freedom is valid only if it satisfies three cumulative conditions: (a) it must be imposed by a **valid "law"** (not merely an executive circular or administrative whim); (b) it must fall strictly within one of the **enumerated grounds** in the corresponding clause `19(2)`–`(6)`—which *Kaushal Kishor* (2023) unanimously confirmed are **exhaustive** so that the State cannot invent extra grounds even to protect another Fundamental Right; and (c) the restriction must be **"reasonable."**
4. **Judicial Test of 'Reasonableness':** In ***State of Madras v. V.G. Row* (1952)**, Chief Justice Patanjali Sastri held that no abstract formula defines reasonableness; courts evaluate the nature of the right infringed, the underlying social evil, the **proportionality** between restriction and objective, and both substantive and **procedural fairness** (such as notice, hearing, and independent review). Crucially, the term "restriction" can, in extreme cases involving inherently dangerous or antisocial activities, include **total prohibition** (*Narendra Kumar v. Union of India*, 1960).

Three landmark Constitutional Amendments reshaped the text of `Article 19`:
- **`1st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951`:** Triggered by early Supreme Court free-speech rulings (*Romesh Thappar* and *Brij Bhushan*, 1950), it inserted the word **"reasonable"** into `19(2)`, added three new speech-restriction grounds (**"friendly relations with foreign states," "public order," and "incitement to an offence"**), and amended `19(6)` to authorise **State trade monopolies**.
- **`16th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963`:** Enacted following the 1962 Sino-Indian War and the recommendations of the National Integration Committee to counter secessionist movements, it inserted **"the sovereignty and integrity of India"** into clauses `19(2)`, `19(3)`, and `19(4)`.
- **`97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011`:** Added the right to form **"co-operative societies"** to `Article 19(1)(c)`.

### Article 19(1)(a) & 19(2): Freedom of Speech and Expression

Freedom of speech and expression is the lifeblood of democratic self-governance, encompassing the right to express one's views freely by word of mouth, writing, printing, pictures, films, digital broadcasts, or any other communicable medium. Though the Indian Constitution—unlike the US First Amendment—does not explicitly mention the **press**, Dr B.R. Ambedkar assured the Constituent Assembly that freedom of the press is inherently subsumed within every citizen's freedom of expression.

#### Implied Dimensions of Free Speech Expanded by the Supreme Court

Over seven decades, the Supreme Court has woven an extraordinarily rich tapestry of implied democratic rights into `Article 19(1)(a)`:

- **Freedom of the Press & Prohibition of Indirect Economic Restraints:** In ***Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras* (1950)** (striking down a ban on the weekly *Cross Roads*) and ***Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi* (1950)** (quashing pre-censorship of *Organiser*), the Court established that liberty of circulation is as vital as liberty of publication. Later, in ***Sakal Papers* (1962)** (invalidating a price-page schedule) and ***Bennett Coleman* (1972)** (striking down a 10-page newsprint cap), the Court ruled that the State cannot indirectly throttle editorial freedom under the guise of regulating business or raw material. In ***Indian Express Newspapers* (1985)**, excessive customs duties on newsprint were subjected to strict scrutiny.
- **Right to Know & Freedom of Information:** Tracing back to *State of U.P. v. Raj Narain* (1975) and ***S.P. Gupta v. Union of India* (1981)**, the Court held that democratic accountability requires citizens to know what their government does—a constitutional foundation eventually codified by Parliament in the **Right to Information (RTI) Act, 2005**.
- **Voters' Right to Information & Electoral Transparency:** In ***Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR)* (2002)** and ***PUCL v. Union of India* (2003)**, the Court held that voters have a Fundamental Right under `Article 19(1)(a)` to know the criminal antecedents, assets, liabilities, and educational qualifications of electoral candidates. In ***PUCL v. Union of India* (2013)**, it recognised the voter's right to cast a negative vote via **NOTA (None of the Above)** on EVMs. Culminating this line of jurisprudence, on **15 February 2024**, a unanimous five-judge Constitution Bench in ***Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India*** struck down the **Electoral Bonds Scheme (2018)** as unconstitutional, holding that anonymous corporate and individual political funding violates voters' right to essential information needed to cast an informed ballot.
- **Commercial Speech:** In ***Tata Press Ltd. v. MTNL* (1995)**, reversing *Hamdard Dawakhana* (1960), the Court held that truthful commercial advertising informs consumers in a free market and enjoys `Article 19(1)(a)` protection (though deceptive or prohibited advertisements remain regulable).
- **Right to Silence:** In ***Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala* (1986)**, three Jehovah's Witnesses schoolgirls stood silently and respectfully during the National Anthem but refrained from singing it due to religious conviction. Overturning their expulsion, the Supreme Court held that freedom of expression includes the **right to remain silent**, and no legal provision compels singing where respectful posture is maintained.
- **Symbolic Speech & National Flag:** In ***Union of India v. Naveen Jindal* (2004)**, flying the National Flag with respect and dignity was recognised as a protected expression of patriotism under `Article 19(1)(a)`.
- **Freedom of Broadcast & Airwaves:** In *Secretary, Ministry of Information & Broadcasting v. Cricket Association of Bengal* (1995), the Court declared that **airwaves are public property** and must be regulated by an independent public authority rather than monopolised by the government.
- **Freedom of Speech & Trade Over the Internet:** In ***Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (January 2020)**—arising from the prolonged communication lockdown in Jammu and Kashmir after August 2019—the Supreme Court held that freedom of speech (`19(1)(a)`) and freedom to practise any profession or trade (`19(1)(g)`) **through the medium of the internet** are constitutionally protected. Indefinite internet shutdowns are impermissible; every suspension order under the Telecom Suspension Rules must be **published**, strictly **necessary and proportionate**, temporary, and subject to periodic review.
- **Right Against Bandh (Forced Shutdown):** Endorsing the Kerala High Court in ***CPI(M) v. Bharat Kumar* (1998)**, the Supreme Court held that calling and enforcing a coercive **bandh** violates the Fundamental Rights of ordinary citizens (`Articles 19` and `21`), distinguishing peaceful *hartals* or voluntary strikes from forcible shutdowns.
- **Pre-Censorship of Films vs Print Media:** In ***K.A. Abbas v. Union of India* (1970)**, the Court upheld pre-censorship of motion pictures (CBFC certification under the Cinematograph Act, 1952) as distinct from print media because cinema's vivid visual impact reaches vast audiences instantly, though censorship standards must remain narrow and rational. In ***S. Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram* (1989)** (the *Ore Oru Gramathile* film case), the Court formulated the celebrated **"spark in a powder keg" test**: expression cannot be suppressed unless the anticipated danger is proximate and directly linked to the speech—the State cannot plead its own inability to maintain law and order as an excuse to capitulate to hecklers' vetoes!

> [!case]
> ***Shreya Singhal v. Union of India* (2015 — Two-Judge Bench: J. Chelameswar & R.F. Nariman JJ.)**
> - **Challenge:** Constitutionality of **Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000**, which criminalised sending "grossly offensive," "menacing," or annoying electronic messages (invoked to arrest two young women over a Facebook post questioning a Mumbai shutdown).
> - **Core Holding:** Struck down Section 66A **in its entirety** as violative of `Article 19(1)(a)` and unsaved by `Article 19(2)`. Drawing a sharp doctrinal triad—**Discussion, Advocacy, and Incitement**—the Court held that discussion and even unpopular advocacy lie at the core of `Article 19(1)(a)`; only when expression crosses the threshold into **incitement** to public disorder or an offence can `Article 19(2)` be triggered. Undefined, elastic terms ("annoyance," "inconvenience," "grossly offensive") suffer from **vagueness and overbreadth**, creating an unconstitutional **chilling effect** on free speech.
> - **Secondary Holding:** Read down **Section 79(3)(b)** (intermediary safe harbour) so social media platforms need only take down content upon a **court order or valid government direction**, not upon private complaints; upheld **Section 69A** (website blocking) due to its built-in procedural safeguards.

#### The Eight Grounds Under Article 19(2) & Contemporary Free-Speech Tensions

Every restriction on speech must tether directly to one of the **eight grounds** in `Article 19(2)`:
1. **Sovereignty and integrity of India** (`16th Amendment, 1963`): Prevents advocacy of secession or territorial dismemberment.
2. **Security of the State:** Covers grave threats such as rebellion, armed insurrection, espionage, or waging war—distinct from ordinary breaches of peace.
3. **Friendly relations with foreign states** (`1st Amendment, 1951`): Bars malicious propaganda endangering diplomatic ties, while permitting bona fide criticism of foreign policy.
4. **Public order** (`1st Amendment, 1951`): In ***Ram Manohar Lohia v. State of Bihar* (1966)**, Justice M. Hidayatullah illustrated the famous **three concentric circles**: the widest circle is **Law and Order** (isolated individual crimes affecting a few); the middle circle is **Public Order** (disturbances disrupting the even tempo of community life); and the smallest, most severe inner circle is **Security of the State** (upheavals threatening the state itself). Mere "law and order" problems do **NOT** justify restricting speech under `Article 19(2)`!
5. **Decency or morality:** Assessed through contemporary **community standards** (*Aveek Sarkar v. State of West Bengal*, 2014, replacing the archaic Victorian Hicklin test used in *Ranjit Udeshi*, 1965).
6. **Contempt of court:** Regulated by the **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** (civil contempt: wilful disobedience of court orders; criminal contempt: scandalising the court or interfering with administration of justice—fair criticism of judgments is protected).
7. **Defamation:** Protects personal reputation (`Article 21`); in ***Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India* (2016)**, the Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of **criminal defamation** (formerly IPC ss. 499–500; now BNS s. 356) as a proportionate restriction under `Article 19(2)`.
8. **Incitement to an offence** (`1st Amendment, 1951`): Bars speech directly instigating commission of any legally defined offence.

**Sedition (IPC Section 124A → BNS Section 152) & Hate Speech:** In ***Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar* (1962, Constitution Bench)**, the Supreme Court upheld colonial-era **Section 124A IPC** (sedition) only by reading it down narrowly to speech that incites **violence or creates public disorder**, explicitly protecting strong criticism of government policies. Amid widespread misuse against journalists and dissenters, a three-judge bench in ***S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India* (May 2022)** placed all pending Section 124A prosecutions in **abeyance** pending reconsideration by a larger bench. Parliament subsequently replaced the IPC with the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023** (effective **1 July 2024**), dropping the word "sedition" while enacting **Section 152**, which punishes acts (including electronic communication or financial means) purposely or knowingly exciting secession, armed rebellion, subversive activities, or endangering the **sovereignty, unity, and integrity of India**—keeping the debate over broad phrasing alive. Meanwhile, on **hate speech** (*Amish Devgan v. Union of India*, 2020; *Shaheen Abdullah*, 2022–23), the Court has distinguished free political speech from dehumanising hate speech targeting protected groups, directing police across all States to register *suo motu* FIRs against hate speeches without waiting for formal complaints.

> - ***ADR v. ### Article 19(1)(b) & 19(3): Freedom of Peaceful Assembly

Every citizen has the right to **assemble peaceably and without arms**—covering public meetings, demonstrations, and processions on public land. Three key constitutional boundaries apply:
- **Peaceful and Unarmed Only:** Violent, riotous, disorderly, or armed assemblies enjoy zero constitutional protection. Under Section 189 of the BNSS / Section 141 IPC (now BNS s. 189), an assembly of **five or more persons** with an unlawful object becomes an unlawful assembly.
- **No Fundamental Right to Strike:** The right to assemble or demonstrate peacefully does **NOT** include a right to strike (*Kameshwar Prasad v. State of Bihar*, 1962; ***T.K. Rangarajan v. Government of Tamil Nadu*, 2003**—holding that government employees have no fundamental, statutory, moral, or equitable right to strike, though industrial workers have a regulated statutory right under the Industrial Relations Code, 2020).
- **Preventive Restrictions (`Article 19(3)`):** Restrictable solely on **two grounds**—**sovereignty and integrity of India** and **public order**. Section 163 of the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023** (formerly **Section 144 CrPC**) empowers magistrates to prohibit assemblies in urgent cases of apprehended danger. In *Anuradha Bhasin* (2020), the Supreme Court held that Section 144/163 orders cannot be used as a tool to suppress legitimate democratic dissent and **cannot be issued repetitively** to create indefinite bans. Equally, in ***Amit Sahni v. Commissioner of Police* (7 October 2020 — the Shaheen Bagh case)**, the Court held that while democracy and dissent go hand in hand, the right to peaceful protest under `Article 19(1)(b)` must be balanced against commuters' right to free movement (`19(1)(d)`): **public ways and thoroughfares cannot be occupied indefinitely**, and protests must take place in designated areas.

### Article 19(1)(c) & 19(4): Freedom of Association, Unions & Co-operative Societies

`Article 19(1)(c)` guarantees citizens the right to form **associations or unions or co-operative societies**—encompassing political parties, companies, partnership firms, clubs, trade unions, and professional bodies. The phrase **"or co-operative societies"** was inserted by the **`97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011`** (note: while the Supreme Court in *Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah*, July 2021, struck down Part IXB insofar as it applied to State co-operative societies for lack of State ratification under `Article 368(2)`, it **left `Article 19(1)(c)` completely intact!**).

The freedom includes the right to **start, continue, and voluntarily refuse to join** an association (*Damyanti Naranga v. Union of India*, 1971—State cannot forcibly alter the composition of a society by inducting outsiders). However, in ***All India Bank Employees' Association v. National Industrial Tribunal* (1962)**, the Supreme Court held that `Article 19(1)(c)` guarantees the right to *form* a union, **NOT a concomitant Fundamental Right** to achieve every collective bargaining objective, obtain mandatory employer recognition, declare a strike, or call a lockout. Restrictions under `Article 19(4)` can be imposed on **three grounds**: sovereignty and integrity of India, public order, and **morality** (for instance, banning unlawful terrorist organisations under the UAPA, 1967, subject to tribunal review).

### Articles 19(1)(d)–(e) & 19(5): Freedom of Movement and Residence

`Article 19(1)(d)` (to move freely throughout the territory of India) and `Article 19(1)(e)` (to reside and settle in any part of India) are twin pillars of **national integration and single citizenship**, designed to dismantle provincial parochialism and treat India as one unified civic space.
- **Internal vs External Movement (Classic Prelims Trap!):** `Article 19(1)(d)` covers **internal movement within India** ONLY. The right to travel **abroad** and return to India falls under personal liberty in **`Article 21`** (*Satwant Singh Sawhney*, 1967; *Maneka Gandhi*, 1978), NOT `Article 19(1)(d)`!
- **Reside vs Settle:** "Reside" denotes staying temporarily in any place; "settle" denotes setting up a permanent home or domicile there.
- **Two Grounds of Restriction (`Article 19(5)`):**
  1. **Interests of the general public:** Permits externment orders against habitual criminals (*N.B. Khare*, 1950), epidemic quarantine measures, and restrictions on prostitutes operating in specified residential areas (*State of U.P. v. Kaushalya*, 1964).
  2. **Protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe:** Designed to safeguard the distinctive culture, language, customary laws, and land ownership of tribal communities against demographic swamping and commercial exploitation by outsiders. This constitutional ground directly sustains the **Inner Line Permit (ILP)** regime under the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation, 1873 (currently operating in **Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram, and Manipur**), as well as restrictions on non-tribal land acquisition in Fifth and Sixth Schedule areas.

### Article 19(1)(g) & 19(6): Freedom of Profession, Occupation, Trade or Business

`Article 19(1)(g)` secures the economic liberty of every citizen to practise any profession or carry on any occupation, trade, or business—including street vending (*Sodan Singh v. NDMC*, 1989, subject to regulation) and closing down a business (*Excel Wear*, 1978, subject to reasonable labour safeguards). However, under the doctrine of ***res extra commercium*** (things outside lawful commerce), citizens have **NO Fundamental Right** to carry on inherently dangerous, immoral, or noxious trades—such as human trafficking, adulterated foods, gambling/betting (*Chamarbaugwalla*, 1957), or trade in intoxicating **liquor** (*Khoday Distilleries v. State of Karnataka*, 1995), which the State may completely prohibit or tightly control.

`Article 19(6)` authorises three distinct forms of restriction:
1. **Interests of the general public:** Reasonable regulation of labour standards, minimum wages, environmental norms, licensing, price controls, and consumer safety.
2. **Professional or technical qualifications:** Prescribing degrees, licensing exams, or registration for doctors, advocates, architects, chartered accountants, or pilots.
3. **State Monopoly (`1st Amendment, 1951`):** Empowering the State (or a State-owned corporation) to carry on any trade, business, industry, or service—whether to the **complete or partial exclusion of citizens** (such as atomic energy, railways, or nationalised road transport). Crucially, a law creating a State monopoly under `19(6)(ii)` is presumptively reasonable and need not separately justify its reasonableness in restricting private competitors (*Akadasi Padhan*, 1963).

> [!prelims]
> **Article 19 Prelims Traps to Master:**
> - **Right to Travel Abroad:** Protected under **`Article 21`**, NOT `Article 19(1)(d)`!
> - **Concentric Circles (`Ram Manohar Lohia`, 1966):** Mere "law and order" is **NOT** a ground in `Article 19(2)`—only **public order** and **security of the State** are!
> - **Morality vs Public Order:** Notice which clauses include **"morality"** (`19(2)` and `19(4)`) and which do NOT (`19(3)` peaceful assembly has only 2 grounds: sovereignty/integrity & public order!).
> - **Right to Strike / Bandh:** Neither `19(1)(b)` nor `19(1)(c)` confers a Fundamental Right to strike (*T.K. Rangarajan*, 2003) or enforce a bandh (*Bharat Kumar*, 1998).

## 5. Protection in Respect of Conviction, Life & Personal Liberty (Articles 20–22)

If `Article 19` is the charter of active democratic citizenship, **`Articles 20`, `21`, `21A`, and `22`** form the citadel of **personal liberty, human dignity, and criminal justice**. Available to **all persons**—citizens and foreigners alike (save enemy aliens under `Article 22(3)`)—these provisions guard the human body and mind against the most coercive power of the State: arrest, criminal punishment, preventive detention, and deprivation of life. Most remarkably, following the **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`**, **`Articles 20` and `21` can NEVER be suspended** even during the gravest National Emergency.

### Article 20: Protection in Respect of Conviction for Offences (Non-Suspendable)

`Article 20` shields every person accused of a crime—whether citizen, foreigner, or legal person such as a company—against arbitrary and excessive criminal punishment through three classic procedural guarantees:

#### Clause 20(1): Protection Against Ex-Post-Facto Criminal Laws

No person shall be: (a) **convicted** of any offence except for violation of a law in force at the time of the commission of the act; nor (b) **subjected to a penalty greater** than that which might have been inflicted under the law in force at the time of the commission of the offence. Four Prelims nuances are vital:
- **Applies ONLY to Criminal Laws:** Legislatures retain full power to enact **retrospective civil liabilities or tax laws** (*Kedar Nath v. State of West Bengal*, 1953).
- **Excludes Preventive Detention & Procedural Changes:** `Article 20(1)` bars retrospective *conviction* and *enhanced sentence*, not retrospective changes in rules of criminal trial procedure or evidence (unless they create a new offence or heavier punishment), nor does it apply to preventive detention or requiring security bonds.
- **Permits Beneficial Reduction of Punishment:** In ***T. Barai v. Henry Ah Hoe* (1983)**, the Supreme Court held that `Article 20(1)` does NOT bar the retrospective application of a new law that **reduces** or softens the punishment for a past offence—only retrospective *enhancement* is forbidden.

#### Clause 20(2): Protection Against Double Jeopardy

*"No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once"*—reflecting the common-law maxim *nemo debet bis vexari* (no one should be vexed twice for the same cause). Note two critical legal limitations that UPSC regularly tests:
- **Both 'Prosecuted AND Punished' Required:** Unlike the broader American Fifth Amendment (which bars a second prosecution even after acquittal), `Article 20(2)` uses the conjunctive **"and"**—meaning there must have been both a previous **prosecution and punishment** (*Venkataraman v. Union of India*, 1954). (Retrial after acquittal is instead regulated statutorily under Section 337 BNSS / Section 300 CrPC.)
- **Judicial Proceedings ONLY — Not Departmental Inquiries:** In ***Maqbool Hussain v. State of Bombay* (1953)** and ***Thomas Dana* (1959)**, the Supreme Court held that `Article 20(2)` applies **exclusively to proceedings before a court of law or judicial tribunal**. Hence, a customs authority confiscating smuggled gold, or a government department dismissing a civil servant after a disciplinary inquiry, does **NOT** bar subsequent criminal prosecution in a court for the same conduct!

#### Clause 20(3): Prohibition Against Self-Incrimination

*"No person accused of any offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself."* Built on the adversarial presumption of innocence, this protection requires three cumulative elements: (i) the person must be **formally accused of an offence** (e.g., via FIR or formal complaint—does not cover civil or administrative witnesses); (ii) there must be **compulsion** (voluntary confessions recorded before a magistrate under BNSS s. 183 / CrPC s. 164 are admissible); and (iii) the compulsion must be to furnish **testimonial evidence** (oral or documentary statements imparting personal knowledge).

In ***State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad* (1961, 11-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court clarified that "to be a witness" does **NOT** prohibit the compulsory collection of **physical/material evidence**—such as **thumb impressions, palm prints, specimen handwriting, signatures, blood samples, DNA profiles, voice samples (*Ritesh Sinha*, 2019), or compulsory exhibition of the body**—because these do not compel mental testimony or change their intrinsic character. By contrast, in the landmark ruling ***Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010, 3-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court held that **involuntary administration of narco-analysis, polygraph (lie-detector), and Brain Electrical Activation Profile (BEAP / brain-mapping) tests** is **unconstitutional**, violating both **`Article 20(3)`** (forcibly extracting mental testimony) and **`Article 21`** (mental privacy and bodily integrity). Even with voluntary consent, results are inadmissible as substantive evidence, though derivative physical recoveries under Section 23 BSA (formerly Section 27, Evidence Act) remain permissible if strictly guided by NHRC norms.

### Article 21: Life and Personal Liberty — From *Gopalan* to the Golden Triangle

`Article 21` consists of a single sentence of deceptive simplicity: *"No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to **procedure established by law**."* Available to citizens and non-citizens alike, no single sentence in the Constitution has undergone a more dramatic judicial metamorphosis—evolving from a weak procedural shield in 1950 into the overarching reservoir of human dignity, socio-economic justice, environmental safety, and privacy in the twenty-first century.

#### Phase I: *A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* (1950) — Narrow Positivist Reading

In the Constituent Assembly, Sir B.N. Rau had advised dropping the American phrase **"due process of law"** (fearing judicial veto over land reforms and welfare legislation, as happened in the US *Lochner* era) in favour of the **Japanese Constitution's** phrase **"procedure established by law."** Months after the Constitution took effect, communist leader A.K. Gopalan challenged his preventive detention under the Preventive Detention Act, 1950. By a 5–1 majority (Justice Fazl Ali famously dissenting), the Supreme Court adopted a strictly textualist interpretation:
- **"Law" meant *lex* (enacted State statute), NOT *jus* (natural justice):** So long as Parliament or a State Legislature enacted *some* procedure, however harsh, arbitrary, or unfair, courts could not examine its reasonableness.
- **Protection Against Executive Only:** `Article 21` restrained arbitrary executive action, NOT arbitrary legislation.
- **Siloed / Compartmentalised Reading of Part III:** `Articles 19`, `21`, and `22` were treated as mutually exclusive silos—a preventive detention law valid under `Article 22` did not need to satisfy `Article 19` or `Article 14`.

This narrow positivism reached its darkest hour during the 1975–77 Emergency in ***ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla* (1976, 5-Judge Bench, 4–1 — Habeas Corpus Case)**, where four judges (CJI A.N. Ray, Beg, Chandrachud, and Bhagwati JJ.) held that once the President suspended `Article 21` enforcement under `Article 359`, no detainee had *locus standi* to seek a writ of *habeas corpus*—even if detained illegally or killed without authority! **Justice H.R. Khanna's immortal lone dissent** declared that the right to life and liberty is a pre-constitutional human right that the Constitution merely recognises, not gifts—and cost him the Chief Justiceship. Parliament promptly vindicated Khanna's dissent via the **`44th Amendment (1978)`** making `Articles 20` and `21` non-suspendable, and a nine-judge bench in ***K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017)** formally overruled *ADM Jabalpur*, burying it as a constitutional stain.

#### Phase II: *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) — The Golden Triangle & Substantive Due Process

When the Janata government impounded journalist Maneka Gandhi's passport in "public interest" under Section 10(3)(c) of the Passports Act, 1967, without furnishing reasons or a hearing, a seven-judge Constitution Bench decisively dismantled *Gopalan* (building on *R.C. Cooper*, 1970) and transformed Indian constitutional law forever:
1. **Procedure Must Be "Just, Fair and Reasonable":** The procedure depriving life or personal liberty under `Article 21` cannot be "arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive"; it must conform to principles of natural justice (*audi alteram partem* — right to be heard). In substance, the Supreme Court **imported American "substantive and procedural due process of law"** into India's "procedure established by law."
2. **The Golden Triangle (`Articles 14`, `19`, and `21`):** Part III is not a set of watertight compartments. Any law restricting personal liberty under `Article 21` must simultaneously pass the **non-arbitrariness test of `Article 14`** and the **reasonableness test of `Article 19`**—forming what Justice Chandrachud later termed the **Golden Triangle** of the Constitution (*Minerva Mills*, 1980).
3. **Expansive Meaning of 'Life' and 'Personal Liberty':** Endorsing Justice Field's US dictum (*Munn v. Illinois*), the Court held that "life" is not mere animal existence—it encompasses every facet that makes human existence meaningful, dignified, and worthwhile.

```flow
# The Golden Triangle (Articles 14, 19 & 21) After Maneka Gandhi (1978)
A.K. Gopalan (1950) — Compartmentalised Silos | Narrow 'procedure established by law'; restrained executive only; no fairness test on statutes
Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978 — 7-Judge Bench) | Overruled Gopalan's siloed approach; fused Arts. 14, 19 & 21 into an integrated constitutional test
Article 14: Non-Arbitrariness | Law & procedure must be fair, non-capricious, non-discriminatory || Article 19: Reasonable Restrictions | Must satisfy specific grounds & proportionality || Article 21: Just, Fair & Reasonable Procedure | Imports substantive due process & human dignity
Modern Rights Standard | Any State deprivation of liberty must clear ALL three limbs + Puttaswamy proportionality
```

### The Expanded Horizon of Article 21: 35+ Implied Fundamental Rights

Since *Maneka Gandhi* (1978), the Supreme Court has recognised **more than 35 distinct unenumerated Fundamental Rights** as integral facets of `Article 21`—a catalogue routinely tested in UPSC Prelims ("Which of the following is protected under Article 21?"):

- **Dignity, Livelihood & Shelter:** (1) Right to live with **human dignity** (*Francis Coralie Mullin*, 1981); (2) Right to **livelihood** (*Olga Tellis v. BMC*, 1985 — Bombay pavement dwellers); (3) Right to **shelter** (*Chameli Singh*, 1996; reinforced by the 13 November 2024 pan-India **Bulldozer Demolition Guidelines**); (4) Right to **social and economic justice and empowerment**; (5) Right to **social security and protection of the family**; (6) Right to **family pension**; (7) Right to **appropriate life insurance policy** (*LIC v. Consumer Education & Research Centre*, 1995); (8) Right to **electricity** and basic civic amenities; (9) Right to **sleep** (*In Re: Ramlila Maidan Incident*, 2012 — midnight police crackdown on sleeping demonstrators).
- **Health, Environment & Climate Justice:** (10) Right to **health** and safe working conditions (*CERC*, 1995); (11) Right to **emergency medical aid** (*Parmanand Katara*, 1989); (12) Right to **timely medical treatment in government hospitals** (*Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity*, 1996); (13) Right to a **decent, pollution-free environment, clean water, and clean air** (*Subhash Kumar*, 1991; *M.C. Mehta* cases); (14) Right to **sustainable development** (*Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum*, 1996); (15) Freedom from **noise pollution** (*In Re: Noise Pollution*, 2005); (16) Right to be **free from the adverse effects of climate change** (*M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India*, 21 March 2024).
- **Criminal Justice & Prisoners' Rights:** (17) Right to **speedy trial** (*Hussainara Khatoon*, 1979); (18) Right to **free legal aid** (*M.H. Hoskot*, 1978; *Khatri*, 1981); (19) Right to a **fair trial** (*Zahira Habibullah Sheikh*, 2004); (20) Right against **solitary confinement** (*Sunil Batra*, 1978); (21) Right against **bar fetters** (*Charles Sobraj*, 1978); (22) Right against **handcuffing** (*Prem Shankar Shukla*, 1980); (23) Right against **inhuman treatment and custodial torture** (*D.K. Basu*, 1997); (24) Right against **delayed execution** of death sentence (*T.V. Vatheeswaran*, 1983; *Shatrughan Chauhan*, 2014); (25) Right against **public hanging** (*Attorney General v. Lachma Devi*, 1985); (26) Right of prisoners to **basic necessities** (adequate nutrition, clothing, reading/writing, family interviews); (27) Right to **appeal from a judgment of conviction**; (28) Right against **bonded labour** (*Bandhua Mukti Morcha*, 1984).
- **Autonomy, Identity, Family & Civic Dignity:** (29) Right to **privacy** (*K.S. Puttaswamy*, 2017); (30) Right to **travel abroad** (*Satwant Singh*, 1967; *Maneka Gandhi*, 1978); (31) Right to **reputation** (*Subramanian Swamy*, 2016); (32) Right to **hearing** and **information**; (33) Right of women to be treated with **decency and dignity** and protection from **workplace sexual harassment** (*Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan*, 1997); (34) Right to **reproductive choices** (*Suchita Srivastava*, 2009; *X v. Principal Secretary, Health*, 2022 — extending safe abortion access under MTP Act to unmarried women); (35) Right to **self-identified gender identity** for transgender persons (*NALSA v. Union of India*, 2014); (36) Right to **choose one's life partner** free from family/khap coercion (*Shafin Jahan v. Asokan K.M.*, 2018 — Hadiya case; *Shakti Vahini*, 2018); (37) Right to **die with dignity** via passive euthanasia (*Common Cause*, 2018); (38) Right to **free education up to age 14** (*Unni Krishnan*, 1993 — elevated into `Article 21A`); (39) Right not to be driven out of a State and right to **road access in hilly areas** (*State of H.P. v. Umed Ram Sharma*, 1986).

Four transformative modern `Article 21` rulings demand deeper analytical mastery for both Prelims and Mains:

#### 1. Right to Privacy & the 4-Fold Proportionality Test (*K.S. Puttaswamy*, 2017 & 2018)

On **24 August 2017**, a unanimous **nine-judge Constitution Bench** in ***Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India*** (*Puttaswamy I*) overruled *M.P. Sharma* (1954) and *Kharak Singh* (1962), declaring the **Right to Privacy** an intrinsic, inalienable Fundamental Right flowing from `Article 21` and the entire dignity architecture of Part III (spanning bodily autonomy, informational privacy, and decisional autonomy). Simultaneously, it formally overruled *ADM Jabalpur* (1976).

Because privacy is not absolute, *Puttaswamy I* (2017) and the five-judge Aadhaar ruling in ***Puttaswamy II* (26 September 2018, 4–1)**—which upheld Aadhaar for welfare subsidies under Section 7 while striking down mandatory private-sector linking (Section 57) and bank/mobile linking—codified India's **Four-Fold Proportionality Test** for testing State intrusions into Fundamental Rights:

```flow
# The Puttaswamy Four-Fold Proportionality Test
Step 1: Legality | Infringement must be backed by a valid enacted law (no mere executive order)
Step 2: Legitimate State Aim | Must pursue a genuine constitutional or public-welfare goal (e.g., national security, preventing welfare leakage)
Step 3: Suitability & Necessity | Rational nexus to aim AND must be the least rights-restrictive alternative available
Step 4: Proportionality Stricto Sensu (Balancing) | Procedural safeguards against abuse + societal benefit must outweigh severity of rights intrusion
```

Fulfilling *Puttaswamy*'s legislative mandate, Parliament enacted the **Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023**, grounded in consent, purpose limitation, data fiduciaries' duties, and a Data Protection Board. In **November 2025**, the Union Government notified the **Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025**, operationalising the statutory regime across an 18-month phased compliance timeline (though debates persist over broad State exemptions under Section 17).

#### 2. Right to Die with Dignity & Passive Euthanasia (*Common Cause*, 2018 & 2023)

Does the "right to life" include the "right to die"? In *P. Rathinam* (1994), a two-judge bench briefly struck down Section 309 IPC (attempt to suicide), only to be swiftly overruled by a five-judge Constitution Bench in ***Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab* (1996)**, which held that `Article 21` protects life, NOT unnatural extinction of life (note: Section 115 of the **Mental Healthcare Act, 2017** now presumes severe stress for suicide attempts, effectively decriminalising it, and the BNS 2023 drops standalone attempt to suicide except when used to compel/restrain a public servant under s. 226).

Crucially, *Gian Kaur* distinguished suicide from **dying with dignity** when the natural process of death has already commenced. Following ***Aruna Ramachandra Shanbaug v. Union of India* (2011)** (which first allowed passive euthanasia subject to High Court approval), a five-judge Constitution Bench in ***Common Cause (A Regd. Society) v. Union of India* (9 March 2018)** unanimously held that the **right to die with dignity** is a Fundamental Right under `Article 21`. It legalised **passive euthanasia** (withdrawing life-support systems or medical treatment from terminally ill or persistently vegetative patients—distinct from **active euthanasia**, which remains illegal) and gave legal force to **Advance Medical Directives ("Living Wills")**. Because the 2018 execution procedure (requiring a Judicial Magistrate First Class countersignature) proved unworkable, a five-judge bench in **January 2023** simplified the guidelines: living wills now require attestation before two independent witnesses and certification by a **notary or gazetted officer**, with a two-tier hospital medical board verifying futility within 48 hours each.

#### 3. Right Against the Adverse Effects of Climate Change (*M.K. Ranjitsinh*, 2024)

On **21 March 2024**, a three-judge bench (CJI D.Y. Chandrachud, J.B. Pardiwala, and Manoj Misra JJ.) in ***M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India***—a case balancing conservation of the critically endangered **Great Indian Bustard** against overhead solar power transmission lines in Gujarat and Rajasthan—explicitly articulated a distinct Fundamental Right: the **right to be free from the adverse effects of climate change**, anchored jointly in **`Article 21`** (life, health, food security, clean environment) and **`Article 14`** (climate inequality disproportionately devastating marginalised and underserved communities), read harmoniously with `Article 48A` (DPSP) and `Article 51A(g)` (Fundamental Duty). The ruling elevated climate adaptation and India's clean-energy transition from policy choices into enforceable constitutional imperatives.

#### 4. Marriage Equality & Intimate Autonomy (*Supriyo v. Union of India*, 2023)

Following *NALSA* (2014) and *Navtej Singh Johar* (2018), queer couples petitioned the Supreme Court seeking recognition of same-sex marriage under the Special Marriage Act, 1954. On **18 October 2023**, a five-judge Constitution Bench in ***Supriyo @ Supriya Chakraborty v. Union of India*** delivered a nuanced verdict across four opinions:
- **Unanimous (5–0):** There is **NO unqualified Fundamental Right to marry** under the Constitution, and courts cannot rewrite or "read into" the Special Marriage Act, 1954, to recognise same-sex marriages without usurping Parliament's legislative role. Unanimously confirmed, however, that **transgender persons in heterosexual relationships** have the right to marry under existing Indian laws.
- **Majority (3–2 — Justices S. Ravindra Bhat, Hima Kohli, and P.S. Narasimha; CJI Chandrachud and Justice S.K. Kaul dissenting):** Held that queer couples do NOT have a constitutionally enforceable right to **civil unions** or joint **adoption** under current CARA regulations (though they possess the right to cohabit and choose partners free from violence or police harassment). The Bench took note of the Union's assurance to constitute a **high-powered Cabinet Secretary committee** to examine social, financial, and welfare entitlements (such as joint bank accounts, medical consent, and ration cards) for queer partners.

### Article 21A: Right to Elementary Education

Originally, free and compulsory education for children up to age 14 stood only as a non-justiciable Directive Principle in `Article 45`. In *Mohini Jain v. State of Karnataka* (1992), the Supreme Court declared education at all levels a Fundamental Right under `Article 21`, which a Constitution Bench in ***Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (1993)** refined by limiting the enforceable Fundamental Right to **children up to the age of 14 years**.

Giving explicit constitutional form to *Unni Krishnan*, Parliament enacted the **`86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002`**—an elegant **tri-part constitutional reform** spanning Parts III, IV, and IVA simultaneously:
1. **Inserted `Article 21A` (Part III — Fundamental Right):** *"The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of **six to fourteen years** in such manner as the State may, by law, determine."* (Note: covers **elementary education only**, not higher/professional or early childhood education; available to **all children**—citizens and non-citizens alike.)
2. **Amended `Article 45` (Part IV — DPSP):** Redirected the State to endeavour to provide **early childhood care and education (ECCE)** for all children until they complete the **age of six years**.
3. **Inserted `Article 51A(k)` (Part IVA — Fundamental Duty):** Imposed a civic duty on every parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to their child/ward aged **6 to 14 years**.

To operationalise `Article 21A`, Parliament enacted the **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009** (in force from **1 April 2010**). Its landmark **Section 12(1)(c)** mandates that all **private unaided non-minority schools** reserve at least **25% of entry-level seats** for children from economically weaker sections and disadvantaged groups (with per-child fee reimbursement by the State), prohibits capitation fees and admission screening, and sets mandatory pupil-teacher ratios and infrastructure norms. While *Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan* (2012) upheld the 25% mandate for private unaided non-minority schools, a five-judge Constitution Bench in ***Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v. Union of India* (2014)** held that the **RTE Act does NOT apply to minority educational institutions—whether aided or unaided**—to preserve their autonomy under `Article 30(1)`.

### Article 22: Protection Against Arrest and Detention

`Article 22` is uniquely bifurcated into two sharply contrasting regimes reflecting the framers' acute dilemma between liberty and state survival: **clauses `(1)` and `(2)`** protect persons subjected to **punitive detention** (arrest under ordinary criminal law after an alleged offence), whereas **clauses `(4)` to `(7)`** regulate **preventive detention** (detaining a person *without trial* on reasonable suspicion to prevent a future threat to public order or national security).

#### Punitive Detention Safeguards: Clauses 22(1)–(3)

Every person arrested and detained in custody under ordinary criminal law enjoys **four mandatory constitutional rights**:
1. **Right to be informed of the grounds of arrest** as soon as may be. Reinforcing this guarantee against draconian financial and anti-terror prosecutions, the Supreme Court in ***Pankaj Bansal v. Union of India* (October 2023 — PMLA)** and ***Prabir Purkayastha v. State (NCT of Delhi)* (15 May 2024 — UAPA)** held that furnishing **written grounds of arrest** promptly is a non-negotiable requirement of `Article 22(1)`, and any arrest violating this mandate is void *ab initio*.
2. **Right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner** of one's choice.
3. **Right to be produced before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours** of arrest (excluding the time necessary for the journey from the place of arrest to the magistrate's court).
4. **Right to be released after 24 hours** unless the magistrate authorises further detention.

Supplementing `Article 22(1)–(2)`, the Supreme Court laid down **eleven mandatory arrest guidelines** in ***D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal* (1997)** (arrest memo attested by a witness, informing a friend/relative, periodic medical checks, police identification badges) and curbed routine mechanical arrests for offences punishable up to 7 years in ***Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar* (2014)** (now codified in **Section 35 BNSS, 2023**). Crucially, **`Article 22(3)`** strips ALL four punitive safeguards from two classes of persons: (a) **enemy aliens** (citizens of a country at war with India), and (b) persons arrested or detained under any **preventive detention law**. Note also that the Supreme Court (*State of Punjab v. Ajaib Singh*, 1953) held that `Article 22(1)–(2)` do NOT cover arrest under a court warrant, civil arrest for debt/tax recovery, or deportation of an alien.

#### Preventive Detention Safeguards: Clauses 22(4)–(7)

Remarkably, **India is among the very few constitutional democracies in the world whose written Constitution explicitly sanctions peacetime preventive detention** (even the US and UK restrict detention without trial strictly to wartime emergencies). Justifying this reluctant inclusion in the Constituent Assembly, Ambedkar cited Partition violence, communal unrest, and subversion, while embedding four procedural brakes in `Articles 22(4)` to `(7)`:

- **Three-Month Ceiling & Advisory Board (`Article 22(4)`):** No preventive detention law can authorise detention beyond **three months** unless an **Advisory Board** consisting of persons who are, have been, or are qualified to be appointed as **High Court judges** reports before the expiry of three months that there is sufficient cause for extended detention. *(Classic Prelims Trap: The **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`** sought to reduce this period from **three months to two months** and require the Board to follow the Chief Justice of the High Court's recommendation—**HOWEVER, Section 3 of the 44th Amendment has NEVER been notified by the Central Government**, and in ***A.K. Roy v. Union of India* (1982)**, a Constitution Bench held that the Court cannot issue a mandamus compelling the executive to bring it into force! Therefore, the operative constitutional limit remains **three months**.)*
- **Communication of Grounds (`Article 22(5)` & `22(6)`):** The detaining authority must communicate the grounds of detention to the detainee as soon as may be—**except** facts which the authority considers against the **public interest** to disclose (`Article 22(6)`).
- **Earliest Opportunity for Representation (`Article 22(5)`):** The detainee must be afforded the earliest opportunity to make an effective representation against the detention order to the appropriate government and the Advisory Board.
- **Parliamentary Override Powers (`Article 22(7)`):** Only **Parliament** (NOT a State Legislature) may by law prescribe: (a) the circumstances and classes of cases in which a person may be detained beyond three months *without* obtaining an Advisory Board's opinion; (b) the maximum period of detention under any preventive detention law; and (c) the procedure to be followed by an Advisory Board.

#### Legislative Competence & Major Preventive Detention / Security Statutes

The Seventh Schedule splits preventive detention legislative power cleanly between Centre and States:
- **Union List (Entry 9 — Exclusive Parliamentary Competence):** Preventive detention for reasons connected with **defence, foreign affairs, or the security of India**.
- **Concurrent List (Entry 3 — Both Parliament & State Legislatures):** Preventive detention for reasons connected with the **security of a State, maintenance of public order, or maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community**.

Major laws enacted over the decades include the **Preventive Detention Act, 1950** (lapsed 1969), **Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA), 1971** (notoriously misused during the 1975–77 Emergency; repealed 1978), **Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act (COFEPOSA), 1974**, **National Security Act (NSA), 1980** (upheld with procedural safeguards in *A.K. Roy*, 1982), **Prevention of Blackmarketing and Maintenance of Supplies of Essential Commodities Act, 1980**, **PITNDPS Act, 1988**, and State laws such as the **Jammu & Kashmir Public Safety Act (PSA), 1978** and various State "Goonda Acts" (e.g., Tamil Nadu Act 14 of 1982). Distinct from pure preventive detention, stringent anti-terror **punitive** statutes with extended pre-charge detention and strict bail bars—such as **TADA (1985, lapsed 1995)**, **POTA (2002, repealed 2004)**, and the **Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act (UAPA), 1967** (amended 2004, 2008, 2012, and 2019 to allow designating individuals as terrorists)—frequently raise acute `Articles 21` and `22` liberty concerns (*Union of India v. K.A. Najeeb*, 2021—holding that constitutional courts can still grant bail under UAPA when prolonged incarceration without trial violates `Article 21`!).

```compare
# Punitive Detention vs Preventive Detention (Article 22)
Punitive Detention (Art. 22(1)–(2)) | Preventive Detention (Art. 22(4)–(7))
Core Purpose :: Punish a person for an offence already committed, after formal trial and conviction in court | Prevent a person from committing a future prejudicial act, based on executive suspicion without trial
Who Is Protected :: Citizens and friendly foreigners (excludes enemy aliens and preventive detainees under Art. 22(3)) | Both citizens and aliens held under preventive detention laws
Judicial Oversight :: Production before a magistrate within 24 hours (excluding journey time) mandatory | No 24-hour magistrate rule; Advisory Board (HC-qualified judges) review required only beyond 3 months
Legal Representation :: Constitutional right to consult and be defended by a lawyer of choice (Art. 22(1)) | No right to a lawyer before the Advisory Board (unless the State itself is represented by counsel — A.K. Roy, 1982)
Disclosure of Grounds :: Mandatory prompt communication of written grounds of arrest (Prabir Purkayastha, 2024) | Grounds communicated, but authority may withhold facts against 'public interest' (Art. 22(6))
Key Statutes :: BNS 2023, BNSS 2023, PMLA 2002, UAPA 1967 (punitive anti-terror with strict bail) | NSA 1980, COFEPOSA 1974, J&K PSA 1978, State Goonda Acts (PDA 1950 & MISA 1971 defunct)
```

## 6. Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)

In a newly independent country scarred by centuries of zamindari serfdom, debt bondage, caste-based unpaid labour (*begar*), trafficking of women and children, and hazardous child labour in sweatshops and mines, civil freedoms alone meant little to the poorest Indians. **`Articles 23` and `24`** therefore directly attack social and economic coercion. Crucially, both Articles protect **citizens and non-citizens alike**, and both operate **horizontally against private individuals** as well as against the State—making them among the sharpest anti-feudal and pro-labour instruments in the entire Constitution.

### Article 23: Prohibition of Traffic in Human Beings, Begar & Forced Labour

`Article 23(1)` declares: *"Traffic in human beings and begar and other similar forms of forced labour are prohibited and any contravention of this provision shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law."* Three distinct forms of exploitation are constitutionally outlawed:

- **Traffic in Human Beings:** Selling and buying of men, women, and children like chattels or commodities; immoral traffic in women and children, including forced prostitution; the exploitative *devadasi* system; and slavery in any form.
- ***Begar*:** A uniquely Indian feudal practice under which landlords, moneylenders, or local officials compelled impoverished peasants and lower-caste labourers to render involuntary personal service **without any remuneration**.
- **Other Similar Forms of Forced Labour:** Covers **bonded labour** (*bandhua mazdoori*), where an impoverished worker or entire family is forced to work for generations to pay off an unconscionable or hereditary debt, as well as any form of coercion—physical, legal, or economic.

#### The *PUDR* (1982) Breakthrough: Paying Below Minimum Wage Is Forced Labour

In a landmark expansion of socio-economic rights, ***People's Union for Democratic Rights (PUDR) v. Union of India* (1982 — the Asiad Workers Case)** transformed `Article 23` into a potent shield for unorganised workers. When private contractors building stadia and flyovers for the 1982 Delhi Asian Games paid workers less than the statutory minimum wage (deducting *jamadar* commissions), the Supreme Court rejected the argument that workers had "voluntarily" agreed to low pay. Justice P.N. Bhagwati ruled that "force" in `Article 23` is **not confined to physical violence or legal compulsion**: it includes **economic compulsion** driven by hunger and destitution that leaves a poor person no genuine alternative. Consequently, **providing labour or service for remuneration less than the statutory minimum wage amounts to "forced labour" prohibited by `Article 23`**.

The Court swiftly reinforced this doctrine across three companion rulings:
- ***Sanjit Roy v. State of Rajasthan* (1983):** Struck down a state exemption paying drought-relief workers below the minimum wage—holding that the State cannot exploit human helplessness even under the cloak of famine relief.
- ***Deena v. Union of India* (1983) & *State of Gujarat v. Hon'ble High Court of Gujarat* (1998):** Held that extracting rigorous labour from convicted prisoners without paying **equitable wages** violates `Article 23` (though reasonable deductions for victim compensation funds are permissible).
- ***Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India* (1984):** Treated a letter about bonded stone-quarry workers in Faridabad as an `Article 32` PIL, holding that the State has a constitutional duty not merely to identify and release bonded labourers, but to **rehabilitate** them meaningfully so they do not relapse into bondage.

#### Statutory Architecture Under Article 23 & Article 35

Because `Article 23(1)` is not self-executing for criminal sanctions, Parliament (holding exclusive punitive authority under `Article 35`) enacted a strong statutory ecosystem:
- **Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956 (ITPA):** Originally the *Suppression of Immoral Traffic in Women and Girls Act (SITA), 1956* (amended in 1978 and 1986), criminalising brothel-keeping, procuring, trafficking, and commercial sexual exploitation.
- **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976:** Extinguished all bonded debts, freed bonded labourers across India, established District Vigilance Committees, and paired release with a **Central Sector Scheme for Rehabilitation of Bonded Labourers**.
- **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023 (Sections 143–146):** Updates criminal sanctions against human trafficking (formerly IPC Section 370), exploitation of trafficked persons, and unlawful compulsory labour.
- **Wage & Labour Protections:** Historically enforced via the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, Contract Labour Act, 1970, and Equal Remuneration Act, 1976—now consolidated into the **Four Labour Codes**.

#### Clause 23(2): Compulsory Service for Public Purposes Exception

`Article 23(2)` carves out one narrow, carefully bounded exception: the State is permitted to impose **compulsory service for public purposes**—for example, military conscription during war or mandatory national social service—and is **not bound to pay remuneration** for such public duty. However, in imposing such compulsory service, the State **shall NOT make any discrimination on grounds only of religion, race, caste, or class, or any of them**.

### Article 24: Prohibition of Employment of Children

`Article 24` commands: *"No child below the age of **fourteen years** shall be employed to work in any factory or mine or engaged in any other hazardous employment."* Notice an essential constitutional subtlety: the bare text of `Article 24` prohibits employing children below 14 only in **factories, mines, or hazardous activities** (such as construction work, fireworks, glassmaking, or railways); it did *not* constitutionally bar harmless or innocent work.

#### Statutory Expansion: Child and Adolescent Labour Amendment Act, 2016

Recognising that any full-time child work destroys a child's right to elementary education under `Article 21A`, Parliament vastly expanded `Article 24`'s floor. Initially enacted as the **Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986**, the law was overhauled by the **Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016** and renamed the **Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986**. Its core legal framework now establishes a **two-tier age shield**:

1. **Children (Below 14 Years) — Complete Prohibition:** Completely bans employment of children under 14 in **ALL occupations and processes** (hazardous and non-hazardous alike), with strictly **two narrow exceptions** (provided schooling under `Article 21A` is never disrupted):
   - Helping in a **family or family enterprise** (other than hazardous occupations) *only after school hours or during vacations*; and
   - Working as an **artist in an audio-visual entertainment industry** (advertisements, films, TV serials) or sports activities (excluding circuses), subject to strict safety conditions.
2. **Adolescents (14 to 18 Years) — Hazardous Work Banned:** Introduces a new statutory category of **"adolescent"** (ages 14–18) and strictly prohibits their employment in **hazardous occupations and processes** (mines, inflammable substances/explosives, and hazardous factory processes under the Factories Act).
3. **Strict Penalties & Rehabilitation:** Makes employing children/adolescents in contravention of the Act a **cognisable offence**, enhancing penalties to 6 months–2 years imprisonment and/or ₹20,000–₹50,000 fine (stricter for repeat offences), while exempting first-time offending parents from immediate punishment. This reform enabled India to ratify **ILO Convention No. 138** (Minimum Age) and **ILO Convention No. 182** (Worst Forms of Child Labour) in **June 2017**.

#### Judicial Activism & Institutional Ecosystem (*M.C. Mehta*, 1996 & NCPCR)

In the landmark judgment ***M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1996)** (concerning children labouring in Sivakasi's hazardous match and fireworks factories), the Supreme Court directed the establishment of a **Child Labour Rehabilitation-cum-Welfare Fund**, ordering offending employers to deposit **₹20,000 per child** into the fund and directing the State either to provide employment to one adult member of the child's family or deposit an additional ₹5,000 for the child's education and nutrition. Together, `Articles 21A`, `23`, `24`, and Directive Principles `Articles 39(e)` and `39(f)` (protecting tender-age children from economic abuse) operate alongside the **Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005**—which established the statutory **National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR)** and State Commissions (SCPCRs) as well as Children's Courts—the **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015**, and the **POCSO Act, 2012**.

> [!prelims]
> **Articles 23–24 High-Yield Prelims Traps:**
> 1. **Prohibited Grounds in `Article 23(2)` Compulsory Service:** Only **4 grounds** — **religion, race, caste, or class**. Notice carefully: **"sex" and "place of birth" are NOT listed** (so the State could constitutionally exempt women from military conscription without violating `Art. 23(2)`!).
> 2. **Constitution vs Statute on Child Labour:** `Article 24` itself bars <14 employment ONLY in factories, mines, and hazardous work; it is the **2016 Amendment Act** that expanded the ban to **ALL occupations for <14** (barring after-school family enterprises and audio-visual/sports) and banned **14–18 adolescents from hazardous work**.
> 3. **Horizontal & Universal Scope:** Both `Articles 23` and `24` apply to **citizens AND foreigners**, and bind **both the State AND private employers/individuals**.

> [!news]
> **Four Labour Codes Enforced (21 November 2025) & the *PUDR* Principle:**
> On **21 November 2025**, the Government of India brought into force all **four consolidated Labour Codes**—the **Code on Wages (2019)**, **Industrial Relations Code (2020)**, **Code on Social Security (2020)**, and **Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions (OSH) Code (2020)**—amalgamating 29 central labour statutes. Directly relevant to `Article 23` and the *PUDR* (1982) minimum-wage ruling, the **Code on Wages** extends statutory minimum-wage coverage universally across *all* organised and unorganised sectors (replacing the earlier scheduled-employment limitation of the 1948 Act) and introduces a legally binding **National Floor Wage** below which no State government may fix minimum wages. Meanwhile, persistent concerns over gig-worker protections, migrant trafficking, and informal family-enterprise loopholes under the 2016 Child Labour Amendment remain key Mains themes.

## 7. Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28) & Cultural-Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)

Few nations match India's astonishing religious, linguistic, and cultural plurality. Writing in the immediate shadow of Partition, the framers faced a delicate twin challenge: to assure every believer and every minority that their faith, language, script, and institutions would remain permanently safe in a majoritarian democracy, while empowering a modern democratic State to dismantle deeply entrenched social evils such as untouchability, caste exclusion from temples, and gender subordination. `Articles 25` to `28` construct India's distinctive secular framework, while `Articles 29` and `30` anchor cultural preservation and minority educational autonomy.

### Indian Secularism & Principled Distance

Unlike the American First Amendment's strict **"wall of separation"** (where State and church mutually exclude one another) or French ***laïcité*** (which aggressively banishes visible religious symbols from public spaces), India adopted a **positive, accommodative model of secularism**—famously conceptualised by political theorist Rajeev Bhargava as **"principled distance."** The Indian State has no official religion, treats all faiths with equal respect (*Sarva Dharma Sambhava*), and protects individual and collective worship. Yet it retains the principled authority to engage with or reform religion whenever constitutional values of equality, dignity, and social justice demand intervention—for example, abolishing untouchability, opening Hindu temples to all castes, or funding religious minority educational institutions without discrimination. In ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994, 9-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court declared **secularism an indestructible part of the Basic Structure**.

### Article 25: Individual Freedom of Conscience & Religion

Available to **all persons** (citizens and non-citizens alike), `Article 25(1)` guarantees every individual four distinct yet interconnected freedoms:

- **Freedom of Conscience:** The inner, inviolable freedom to mould one's personal relationship with God, spiritual truth, or atheism/agnosticism in whatever manner one chooses.
- **Right to Profess:** The open, uninhibited declaration of one's religious beliefs and faith.
- **Right to Practise:** The performance of religious worship, rituals, ceremonies, and exhibition of beliefs and ideas.
- **Right to Propagate:** The transmission and dissemination of one's religious tenets to others, or exposition of its doctrines. Crucially, in ***Rev. Stainislaus v. State of Madhya Pradesh* (1977, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court held that the right to "propagate" **does NOT include a Fundamental Right to convert** another person to one's own religion—because forcible, fraudulent, or induced conversion infringes the *other* person's equal freedom of conscience. On this basis, the Court upheld the Madhya Pradesh and Orissa Freedom of Religion Acts (and later state anti-conversion statutes continue to invoke this precedent, though several stringent post-2018 state amendments face constitutional challenges over vagueness and interfaith marriage privacy under `Article 21`).

#### Constitutional Limits, State Reform Powers & Explanations I–II

Individual religious freedom under `Article 25(1)` is **NOT absolute**. It is expressly subordinated to **four constitutional checks**: (1) **public order**, (2) **morality**, (3) **health**, and (4) **the other provisions of Part III** (meaning `Article 25` must yield when it collides with `Articles 14`, `15`, `17`, or `21`—a vital contrast with `Article 26`, which omits the phrase "other provisions of this Part").

Further, **`Article 25(2)`** expressly saves the State's power to enact laws:
- **`25(2)(a)` — Regulating Secular Activities:** Regulating or restricting any **economic, financial, political, or other secular activity** associated with religious practice (such as auditing temple trusts, regulating Tirupati or Vaishno Devi shrine boards, or managing waqf properties).
- **`25(2)(b)` — Social Welfare & Temple Entry:** Providing for **social welfare and reform**, OR **throwing open Hindu religious institutions of a public character** to all classes and sections of Hindus.

Two vital **Explanations** accompany `Article 25` (perennial Prelims favourites):
- **Explanation I:** The **wearing and carrying of *kirpans*** is deemed to be included in the profession of the Sikh religion.
- **Explanation II:** For the purposes of sub-clause `25(2)(b)` (social reform and public temple entry), the reference to **"Hindus"** shall be construed as including persons professing the **Sikh, Jain, or Buddhist** religion, and references to Hindu religious institutions are construed accordingly.

#### The Essential Religious Practices (ERP) Test

Because `Article 25` protects religious practices while `Article 25(2)(a)` lets the State regulate secular practices, courts needed a doctrinal boundary. In ***Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt* (1954, 7-Judge Bench)**, Justice B.K. Mukherjea held that `Article 25` protects not merely religious beliefs but also rituals and ceremonies **integral** to a religion—determined primarily with reference to the doctrines of that religion itself. Over subsequent decades, however, the Supreme Court narrowed this into the judge-driven **Essential Religious Practices (ERP) Test**, protecting *only* practices deemed strictly essential and core to a faith:

- ***Mohd. Hanif Quareshi v. State of Bihar* (1958):** Held that slaughtering cows on Bakr-Id is optional rather than an essential Islamic practice, upholding state cow-slaughter bans.
- ***Durgah Committee, Ajmer v. Syed Hussain Ali* (1961):** Justice Gajendragadkar warned courts to separate authentic essential practices from "superstitious beliefs" and extraneous accretions.
- ***Acharya Jagadishwarananda Avadhuta* (Ananda Margis Cases, 1984 & 2004):** Held that performing the *tandava* dance with skulls and daggers in public processions was not an essential tenet of the Ananda Marga faith.
- ***Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017):** Held that instant triple talaq (*talaq-e-biddat*) was neither sanctioned by the Quran nor an essential religious practice of Islam.
- ***Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala* (Sabarimala Case, 28 September 2018, 5-Judge Bench, 4–1):** Held that excluding women aged 10 to 50 from the Sabarimala Ayyappa temple violated `Articles 14`, `15`, `17`, and women's individual `Article 25(1)` right, ruling that Ayyappa devotees did not form a separate religious denomination under `Article 26` and exclusion was not an essential Hindu practice. **Justice Indu Malhotra** delivered a powerful lone dissent arguing that constitutional courts should not impose external notions of rationality on matters of faith unless a practice is pernicious (like *sati*). In **November 2019 (*Kantaru Rajeevaru*, 3–2)**, a five-judge bench kept the review petitions pending and referred **seven broader constitutional questions**—including the scope of the ERP test, the interplay of `Articles 25` and `26`, the meaning of "morality," and women's access to mosques, Parsi fire temples, and Dawoodi Bohra practices—to a **nine-judge Constitution Bench** (still pending adjudication).
- ***Aishat Shifa v. State of Karnataka* (13 October 2022 — Hijab Case):** A two-judge bench delivered a **1–1 split verdict** on Karnataka's uniform ban prohibiting the *hijab* in government pre-university colleges (Justice Hemant Gupta upholding the uniform rule and secular discipline; Justice Sudhanshu Dhulia striking it down as a matter of personal choice under `Articles 19(1)(a)` and `21` without needing the ERP test), placing the matter before a larger bench.

> [!debate]
> **Should Secular Judges Decide "Essential Religious Practices"?**
> - **Critique of the ERP Test:** Scholars and dissenters (notably Justice Indu Malhotra in *Sabarimala*) argue that ERP forces secular judges to act as clergy and theologians, freezes living religions into rigid textual orthodoxy, and ignores internal diversity within faiths.
> - **Proposed Alternative — The Anti-Exclusion Test:** Articulated by Justice D.Y. Chandrachud in *Sabarimala* (2018), courts should stop playing theologian and instead ask a purely constitutional question: *Does the impugned religious practice systematically exclude individuals from dignity, civic equality, or public access?* If yes, constitutional morality overrides the practice; if no, the State stays out.

### Article 26: Collective Freedom to Manage Religious Affairs

While `Article 25` protects the **individual**, **`Article 26`** protects **collective religious groups**. Subject to **public order, morality, and health** (note: NOT expressly subject to other provisions of Part III, though courts harmonise it with constitutional values), every **religious denomination** or any section thereof has four rights:
1. **`26(a)`:** To establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes.
2. **`26(b)`:** To manage its own affairs in **matters of religion** (protected from State interference unless breaching public order, morality, or health).
3. **`26(c)`:** To own and acquire movable and immovable property.
4. **`26(d)`:** To **administer such property in accordance with law** (note the sharp contrast between `26(b)` and `26(d)`: while *matters of religion* are insulated, *administration of secular property* can be regulated by State legislation, though the right to administer cannot be totally extinguished—*Shirur Mutt*, 1954).

What qualifies as a **"religious denomination"**? In ***S.P. Mittal v. Union of India* (1983)**, the Supreme Court laid down a mandatory **three-part cumulative test**: (1) a collection of individuals having a **system of beliefs (common faith)** regarded as conducive to their spiritual well-being; (2) a **common organisation**; and (3) a **distinctive name**. Applying this test, the Court recognised the Ramakrishna Mission and Ananda Marga as religious denominations within Hinduism, while holding that **Aurobindo Society / Auroville** did NOT qualify as a religious denomination because Sri Aurobindo's teachings constituted a universal philosophy rather than a separate religion.

### Articles 27 & 28: Secular Taxation & Religious Instruction

#### Article 27: Freedom from Taxation for Promotion of a Religion

`Article 27` states: *"No person shall be compelled to pay any **taxes**, the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated in payment of expenses for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination."* Two vital constitutional distinctions govern `Article 27`:
- **No Specific Favouritism, Equal Support Allowed:** The State cannot spend public tax revenues to favour or promote **one particular religion** over others; however, non-discriminatory expenditure benefiting **all religions equally** (such as public safety at Kumbh Mela, Haj facilitation, or restoring heritage monuments across faiths—*Prafull Goradia*, 2011) does not violate `Article 27`.
- **Tax vs Fee Distinction (*Shirur Mutt*, 1954; *Ratilal Panachand Gandhi*, 1954):** `Article 27` prohibits only a **tax** (compulsory exaction for general revenue without *quid pro quo*), **NOT a fee** (levied to cover the cost of secular administration, regulation of religious endowments, or special facilities and sanitation for pilgrims).

#### Article 28: Freedom from Attending Religious Instruction

`Article 28` creates an exact **four-tier classification** governing religious instruction in educational institutions across India:

| Type of Educational Institution | Constitutional Rule Under Article 28 |
|---|---|
| **1. Wholly maintained out of State funds** (`28(1)`) | Religious instruction is **completely prohibited** *(value-based academic study of comparative religions is allowed — Aruna Roy v. Union of India, 2002)*. |
| **2. Administered by the State, but established under an endowment or trust requiring religious instruction** (`28(2)`) | Religious instruction is **permitted** (exception to `28(1)`). |
| **3. Recognised by the State** (`28(3)`) | Religious instruction is **permitted on a voluntary basis** — no student can be compelled to attend without their consent (or guardian's consent if a minor). |
| **4. Receiving aid out of State funds** (`28(3)`) | Religious instruction is **permitted on a voluntary basis** — attendance cannot be made compulsory without student/guardian consent. |

### Article 29: Protection of Interests of Minorities (and Any Section of Citizens)

Shifting from freedom of worship to cultural preservation, **`Articles 29` and `30`** protect **citizens ONLY** (foreigners are excluded). `Article 29` contains two carefully drafted clauses:

- **`Article 29(1)` — Right to Conserve Distinct Culture:** *"Any section of the citizens residing in the territory of India or any part thereof having a distinct **language, script or culture** of its own shall have the right to conserve the same."* **Major Prelims Trap:** Although the marginal heading of `Article 29` reads *"Protection of interests of minorities,"* the text deliberately uses the wider phrase **"any section of the citizens."** In ***Ahmedabad St. Xavier's College Society v. State of Gujarat* (1974, 9-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court held that `Article 29(1)` is **NOT restricted to minorities**—it protects BOTH religious/linguistic minorities AND sections of the **majority** possessing a distinct language, script, or culture! Further, in ***Jagdev Singh Sidhanti v. Pratap Singh Daulta* (1965)**, the Court held that the right to conserve a language includes the right to **agitate politically** for its protection (so electoral appeals seeking protection of a language do not constitute a "corrupt practice" under Section 123(3) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951).
- **`Article 29(2)` — Non-Discrimination in State / Aided Institutions:** *"No citizen shall be denied admission into any educational institution maintained by the State or receiving aid out of State funds on grounds **only of religion, race, caste, language or any of them**."* Note two subtle comparisons with `Article 15(1)`: first, `29(2)` drops **sex and place of birth** (so women-only colleges or state-domicile admissions do not breach `29(2)`) while adding **language**; second, unlike `29(1)` (which protects groups), `29(2)` guarantees an **individual citizen's** right to admission irrespective of community!

### Article 30: Right of Minorities to Establish & Administer Educational Institutions

Unlike `Article 29(1)`, **`Article 30` is reserved EXCLUSIVELY for minorities**—specifically **religious minorities** and **linguistic minorities** (citizens only). Notably, the Constitution **never defines the term "minority."** Three clauses form the backbone of `Article 30`:

- **`Article 30(1)`:** All minorities, whether based on religion or language, have the right to **establish and administer educational institutions of their choice** (which includes the right to impart education to their children in their own language).
- **`Article 30(1A)` (Inserted by `44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`):** When Parliament removed the general Right to Property (`Article 31`), it simultaneously inserted `30(1A)` to ensure that whenever the State compulsorily acquires the property of a minority educational institution, the compensation fixed by law must **not restrict or abrogate** the right guaranteed under `30(1)`.
- **`Article 30(2)`:** The State shall not, in granting aid to educational institutions, **discriminate** against any educational institution on the ground that it is under the management of a minority, whether based on religion or language.

#### Defining Minority Status, Autonomy vs Regulation & the AMU Ruling (Nov 2024)

The Supreme Court has developed an intricate jurisprudence balancing minority autonomy against academic excellence:

1. **State as the Unit for Minority Status (*T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka*, 2002, 11-Judge Bench):** Settled that because States were reorganised linguistically and populations vary sharply across India, minority status—for **both religious and linguistic minorities**—must be determined **State by State** (not nationwide). Thus, Sikhs in Punjab or Muslims in Jammu & Kashmir or Christians in Nagaland are numerical majorities at the State level, whereas Hindus are numerical minorities in several States/UTs.
2. **Right to Administer ≠ Right to Maladminister (*St. Xavier's*, 1974; *Malankara Syrian Catholic College*, 2007):** Minority institutions fall into three categories: (i) institutions seeking both recognition and aid from the State; (ii) institutions seeking recognition but no aid; and (iii) institutions seeking neither. While the first two are subject to reasonable **regulatory measures** ensuring academic excellence, teacher qualifications, syllabus standards, sanitation, and service fairness (*Malankara*, 2007 codified five core administrative rights: selecting governing body, appointing teachers/principal, admitting students, and setting reasonable fees), the State cannot hijack core management.
3. **Exemption from State Quotas & RTE (*P.A. Inamdar*, 2005; *Pramati*, 2014):** In *P.A. Inamdar* (2005, 7-judge bench), the Court barred the State from imposing seat-sharing quotas on unaided institutions. Subsequently, Parliament's `93rd Amendment` (`Article 15(5)`) and `103rd Amendment` (`Article 15(6)`) **expressly exempted minority educational institutions**, and *Pramati Educational Trust* (2014) similarly exempted both aided and unaided minority schools from the **RTE Act, 2009**.
4. **Statutory & Institutional Minority Ecosystem:** Under Section 2(c) of the **National Commission for Minorities (NCM) Act, 1992**, the Central Government has notified **six religious communities** as national minorities: **Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, and Parsis** (notified in **1993**), plus **Jains** (added in **January 2014**). The **National Commission for Minority Educational Institutions (NCMEI)**, established under the NCMEI Act, 2004, adjudicates minority status certification for institutions, while the constitutional **Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (`Article 350B`, inserted by `7th Amendment, 1956`)** monitors linguistic safeguards nationwide.

## 8. Constitutional Remedies, Writs, Armed Forces & Savings (Articles 32–35 & 31A–31C)

A charter of rights without an accessible, speedy, and unassailable enforcement mechanism is merely a pious aspiration on parchment—remembering the classic legal maxim ***ubi jus, ibi remedium*** (where there is a right, there is a remedy). The closing cluster of Part III achieves four vital tasks: `Article 32` elevates judicial enforcement itself into a Fundamental Right backed by five English prerogative writs; `Articles 33` to `35` calibrate Part III for national defence, martial law, and uniform Parliamentary legislation; and `Articles 31A`, `31B`, and `31C` construct constitutional safe harbours reconciling socio-economic redistribution with individual rights.

### Article 32: Right to Constitutional Remedies & Public Interest Litigation

`Article 32` empowers every person—citizen or non-citizen—whose Fundamental Right has been infringed to move the **Supreme Court directly**, bypassing every subordinate tribunal and High Court. Because the right to seek a constitutional remedy is **itself a Fundamental Right**, Parliament cannot strip it away by ordinary legislation. During the Constituent Assembly Debates, Dr B.R. Ambedkar delivered his most famous tribute to any provision of the Constitution:

> "If I was asked to name any particular article in this Constitution as the most important—an article without which this Constitution would be a nullity—I could not refer to any other article except this one. It is the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it." — Dr B.R. Ambedkar on Draft Article 25 (now `Article 32`), Constituent Assembly, 9 December 1948

In ***Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras* (1950)**, the Supreme Court declared itself the **"protector and guarantor"** of Fundamental Rights and held that it cannot refuse to entertain an `Article 32` petition merely because an alternative remedy exists before a High Court. Later, in ***L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997, 7-Judge Bench)**, the Court held that the power of judicial review vested in the Supreme Court (`Article 32`) and High Courts (`Article 226`) is an inviolable part of the **Basic Structure**—even a constitutional amendment creating specialised tribunals cannot oust it.

#### The Four Clauses & Jurisdictional Boundaries of Article 32

1. **`Article 32(1)`:** Guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court by **appropriate proceedings** for the enforcement of the rights conferred by Part III.
2. **`Article 32(2)`:** Empowers the Supreme Court to issue **directions, orders, or writs**—including writs in the nature of *habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto,* and *certiorari*, whichever may be appropriate—for enforcing Part III rights. (The phrase *"in the nature of"* frees the Court from archaic English technicalities, enabling innovative remedies like constitutional compensation—*Rudul Sah*, 1983; *Nilabati Behera*, 1993.)
3. **`Article 32(3)`:** Empowers **Parliament** (not State Legislatures—`Article 35`) to confer by law the power to issue these writs on **any other court** within its local jurisdiction, without prejudice to the Supreme Court's powers. *(Prelims Fact: Parliament has **never exercised** `Article 32(3)` so far! High Courts derive their independent writ jurisdiction directly from `Article 226`, NOT from `Article 32(3)`!)*
4. **`Article 32(4)`:** Mandates that the right guaranteed by `Article 32` **shall NOT be suspended** except as otherwise provided by the Constitution—which refers strictly to **`Article 359`**, permitting the President to suspend court enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights (NEVER `Articles 20` and `21`) during a National Emergency.

Crucially, the Supreme Court's jurisdiction under `Article 32` is **original** (direct first-instance access) but **NOT exclusive**—it is **concurrent** with the High Courts' jurisdiction under `Article 226`. Furthermore, `Article 32` can be invoked **ONLY for the violation of a Fundamental Right in Part III**—never to enforce an ordinary statutory right, a contractual dispute, or even a constitutional right outside Part III (such as `Article 300A` property rights or `Article 265` illegal taxation).

#### Public Interest Litigation (PIL) & Epistolary Jurisdiction

For three decades after 1950, traditional Anglo-Saxon rules of ***locus standi*** dictated that only the person whose personal legal right was violated could approach a court—locking out millions of impoverished, illiterate, bonded, or imprisoned Indians. In the late 1970s and early 1980s, pioneering judges **Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer** (*Mumbai Kamgar Sabha*, 1976) and **Justice P.N. Bhagwati** (*Hussainara Khatoon*, 1979; ***S.P. Gupta v. Union of India*, 1981**—the Judges' Transfer Case) revolutionised `Article 32` by introducing **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)** (also called Social Action Litigation).

Under PIL, any bona fide, public-spirited citizen or organisation can petition the Supreme Court or a High Court on behalf of socially or economically disadvantaged groups unable to reach court themselves. Through **"epistolary jurisdiction,"** the Supreme Court even converted ordinary **letters and postcards** into writ petitions (*Sunil Batra*, 1978; *Bandhua Mukti Morcha*, 1984). PIL democratised access to justice across bonded labour, undertrial detention, prison reform, environmental conservation, and gender dignity (*Vishaka*). At the same time, Mains answers must acknowledge legitimate concerns raised by the Court itself (*BALCO Employees' Union*, 2001; *State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal*, 2010): the proliferation of frivolous "publicity/political/paisa interest litigation," mounting docket pendency, and risks of **judicial overreach** blurring the separation of powers when courts micro-manage executive policy.

### The Five Prerogative Writs in Full Detail

Borrowed from English common law—where they were issued as "prerogative writs" in the King's name as the fountain of justice—writs are formal judicial commands enforcing constitutional and legal duties. Before 1950, only the three Chartered High Courts of **Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras** possessed writ power; `Articles 32` and `226` democratised all five writs nationwide.

#### 1. Habeas Corpus ("To Have the Body Of")

The supreme bulwark of individual liberty against arbitrary detention, *habeas corpus* is a court order commanding the detaining person or authority to **produce the detained body before the court** so judges can verify the legality and cause of detention. If no lawful justification exists, the detainee is set free forthwith.
- **Against Whom Issued:** Uniquely among writs, it lies against **BOTH public authorities AND private individuals** (e.g., illegal detention by police, or unlawful confinement of an adult spouse/child by private relatives—*Shafin Jahan*, 2018).
- **Four Exceptions (When NOT Issued):** (i) Detention is lawful; (ii) proceeding is for contempt of a legislature or a court; (iii) detention is ordered by a **competent court**; or (iv) detention is outside the territorial jurisdiction of the court.

#### 2. Mandamus ("We Command")

A positive judicial command issued to a **public official** directing them to perform an official **public duty** that they have failed or refused to perform. It also lies against any public body, statutory corporation, inferior court, tribunal, or government for the same purpose.
- **Six Exceptions (When NOT Issued):** (i) Against a **private individual or purely private body**; (ii) to enforce a **departmental instruction** lacking statutory force; (iii) when the duty is **discretionary** rather than mandatory; (iv) to enforce a purely **contractual obligation**; (v) against the **President of India or State Governors** (`Article 361`); and (vi) against the **Chief Justice of a High Court acting in a judicial capacity**.

#### 3. Prohibition ("To Forbid")

Issued by a higher court (Supreme Court or High Court) to a **lower court or tribunal** to prevent it from exceeding its jurisdiction or usurping a jurisdiction it does not possess. While *mandamus* directs **activity**, *prohibition* directs **inactivity**; it is strictly **preventive** (issued while proceedings are still pending, before a final order is passed).
- **Against Whom Issued:** Available **ONLY against judicial and quasi-judicial authorities**. It is **NOT** available against administrative authorities, legislative bodies, or private individuals/bodies.

#### 4. Certiorari ("To Be Certified" or "To Be Informed")

Issued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal either to **transfer a pending case** to itself or to **quash an order** already passed by the lower authority. It lies on grounds of **excess of jurisdiction, lack of jurisdiction, error of law apparent on the face of the record**, or violation of principles of natural justice. Thus, unlike *prohibition* (purely preventive), *certiorari* is **BOTH preventive and curative**.
- **Against Whom Issued (1991 Expansion!):** Historically, *certiorari* lay only against judicial and quasi-judicial authorities. Building on *A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India* (1969), the Supreme Court held in **1991** that *certiorari* can be issued even against **administrative authorities** exercising powers that affect the rights of individuals. Like *prohibition*, however, it is **NOT** available against legislative bodies or private individuals/bodies.

#### 5. Quo Warranto ("By What Authority or Warrant")

Issued by a court to enquire into the legality of a person's claim to hold a **public office**, thereby preventing illegal usurpation of public office.
- **Key Conditions & Unique Standing Rule:** Lies ONLY in respect of a **substantive public office of a permanent character** created by a statute or the Constitution (e.g., Advocate General, University Vice-Chancellor, High Court Judge, statutory board chair). It CANNOT be issued against a **ministerial office** (serving at pleasure without independent statutory public duties) or a **private office**. Crucially, unlike the other four writs, *quo warranto* can be sought by **ANY interested person**—the petitioner need NOT be personally aggrieved!

| Writ | Literal Meaning | Core Purpose | Lies Against | Key Exceptions / Prelims Traps |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Habeas Corpus** | "To have the body of" | Release person from unlawful detention | **Public authorities AND private individuals** | Not for lawful detention, contempt of court/legislature, order of competent court, or outside jurisdiction |
| **Mandamus** | "We command" | Compel performance of mandatory public duty | Public officials, public bodies, corporations, inferior courts, tribunals, government | Not against private persons, non-statutory instructions, discretionary duties, contracts, President/Governors, or HC CJ acting judicially |
| **Prohibition** | "To forbid" | Prevent lower court/tribunal from exceeding jurisdiction (**preventive only**) | **Judicial & quasi-judicial** authorities ONLY | Not against administrative authorities, legislative bodies, or private individuals |
| **Certiorari** | "To be certified / informed" | Transfer pending case or quash illegal order (**preventive & curative**) | Judicial, quasi-judicial, AND **administrative authorities** affecting rights (since **1991**) | Not against legislative bodies or private individuals/bodies |
| **Quo Warranto** | "By what authority" | Test legality of claim to public office | Holder of a **substantive permanent public office** created by statute/Constitution | Not for ministerial or private offices; **any interested person** (not only aggrieved) may file |

### Supreme Court (Article 32) vs High Court (Article 226)

A perennial UPSC Prelims favourite compares the writ jurisdictions of the Supreme Court and the High Courts—highlighted by the counter-intuitive truth that the **High Court's writ jurisdiction is wider in subject-matter scope**, whereas the **Supreme Court's writ jurisdiction is wider in territorial reach and mandatory as a Fundamental Right**:

```compare
# Supreme Court (Article 32) vs High Court (Article 226)
Supreme Court (Article 32) | High Court (Article 226)
Subject-Matter Scope :: Narrower — ONLY for enforcing Fundamental Rights (Part III) | Wider — Fundamental Rights AND "for any other purpose" (ordinary legal, statutory, or non-Part III constitutional rights like Art. 300A)
Territorial Reach :: Wider — writs run against any person or government anywhere in the territory of India | Narrower — within its own State/UT territory, or outside it ONLY if the cause of action arises (wholly or partly) within its territory

Amenability of Private/Public Bodies :: Limited strictly to 'State' under Art. 12 (and horizontal FRs) | Reaches even non-Art. 12 bodies performing public duties (e.g., BCCI — Zee Telefilms, 2005)
During National Emergency (Art. 359) :: Enforcement of specified FRs (except Arts. 20–21) can be suspended by Presidential order | Suspended similarly for specified FRs, BUT remains fully open for "any other purpose" (ordinary legal rights)
```

### Articles 33, 34 & 35: Armed Forces, Martial Law & Parliamentary Exclusivity

#### Article 33: Armed Forces, Police & Intelligence Agencies

`Article 33` empowers **Parliament alone** (never State Legislatures) to restrict or abrogate the Fundamental Rights of personnel serving in security and disciplined services so as to ensure the proper discharge of duties and maintenance of strict discipline. Following expansion by the **`50th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1984`**, `Article 33` covers **four categories**:
1. Members of the **Armed Forces** (Army, Navy, Air Force).
2. Members of forces charged with the **maintenance of public order** (Police, Paramilitary/CAPFs like CRPF, BSF, CISF, ITBP).
3. Persons employed in any **bureau or organisation established by the State for intelligence or counter-intelligence** (IB, R&AW).
4. Persons employed in, or in connection with, **telecommunication systems** set up for any force, bureau, or organisation above.

Under `Article 33`, Parliament enacted the **Army Act, 1950**, **Navy Act, 1957**, **Air Force Act, 1950**, **Police Forces (Restriction of Rights) Act, 1966**, and **BSF Act, 1968**—curbing freedom of speech (speaking to press), forming trade unions or political associations, and staging protests. Two vital Prelims rules apply: first, in ***R. Viswan v. Union of India* (1983)**, the Supreme Court held that "members of the armed forces" also includes **non-combatant civilian support staff** (barbers, carpenters, mechanics, cooks, chowkidars, bootmakers, tailors) attached to the forces; second, a Parliamentary law under `Article 33` may exclude **courts-martial** (military tribunals) from the writ jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts insofar as Fundamental Rights enforcement is concerned, and no `Article 33` statute can be challenged on Part III grounds.

#### Article 34: Martial Law & Parliamentary Act of Indemnity

`Article 34` provides for restrictions on Fundamental Rights while **martial law** is in force in any area within India. It empowers **Parliament alone** to pass an **Act of Indemnity** protecting any government servant or other person in respect of any act done in connection with the maintenance or restoration of order in a martial-law area, and to **validate** any sentence passed, punishment inflicted, or forfeiture ordered under martial law—shielding such Acts of Indemnity from Part III challenge.

Crucially, the expression **"martial law" is NOT defined anywhere in the Constitution** (nor is there any explicit provision specifying who declares it or how; the concept is borrowed implicitly from English common law). In essence, martial law signifies **"military rule"**—the extraordinary suspension of ordinary civil law and ordinary courts and the temporary governance of a disturbed area by military authorities and military tribunals during war, invasion, insurrection, riot, or total breakdown of civil administration. Importantly, the Supreme Court has affirmed that **declaring martial law does NOT by itself automatically suspend the writ of *habeas corpus***.

```compare
# Martial Law (Article 34) vs National Emergency (Article 352)
Martial Law (Article 34) | National Emergency (Article 352)
Constitutional Impact :: Affects ONLY Fundamental Rights (restricts them in disturbed area) | Affects Fundamental Rights PLUS Centre–State legislative/executive/financial relations AND can extend Lok Sabha tenure
Effect on Government & Courts :: Suspends ordinary civil government and ordinary law courts in the area | Civil government and ordinary courts continue functioning normally
Grounds for Imposition :: Breakdown of law and order due to ANY reason (war, invasion, riot, violent resistance) | Strictly three enumerated grounds: War, External Aggression, or Armed Rebellion
Territorial Scope :: Imposed only in a specific disturbed area of the country | Can cover the whole of India or any part thereof
Constitutional Basis :: Implicit — NOT defined and no specific procedure laid down in the Constitution | Explicit, elaborate constitutional machinery (Articles 352–359, written Cabinet advice, Parliamentary approval)
```

#### Article 35: Exclusive Parliamentary Legislative Competence

To guarantee nationwide uniformity in the scope of Fundamental Rights and their criminal sanctions, **`Article 35`** strips State Legislatures of legislative competence and vests power **exclusively in Parliament** (even when a matter otherwise touches the State List!) across two distinct domains:

1. **Four Enabling Matters (`Article 35(a)(i)`):** Only Parliament can make laws:
   - prescribing residence as a condition for certain employments in a State/UT (`Article 16(3)`);
   - empowering courts other than the Supreme Court and High Courts to issue writs (`Article 32(3)`);
   - restricting or abrogating Fundamental Rights of armed forces, police, and intelligence personnel (`Article 33`);
   - indemnifying government servants or other persons for acts done during martial law (`Article 34`).
2. **Two Punitive Offence Laws (`Article 35(a)(ii)`):** Only Parliament has the power (and constitutional mandate) to prescribe **punishments** for acts declared offences under Part III—namely **untouchability (`Article 17`)** and **traffic in human beings, *begar*, and forced labour (`Article 23`)**.
3. **Continuance of Pre-Constitutional Laws (`Article 35(b)`):** Any pre-1950 law already in force on any of the above matters continues in operation until altered, repealed, or amended by **Parliament** (not by a State Legislature).

### Saving of Certain Laws: Articles 31A, 31B, Ninth Schedule & Article 31C

Inserted through the `1st` and `25th Constitutional Amendments`, **`Articles 31A`, `31B`, and `31C`** form the constitutional exceptions to Fundamental Rights that became the epicentre of independent India's greatest ideological clash—between Nehruvian agrarian/economic redistribution and the Supreme Court's defence of property and equality.

#### Article 31A: Agrarian, Corporate & Mining Reforms (`1st Amendment, 1951`)

Inserted by the **`1st Amendment (1951)`** (and refined by the `4th` [1955] and `17th` [1964] Amendments), `Article 31A` saves **five specific categories of socio-economic laws** from being invalidated on the ground of violating **`Article 14` or `Article 19`** (originally `Article 31` too, omitted by `44th Amendment`):
1. Acquisition of **estates** (zamindari, jagirdari, inamdari) and related agricultural land rights by the State.
2. Taking over the **management of properties** by the State for a limited period in public interest.
3. **Amalgamation of corporations** in public interest or to secure proper management.
4. Extinguishment or modification of rights of **directors or shareholders** of corporations.
5. Extinguishment or modification of **mining leases** or mineral licences.

Two mandatory **provisos** restrain `Article 31A`: (i) a **State law** enjoys `31A` immunity ONLY if it has been reserved for the consideration of the **President and received Presidential assent**; and (ii) where the State acquires agricultural land held by a person under **personal cultivation within the statutory ceiling limit**, the law MUST provide compensation at a rate **not less than the market value** (`17th Amendment, 1964`).

#### Article 31B & the Ninth Schedule (`1st Amendment, 1951` & *I.R. Coelho*, 2007)

Also introduced by the **`1st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951`**, `Article 31B` created the **Ninth Schedule** as an extraordinary protective vault: any Act or Regulation placed in the Ninth Schedule by constitutional amendment was declared immune from challenge on the ground of violating **ANY Fundamental Right** (making `31B` far broader than `31A`, which only shields five categories against `Articles 14` and `19`). Originally housing **13 land-reform laws** in 1951, successive governments packed the Ninth Schedule with **284 statutes**—spanning not just agrarian reforms, but nationalisation laws, election laws, and Tamil Nadu's 69% caste reservation Act (`76th Amendment, 1994`).

To halt this subversion of judicial review, the Supreme Court drew a firm constitutional line in ***Waman Rao v. Union of India* (1981)** and decisively settled the law via a unanimous **nine-judge Constitution Bench** in ***I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu* (11 January 2007)**:
- **Cutoff Date — 24 April 1973:** Laws placed in the Ninth Schedule *before* 24 April 1973 (the date of *Kesavananda Bharati*) remain immune; however, **every law inserted into the Ninth Schedule on or after 24 April 1973 is open to judicial review**!
- **Rights Test / Essence of Rights Test:** There is **no blanket immunity** in the Ninth Schedule. Post-1973 laws violating `Articles 14, 15, 19`, or `21` (the Golden Triangle and Equality Code) will be tested on whether they damage or destroy the **Basic Structure** of the Constitution; if they do, courts will strike them down despite Ninth Schedule placement.

#### Article 31C (`25th Amendment, 1971`) & *Property Owners Association* (Nov 2024)

Inserted by the **`25th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971`** (after *R.C. Cooper*, 1970, struck down Bank Nationalisation), `Article 31C` originally contained two drastic clauses:
- **First Clause:** No law giving effect to the Directive Principles in **`Article 39(b)`** (equitable distribution of ownership and control of *material resources of the community* to subserve the common good) or **`Article 39(c)`** (preventing concentration of wealth and means of production) shall be deemed void for contravening **`Article 14` or `Article 19`**. (State laws require Presidential assent.)
- **Second Clause:** No law containing a legislative declaration that it gives effect to `39(b)`/`39(c)` shall be called in question in any court on the ground that it does not do so.

In ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)**, the Supreme Court **upheld the first clause** but **struck down the second clause** because ousting judicial review destroys the Basic Structure. Undeterred, the Emergency-era **`42nd Amendment Act, 1976`** expanded `Article 31C` to give **ALL Directive Principles** primacy over `Articles 14` and `19`. In ***Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Court struck down that 42nd Amendment expansion, holding that the **harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles** is itself part of the Basic Structure.

For over four decades afterward, two mammoth constitutional questions lingered unresolved in litigation challenging Maharashtra's housing takeover law (Chapter VIII-A of MHADA, 1976): *(1) Did the pre-42nd text of `Article 31C` even survive after `Minerva Mills` invalidated the 42nd Amendment's substitution?* and *(2) Does "material resources of the community" under `Article 39(b)` cover ALL privately owned property?* On **5 November 2024**, a historic **nine-judge Constitution Bench** in ***Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra*** settled both questions definitively:
1. **Survival of `Article 31C` — Unanimous (9–0):** Held that **`Article 31C` (as upheld in *Kesavananda Bharati*, protecting `Articles 39(b)` and `39(c)` laws from `Articles 14` and `19`) CONTINUES TO EXIST** in the Constitution. When an amending Act substituting text is declared unconstitutional, the prior valid constitutional text automatically revives.
2. **Scope of "Material Resources of the Community" (`Article 39(b)`) — 8–1 Majority:** Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud (for 7 judges; Justice B.V. Nagarathna partly concurring in outcome while disagreeing with remarks criticising Justice Krishna Iyer; Justice Sudhanshu Dhulia dissenting in full) held that **NOT ALL privately owned property automatically constitutes a "material resource of the community"** under `Article 39(b)`—explicitly overruling Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer's sweeping socialist dicta in *State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy* (1977) and *Sanjeev Coke Manufacturing Co.* (1982). While *some* private resources can qualify depending on context, courts must apply a **multi-factor inquiry**: (i) nature of the resource and its inherent characteristics; (ii) impact on community well-being; (iii) scarcity of the resource; and (iv) consequences of concentrated private ownership (guided by the **Public Trust Doctrine**—e.g., spectrum, mines, forests, rivers, or critical infrastructure, not ordinary private homes or personal assets). The Court underlined that India's Constitution was deliberately crafted as an **economically flexible framework**, not wedded to rigid state-socialist dogma.

> - ***In Re: Directions in the Matter of Demolition of Structures* (13 November 2024 — `Article 32` "Bulldozer Justice" Guidelines):** Exercising direct `Article 32` jurisdiction, Justices B.R. Gavai and K.V. Viswanathan struck down punitive executive demolitions of the homes of persons merely accused (or even convicted) of crimes. Invoking the rule of law, separation of powers (the executive cannot play judge, jury, and executioner), and the `Article 21` **right to shelter**, the Court issued binding pan-India guidelines—mandating at least **15 days' prior written show-cause notice**, personal hearing, reasoned speaking order, digital portal upload, videographed demolition, and personal liability/contempt for errant officers (excluding unauthorised encroachments on public roads, footpaths, waterways, or railway lines).

### Rights Outside Part III

Fundamental Rights in Part III are not the only rights guaranteed by the Constitution. Four major **constitutional (non-fundamental) rights** reside outside Part III—a frequent UPSC Prelims elimination trap:

1. **`Article 265` (Part XII):** No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law.
2. **`Article 300A` (Part XII, added by `44th Amendment, 1978`):** No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law.
3. **`Article 301` (Part XIII):** Trade, commerce, and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be free.
4. **`Article 326` (Part XV):** Elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies shall be on the basis of **universal adult suffrage** (voting age lowered from 21 to 18 by the `61st Amendment, 1988`; note that the right to vote is a constitutional/statutory right, NOT a Fundamental Right—*Kuldip Nayar*, 2006).

> [!example]
> **Why the Part III vs Outside-Part III Distinction Matters in Practice:**
> If the State illegally confiscates a farmer's land without legislative authority (`Article 300A`) or levies an unlegislated cess (`Article 265`), the aggrieved citizen **cannot file a direct writ petition in the Supreme Court under `Article 32`**! Instead, the citizen must approach the **High Court under `Article 226`** (under "any other purpose") or institute an ordinary civil suit. By contrast, if the State destroys a home as arbitrary punishment without due process—violating the `Article 21` right to shelter and `Article 14` rule of law—direct `Article 32` relief lies immediately (*Bulldozer Guidelines*, 13 November 2024).

### Criticism, Significance & Mains Answer Synthesis

#### Six Classical Criticisms of Part III

1. **Excessive Limitations and Qualifications:** Critics observe that what Part III grants with one hand, it takes away with the other via exhaustive exceptions, provisos, and saving clauses (`Articles 19(2)–(6)`, `31A–31C`, `33–34`). Constituent Assembly member Jaspat Roy Kapoor quipped that Part III should be renamed "Limitations on Fundamental Rights."
2. **Absence of Social and Economic Rights:** Unlike socialist constitutions or modern bills of rights (e.g., South Africa's 1996 Constitution), Part III focuses predominantly on political and civil rights, relegating right to work, living wage, social security, and health to non-justiciable Directive Principles (though judicial activism under `Article 21` has partially bridged this gap).
3. **Lack of Clarity — "A Paradise for Lawyers":** British jurist **Sir Ivor Jennings** criticised the convoluted legal jargon ("public order," "reasonable restrictions," "minorities," "untouchability") as creating endless litigation accessible chiefly to wealthy litigants.
4. **No Permanency:** Because Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights under `Article 368` (subject only to the judge-crafted Basic Structure limit), rights remain vulnerable to strong majorities—evidenced by the removal of property in 1978.
5. **Suspension During Emergency & Peacetime Preventive Detention:** `Articles 358–359` enable sweeping emergency curtailments, and **`Article 22`'s constitutional sanction of peacetime preventive detention** draws sharp international criticism for enabling prolonged detention without trial under laws like NSA and State Goonda Acts.
6. **Expensive & Slow Judicial Remedy:** Approaching High Courts or the Supreme Court entails heavy legal fees and years of pendency, often placing enforcement out of reach for the poorest citizens despite PIL and NALSA legal aid.

#### Six Enduring Significances

Despite these valid critiques, seven and a half decades of lived constitutionalism prove that Part III is the **bedrock of India's democratic resilience**:
1. **Establishes Rule of Law & Constitutional Supremacy:** Checks both executive high-handedness and majoritarian legislative tyranny, ensuring a government of laws, not men.
2. **Protects Minorities & Vulnerable Sections:** Safeguards religious, linguistic, caste, tribal, gender, and queer minorities from social and political marginalisation.
3. **Strengthens India's Secular Fabric:** Balances equal respect for all faiths with transformative social reform (`Articles 25–28`).
4. **Fosters Social Equality & Human Dignity:** Abolishes untouchability (`Art. 17`), forced labour (`Art. 23`), and child exploitation (`Art. 24`), while enabling substantive affirmative action (`Arts. 15–16`).
5. **Enables Participatory Political Democracy:** Protects free press, dissent, peaceful assembly, associations, and voters' right to information (`Art. 19`).
6. **Living Instrument of Socio-Economic & Ecological Justice:** Through the Golden Triangle (`Arts. 14–19–21`), proportionality (*Puttaswamy*), and climate jurisprudence (*M.K. Ranjitsinh*, 2024), Part III continually evolves to meet twenty-first-century challenges of data surveillance, algorithmic governance, and climate crisis.

## 1. Nature, Origins & Salient Features (`Part III, Arts. 12–35`)

- **Magna Carta of India:** `Part III (Arts. 12–35)` establishes political democracy and a "government of laws, not men." Historical roots: Constitution of India Bill (1895), Commonwealth of India Bill (1925), **Nehru Report (1928)**, **Karachi Congress Resolution (1931)**, **Sapru Committee (1945)** (split justiciable vs non-justiciable). Drafted by FR Sub-Committee chaired by **J.B. Kripalani** under Advisory Committee chaired by **Sardar Patel**. Inspired by **US Bill of Rights**, but includes express textual qualifications.
- **7 Groups → 6 Groups:** **Right to Property** (`Art. 19(1)(f)` & `Art. 31`) deleted from Part III by **`44th Amendment Act, 1978`** and shifted to **`Art. 300A`** (Part XII, constitutional/legal right — enforceable via `Art. 226`, NOT `Art. 32`; protects against executive action, not valid law). Remaining 6 groups: Equality (`14–18`), Freedom (`19–22`), Exploitation (`23–24`), Religion (`25–28`), Cultural & Educational (`29–30`), Constitutional Remedies (`32`).
- **Citizens-Only Rights:** **`Arts. 15, 16, 19, 29, 30`** (`Art. 19` excludes legal persons/companies too, though citizen shareholders may sue — *Bennett Coleman*, 1972). **All Persons (Citizens + Foreigners):** `Arts. 14, 20, 21, 21A, 22, 23, 24, 25–28` (enemy aliens excluded from `Art. 22(1)–(2)`).
- **Horizontal Rights (Against Private Individuals Too):** Textually **`Arts. 15(2), 17, 23, 24`**; judicially expanded to **`Arts. 19 & 21`** in ***Kaushal Kishor v. State of U.P.* (Jan 2023, 4–1)**.
- **Emergency Suspension (`44th Amendment, 1978`):** **`Arts. 20 & 21` can NEVER be suspended** (`Art. 359`). `Art. 19` suspends automatically (`Art. 358`) ONLY on **war or external aggression**—NEVER on **armed rebellion**.

## 2. Article 12 ('State') & Article 13 (Judicial Review)

- **`Art. 12` Four Limbs of 'State':** (1) Union Govt & Parliament; (2) State Govts & Legislatures; (3) Local authorities (municipalities, panchayats, district/port trusts); (4) **Other authorities** (statutory & non-statutory).
- **'Other Authorities' Evolution:** *Rajasthan State Electricity Board* (1967, rejected *ejusdem generis*) → *Sukhdev Singh* (1975, ONGC/LIC/IFC) → *R.D. Shetty* (1979) → ***Ajay Hasia* (1981)** (6-factor instrumentality test) → ***Pradeep Kumar Biswas* (2002, 7-judge)** (CSIR is State: deep & pervasive financial, functional & administrative domination). ***Zee Telefilms* (2005, 5-judge 3–2):** **BCCI is NOT 'State'** under `Art. 12`, but amenable to High Court writ under `Art. 226` for public duties. NCERT is not State.
- **Judiciary as 'State':** Administrative/rule-making functions = State (*Prem Chand Garg*, 1963); **judicial orders** = NOT State under `Art. 12`/`32` (*Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar*, 1966, 9-judge; curative remedy via *Rupa Ashok Hurra*, 2002).
- **`Art. 13` Judicial Review:** `13(1)` pre-constitutional inconsistent laws void prospectively (*Keshavan Madhava Menon*, 1951); `13(2)` post-constitutional inconsistent laws void *ab initio*; `13(3)(a)` broad "Law" covers statutes, ordinances, rules, bye-laws, orders, notifications, and **custom/usage having force of law**. Personal laws excluded in *Narasu Appa Mali* (1952, Bombay HC), criticised in *Sabarimala* (2018) & bypassed via 1937 Shariat Act in *Shayara Bano* (2017).
- **Four Doctrines Under `Art. 13`:** (1) **Severability** (*F.N. Balsara*, 1951; *R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla*, 1957); (2) **Eclipse** (*Bhikaji Narain Dhakras*, 1955 — pre-constitutional laws dormant & revive upon amendment; *Deep Chand*, 1959 post-constitutional stillborn, nuanced for non-citizens in *Ambica Mills*, 1974); (3) **No Waiver** (*Basheshar Nath*, 1959; *Olga Tellis*, 1985); (4) **Prospective Overruling** (*Golaknath*, 1967 — SC only, constitutional matters).
- **`Art. 13(4)` & Amendments:** *Shankari Prasad* (1951) & *Sajjan Singh* (1965) → *Golaknath* (1967, 6–5 held amendment is "law") → **`24th Amendment (1971)`** inserted `Art. 13(4)` & `Art. 368(3)` → ***Kesavananda Bharati* (24 April 1973, 7–6)** upheld `24th Amend` but imposed **Basic Structure** limit.

## 3. Right to Equality (`Articles 14–18`)

- **`Art. 14` (All Persons):** Pairs **Equality Before Law** (British, negative, Dicey's Rule of Law — only Dicey's 1st & 2nd elements apply in India, NOT 3rd) + **Equal Protection of Laws** (US 14th Amend, positive, substantive equality).
  - **Reasonable Classification (*Anwar Ali Sarkar*, 1952; *R.K. Dalmia*, 1958):** Requires (1) **Intelligible Differentia** + (2) **Rational Nexus** to statutory object. Forbids class legislation.
  - **Arbitrariness & Manifest Arbitrariness:** ***E.P. Royappa* (1974)** (equality & arbitrariness "sworn enemies") → *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) → ***Shayara Bano* (2017, 3–2)** (manifest arbitrariness applied to primary statute, striking down instant triple talaq) → ***Navtej Singh Johar* (2018)** (s. 377 read down) → ***Joseph Shine* (2018)** (s. 497 adultery struck down) → ***ADR* (Feb 2024)** (Electoral Bonds uncapped corporate funding).
  - **6 Exceptions to `Art. 14`:** `Art. 361` (President/Governors — no criminal proceedings/arrest during term; 2 months civil notice); `Art. 361A` (true legislative reporting); `Arts. 105 & 194` (MP/MLA floor immunity — excludes bribery per *Sita Soren*, Mar 2024); `Art. 31C` (`39(b)/(c)` laws); foreign sovereigns/diplomats; UN & agencies.
- **`Art. 15` (Citizens Only):** `15(1)` no State discrimination on **5 grounds "only"** — religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth (NOT residence/language). `15(2)` **horizontal** ban on public access restrictions (shops, restaurants, hotels, wells, roads). Exceptions: `15(3)` women & children; `15(4)` SEBCs/SCs/STs (**`1st Amend, 1951`** after *Champakam Dorairajan*, 1951); `15(5)` admissions incl. private unaided colleges, **exempting minority institutions** (**`93rd Amend, 2005`**; upheld in *Ashoka Kumar Thakur*, 2008 & *Pramati*, 2014); `15(6)` **10% EWS** (**`103rd Amend, 2019`**; upheld 3–2 in ***Janhit Abhiyan*, Nov 2022**).
- **`Art. 16` (Citizens Only — Public Employment):** `16(2)` bars discrimination on **7 grounds "only"** — adds **descent & residence** to Art. 15's 5. Exceptions: `16(3)` **Parliament only** may prescribe residence; `16(4)` backward class quota (enabling, not mandatory); `16(4A)` SC/ST **promotion** (`77th Amend, 1995`) + **consequential seniority** (`85th Amend, 2001`); `16(4B)` **carry-forward backlog** outside 50% cap (`81st Amend, 2000`); `16(5)` religious institutions; `16(6)` 10% EWS (`103rd Amend, 2019`).
  - **Reservation Cases:** ***Indra Sawhney* (1992, 9-judge)** (27% OBC, creamy layer, 50% cap, vertical vs horizontal, no promotion quota); ***M. Nagaraj* (2006)** (3 quantifiable conditions); ***Jarnail Singh* (2018)** (drops backwardness data for SC/ST; affirms creamy layer); *Maratha Quota* (2021 → `105th Amend, 2021`); ***State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* (1 Aug 2024, 7-judge 6–1)** overruled *E.V. Chinnaiah* (2004), permitting **SC/ST sub-classification** on empirical data (no exclusion/100% quota); 4 judges urged SC/ST creamy layer.
- **`Art. 17` (Untouchability):** Absolute, horizontal, undefined in Constitution/statute (historical caste practice — *Devarajiah*, 1958). Enforced via **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** (renamed 1976) & **SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989** (2018 Amend upheld in *Prathvi Raj Chauhan*, 2020).
- **`Art. 18` (Titles):** Bars State titles except **military & academic** distinctions; citizens barred from foreign titles; foreigners/office-holders need **President's consent** for foreign titles/presents/offices. ***Balaji Raghavan* (1996):** Bharat Ratna & Padma awards are NOT titles, valid, **no prefix/suffix**.

## 4. Right to Freedom — Six Freedoms (`Article 19`, Citizens Only)

- **Six Freedoms (originally 7; `19(1)(f)` property omitted by `44th Amend, 1978`):** Natural citizens only (not foreigners/companies). Exhaustive statutory restrictions (`Kaushal Kishor`, Jan 2023).
- **`19(1)(a)` Speech & Expression ↔ `19(2)` (8 Grounds):** Sovereignty & integrity (`16th Amend, 1963`), security of State, friendly relations (`1st Amend, 1951`), **public order** (`1st Amend` — *Ram Manohar Lohia* concentric circles: law & order ≠ public order ≠ security), decency/morality, contempt of court, defamation (*Subramanian Swamy*, 2016), incitement to an offence (`1st Amend`).
  - **Implied Speech Rights:** Press (*Romesh Thappar*, *Brij Bhushan*, *Sakal Papers*, *Bennett Coleman*), Right to Know (*S.P. Gupta*, 1981 → RTI 2005), Voter Right to Info (*ADR* 2002, *PUCL* 2003, NOTA 2013, **Electoral Bonds struck down in *ADR*, 15 Feb 2024**), Commercial Speech (*Tata Press*, 1995), Silence (*Bijoe Emmanuel*, 1986), National Flag (*Naveen Jindal*, 2004), Internet (*Anuradha Bhasin*, 2020).
  - **Key Free-Speech Rulings & Laws:** ***Shreya Singhal* (2015)** struck down **s. 66A IT Act** (discussion/advocacy vs incitement; vagueness/chilling effect); **Sedition:** *Kedar Nath Singh* (1962) → abeyance in *S.G. Vombatkere* (May 2022) → replaced by **BNS Section 152** (1 July 2024).
- **`19(1)(b)` Peaceful Assembly ↔ `19(3)` (2 Grounds: sovereignty/integrity, public order):** Unarmed only; **no right to strike** (*T.K. Rangarajan*, 2003) or *bandh* (*Bharat Kumar*, 1998); CrPC s. 144 / **BNSS s. 163** (*Anuradha Bhasin* bars repetitive use); ***Amit Sahni / Shaheen Bagh* (Oct 2020)** bars indefinite occupation of public roads.
- **`19(1)(c)` Associations, Unions & Co-operative Societies (`97th Amend, 2011`) ↔ `19(4)` (3 Grounds: sovereignty/integrity, public order, morality):** No fundamental right to recognition or strike (*All India Bank Employees*, 1962).
- **`19(1)(d)` Movement & `19(1)(e)` Residence ↔ `19(5)` (2 Grounds: general public interest, protection of Scheduled Tribes):** Internal movement ONLY (foreign travel = `Art. 21`!). ST protection sustains **Inner Line Permit (ILP)** in Arunachal, Nagaland, Mizoram, Manipur.
- **`19(1)(g)` Profession/Trade ↔ `19(6)` (3 Limbs: general public interest, technical qualifications, State monopoly [`1st Amend, 1951`]):** No right to dangerous/immoral trade (*res extra commercium* — liquor, trafficking, gambling).

## 5. Conviction, Life, Education & Arrest (`Articles 20–22`, All Persons)

- **`Art. 20` (Non-Suspendable):** `20(1)` No **ex-post-facto criminal law** (allows retrospective civil/tax laws & beneficial sentence reduction *T. Barai*); `20(2)` No **double jeopardy** (prosecuted AND punished; **courts/judicial tribunals ONLY**, NOT departmental inquiries — *Maqbool Hussain*); `20(3)` No **self-incrimination** (testimonial compulsion only; allows fingerprints/DNA/blood/voice samples *Kathi Kalu Oghad*; bars involuntary **narco-analysis, polygraph, brain mapping** — ***Selvi*, 2010**).
- **`Art. 21` (Non-Suspendable):** ***A.K. Gopalan* (1950)** (Japanese 'procedure established by law', executive only, compartmentalised) → ***Maneka Gandhi* (1978, 7-judge)** ("just, fair and reasonable", **Golden Triangle `14–19–21`**, substantive due process). *ADM Jabalpur* (1976, H.R. Khanna dissent) overruled in *Puttaswamy* (2017).
  - **Landmark Implied Rights (35+):** Dignity (*Francis Coralie*), Livelihood (*Olga Tellis*), Shelter (*Chameli Singh*; **Bulldozer Guidelines, 13 Nov 2024**), Speedy Trial (*Hussainara*), Legal Aid (*Hoskot*), Health/Emergency Care (*Paschim Banga*, *Parmanand Katara*), Clean Environment (*Subhash Kumar*), **Right Against Adverse Climate Change (*M.K. Ranjitsinh*, 21 Mar 2024 — Arts. 14 & 21)**, Gender Identity (*NALSA*, 2014), Choice of Partner (*Shafin Jahan*, 2018), Sleep (*Ramlila Maidan*, 2012), Foreign Travel (*Satwant Singh*), **Privacy & 4-Fold Proportionality (*K.S. Puttaswamy*, 24 Aug 2017, 9-judge unanimous; DPDP Act 2023 & DPDP Rules Nov 2025)**, **Passive Euthanasia & Living Wills (*Common Cause*, 2018 & Jan 2023 simplified norms)**, **Marriage Equality (*Supriyo*, 18 Oct 2023 — 5–0 no FR to marry; 3–2 no civil union/adoption)**.
- **`Art. 21A` (`86th Amendment, 2002`):** Free & compulsory education for **ages 6–14** (accompanied by recast `Art. 45` for <6 ECCE & `Art. 51A(k)` parental duty). Operationalised by **RTE Act, 2009** (25% entry seats in private unaided non-minority schools; minority schools exempt — *Pramati*, 2014).
- **`Art. 22` Arrest & Detention:**
  - **Punitive (`22(1)–(2)`):** 4 rights — grounds of arrest (**written grounds mandatory**, *Pankaj Bansal* 2023 & *Prabir Purkayastha* May 2024), lawyer of choice, magistrate within **24 hours** (excl. journey), judicial authorisation for further detention. *D.K. Basu* (1997) & *Arnesh Kumar* (2014). **Excluded (`22(3)`):** Enemy aliens & preventive detainees.
  - **Preventive (`22(4)–(7)`):** Max **3 months** unless **Advisory Board** (HC-qualified judges) approves (**`44th Amend` 2-month cap NEVER notified** — *A.K. Roy*, 1982); grounds communicated (subject to public interest `22(6)`); earliest representation; Parliament override (`22(7)`). Union List Entry 9 (defence, foreign affairs, security of India) vs Concurrent List Entry 3 (state security, public order, essential supplies). Laws: NSA 1980, COFEPOSA 1974, J&K PSA 1978, State Goonda Acts (plus punitive anti-terror UAPA 1967).

## 6. Right Against Exploitation (`Articles 23–24`, All Persons, Horizontal)

- **`Art. 23`:** Prohibits traffic in human beings, *begar*, and forced labour. ***PUDR v. Union of India* (1982, Asiad Workers):** Paying **below minimum wage** is forced labour! Laws: **ITPA 1956**, **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1976**, BNS ss. 143–146, **Four Labour Codes (in force 21 Nov 2025 — Code on Wages national floor wage)**. **Exception `23(2)`:** Compulsory public service (need not pay) without discrimination on **religion, race, caste, or class** (**NOT sex**!).
- **`Art. 24`:** Bars children **<14 years** in factories, mines, or hazardous employment. **Child & Adolescent Labour Amendment Act, 2016:** Total ban **<14** in ALL occupations (except non-hazardous family enterprises after school & audio-visual/sports) + bans **adolescents (14–18)** from hazardous work. ***M.C. Mehta* (1996)** (Child Labour Rehabilitation Fund); **NCPCR** (2005 Act); ILO Conventions 138 & 182 ratified (2017).

## 7. Religion (`Articles 25–28`) & Cultural-Educational Rights (`Articles 29–30`)

- **`Art. 25` (All Persons):** Freedom of conscience, profess, practise, **propagate** (NO right to convert — ***Rev. Stainislaus*, 1977**). Subject to public order, morality, health, **AND other Part III rights**. State may regulate secular activities (`25(2)(a)`) & enact social reform / throw open public Hindu temples (`25(2)(b)`). Expl. I: Sikh *kirpans*; Expl. II: "Hindus" includes Sikhs, Jains, Buddhists. **ERP Test:** *Shirur Mutt* (1954) → *Sabarimala* (Sept 2018, 4–1; Indu Malhotra dissent; Nov 2019 9-judge bench reference) → *Aishat Shifa* Hijab split verdict (Oct 2022).
- **`Art. 26` (Religious Denominations):** Establish institutions, manage religious affairs, acquire property, administer property per law. Subject ONLY to public order, morality, health. 3-part test (***S.P. Mittal*, 1983**): common faith, organisation, distinctive name. **Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025:** SC interim order (15 Sept 2025) stayed select clauses (incl. 5-year Islam practice rule), not whole Act.
- **`Art. 27`:** No **tax** specifically for promoting a particular religion; **fee** for secular administration/pilgrim services permitted (*Shirur Mutt*, 1954).
- **`Art. 28` (4 Institution Types):** Wholly State-maintained = prohibited; State-administered under endowment/trust = permitted; State-recognised / State-aided = permitted voluntarily with student/guardian consent.
- **`Art. 29` (Citizens Only):** `29(1)` **"any section of citizens"** (includes **majority sections too** — *St. Xavier's*, 1974) may conserve distinct language, script, or culture. `29(2)` No admission denial in State/aided institutions on **religion, race, caste, or language** only (not sex/place of birth).
- **`Art. 30` (Minorities Only — Citizens):** Religious & linguistic minorities ("minority" undefined; determined **State-wise** — ***T.M.A. Pai*, 2002, 11-judge**) right to establish & administer educational institutions; `30(1A)` (`44th Amend`) compensation shield; `30(2)` no aid discrimination. Exempt from `Arts. 15(5)/(6)` & RTE (*P.A. Inamdar*, 2005; *Pramati*, 2014). ***AMU v. Naresh Agarwal* (8 Nov 2024, 7-judge 4–3)** overruled *S. Azeez Basha* (1967): statutory creation does NOT negate minority character. Six national minorities under **NCM Act 1992**: Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Parsis (1993) + Jains (2014); **NCMEI (2004)**; Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (`Art. 350B`).

## 8. Remedies, Writs, Armed Forces & Savings (`Arts. 32–35 & 31A–31C`)

- **`Art. 32`:** Ambedkar's **"very soul and very heart"** (9 Dec 1948); SC = "protector and guarantor" (*Romesh Thappar*, 1950); Basic Structure (*L. Chandra Kumar*, 1997). Original yet concurrent with `Art. 226`; **Part III rights ONLY** (not `Art. 300A`). `32(3)` Parliament never empowered lower courts. **PIL:** Relaxed *locus standi* & epistolary jurisdiction (*S.P. Gupta*, 1981). **Bulldozer Guidelines (13 Nov 2024)** issued under `Art. 32`.
- **Five Writs:** **Habeas Corpus** (detention; against public AND private); **Mandamus** (mandatory public duty; NOT private, President/Governors, discretionary, contractual, non-statutory, HC CJ judicially); **Prohibition** (preventive; judicial/quasi-judicial ONLY); **Certiorari** (preventive & curative; judicial, quasi-judicial & **administrative since 1991**); **Quo Warranto** (substantive permanent public office; **any interested person** may file).
- **`Art. 32` vs `Art. 226`:** `Art. 226` is **wider in scope** (FRs + "any other purpose") but **discretionary**; `Art. 32` is **narrower in scope** (FRs only) but **itself a Fundamental Right** with all-India reach.
- **`Art. 33`:** Parliament alone restricts FRs of armed forces, police/paramilitary, intelligence & telecom (`50th Amend, 1984`); covers non-combatants (*R. Viswan*, 1983).
- **`Art. 34`:** Martial Law (undefined; military rule in specific area; suspends ordinary courts; does NOT auto-suspend *habeas corpus*) & Parliamentary Act of Indemnity.
- **`Art. 35`:** Exclusive Parliamentary competence for `Arts. 16(3), 32(3), 33, 34` & punishments under `Arts. 17 & 23`.
- **Savings (`Arts. 31A–31C`):** **`31A`** (`1st Amend`, 5 agrarian/corporate/mining categories from `14/19`; Presidential assent for state laws; market-value compensation for personal cultivation within ceiling); **`31B` & Ninth Schedule** (`1st Amend`; 13 → 284 laws; ***I.R. Coelho*, 2007** — post-**24 April 1973** laws subject to Basic Structure review); **`31C`** (`25th Amend`, `39(b)/(c)` over `14/19`; judicial-review bar struck down in *Kesavananda*, 1973; 42nd Amend extension struck down in *Minerva Mills*, 1980; ***Property Owners Association*, 5 Nov 2024, 9-judge** held **9–0** pre-42nd `Art. 31C` survives and **8–1** NOT all private property is a "material resource of the community" under `Art. 39(b)`).
- **Rights Outside Part III:** `Art. 265` (no tax without law), `Art. 300A` (property), `Art. 301` (trade/commerce), `Art. 326` (adult suffrage) — enforced via `Art. 226`, NOT `Art. 32`.
