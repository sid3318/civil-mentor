## 1. Directions to the State: origins and design

Part III restrains the State to protect individual liberty; **Part IV** (Articles 36 to 51) directs the State to build a welfare society. These **Directive Principles of State Policy** (DPSPs) embody the social and economic democracy a newly independent, resource-scarce republic could not make immediately enforceable in court. The **Sapru Committee (1945)** first suggested splitting rights into justiciable and non-justiciable categories. Constitutionally, the framers borrowed the idea from Article 45 of the **Constitution of Ireland (1937)**, which had drawn it from the **Constitution of Spain (1931)**. In form, the principles echoed the **Instrument of Instructions** issued to the Governor-General and Governors under the **Government of India Act, 1935**—now addressed to elected legislatures and executives. Introducing the Draft Constitution, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called Part IV a "novel feature" in a parliamentary democracy:

> "If it is said that the Directive Principles have no legal force behind them, I am prepared to admit it. But I am not prepared to admit that they have no sort of binding force at all. Nor am I prepared to concede that they are useless because they have no binding force in law." — B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 4 November 1948

Governments that ignore Part IV, Ambedkar argued, answer not to judges but to voters. Granville Austin later captured the moral unity of Parts III and IV:

> "The core of the commitment to the social revolution lies in Parts III and IV, in the Fundamental Rights and in the Directive Principles of State Policy. These are the conscience of the Constitution." — Granville Austin, *The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation* (1966)

Two provisions open Part IV. **Article 36** adopts Article 12's definition of "the State". **Article 37** states three rules: the principles are **not enforceable by any court**, yet are **fundamental in the governance of the country**, and it is the **duty of the State to apply them in making laws**.

> **Prelims pointer:** Non-justiciable does not mean optional. Article 37 makes the Directive Principles **fundamental in the governance of the country** and a duty of the State in making laws. UPSC's stock phrases come from here: the ideal of a **welfare State** is enshrined in Part IV, the principles spell out **social and economic democracy**, and they work as a check on how the legislature and the executive use their power. With the Preamble and the Fundamental Duties, they also echo the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948).

## 2. Articles 38 to 51: ideological strands and amendments

**The Constitution does not classify the Directive Principles.** Scholars divide Articles 38 to 51 into **socialistic**, **Gandhian** and **liberal-intellectual** principles to reflect the Assembly's ideological currents; Articles 43, 47 and 48 each carry two ideas, so each appears in two groups.

### Socialistic principles

These pursue economic democracy, reduce inequality and protect workers and children:

- **Article 38:** Secure a social order with social, economic and political justice (38(1)); clause **(2)** (**44th Amdt, 1978**) directs minimising income inequalities and eliminating inequalities in **status, facilities and opportunities** among individuals and groups.
- **Article 39:** Secure **(a)** adequate means of livelihood for men and women equally; **(b)** distribution of **material resources of the community** for the common good; **(c)** no **concentration of wealth** and means of production; **(d)** **equal pay for equal work**; **(e)** protection of workers' health and children's tender age; and **(f)** healthy development of children in freedom and dignity and protection from exploitation and moral or material abandonment (healthy-development limb added by **42nd Amdt, 1976**).
- **Article 39A** (**42nd Amdt**): equal justice and **free legal aid**.
- **Article 41:** Right to **work**, **education** and **public assistance** in unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement, within economic capacity.
- **Article 42:** Just, humane work conditions and **maternity relief**.
- **Article 43:** A **living wage** (above minimum wage), decent work, leisure and cultural opportunities.
- **Article 43A** (**42nd Amdt**): **workers' participation in industrial management**.
- **Article 47:** Raise **nutrition** and living standards; improve **public health**.

### Gandhian principles

These reflect Gandhi's programme of village self-rule, rural industry and social uplift:

- **Article 40:** Organise **village panchayats** as units of self-government.
- **Article 43:** Promote rural **cottage industries** on an individual or co-operative basis.
- **Article 43B** (**97th Amdt, 2011**): promote voluntary, autonomous, democratic and professionally managed **co-operative societies**.
- **Article 46:** Promote educational and economic interests of **SCs, STs and weaker sections**, protecting them from exploitation.
- **Article 47:** **Prohibition** of intoxicating drinks and injurious drugs except for medicinal use.
- **Article 48:** Preserve and improve breeds and **prohibit the slaughter of cows, calves** and other milch and draught cattle (a total ban on slaughtering bulls and bullocks was upheld in *Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat*, 2005).

### Liberal-intellectual principles

These draw on secular constitutionalism, conservation and international law:

- **Article 44:** Secure a **Uniform Civil Code** (UCC) across India.
- **Article 45:** **Early childhood care and education** until age **six** (recast by **86th Amdt, 2002**, after ages 6–14 moved to Art. 21A).
- **Article 48:** Organise **agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines**.
- **Article 48A** (**42nd Amdt**): protect the **environment**, forests and wildlife.
- **Article 49:** Protect monuments and objects of **national importance**.
- **Article 50:** Separate the **judiciary from the executive** in public services.
- **Article 51:** Promote **international peace and security**, just relations, respect for **international law and treaties**, and dispute settlement by **arbitration**.

| Amendment | Changes to Part IV |
|---|---|
| **42nd (1976)** | Recast **39(f)**; added **39A, 43A, 48A** |
| **44th (1978)** | Inserted **38(2)** (inequalities among individuals and groups) |
| **86th (2002)** | Substituted **45** (early childhood care below age six) |
| **97th (2011)** | Inserted **43B** (co-operative societies) |

> **Prelims pointer:** The **42nd Amendment** made four Part IV changes—one recast (39(f)) and three new Articles (39A, 43A, 48A). Distinguish original **Article 48** (cattle/agriculture, 1950) from **Article 48A** (environment, 1976), and remember Part IV has no textual classification.

## 3. Directives outside Part IV

Three non-justiciable directives sit outside Part IV and recur in Prelims traps:

- **Article 335 (Part XVI):** Consider **SC/ST** claims in public appointments consistently with administrative efficiency (the **82nd Amendment, 2000** allowed relaxed qualifying marks and standards in promotions).
- **Article 350A (Part XVII, 7th Amendment, 1956):** Provide facilities for **mother-tongue instruction at the primary stage** for linguistic minorities.
- **Article 351 (Part XVII):** Union's duty to promote **Hindi** as a medium for India's composite culture.

> **Prelims pointer:** Only Articles 36–51 belong to Part IV. Articles **335, 350A and 351** are directives *outside* Part IV.

## 4. Criticism, utility and implementation

Sceptics attacked Part IV from the start. **K.T. Shah** called DPSPs a cheque payable at a bank's convenience; **K.C. Wheare** termed them a "manifesto of aims and aspirations"; **Ivor Jennings** saw dated Fabian socialism; and **T.T. Krishnamachari** called Part IV a "veritable dustbin of sentiment" letting any member ride a hobby-horse into it. Four criticisms recur: **no legal force**, **illogical arrangement**, **conservative priorities**, and potential **constitutional conflict** between centre and states or legislatures and courts.

Practice proved otherwise. DPSPs ensure policy continuity across governments, offer voters a welfare yardstick, and help courts gauge whether statutory limits on Articles 14 and 19 are reasonable. Five-Year Plans under the Planning Commission—and now NITI Aayog's SDG localisation—operationalised Part IV alongside major statutes:

| Directive | Key implementation |
|---|---|
| **39(b)–(c)** | Land reforms, bank nationalisation (1969), competition law |
| **39A** | Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (NALSA, Lok Adalats) |
| **40** | 73rd Amendment (1992): constitutional Panchayati Raj (Part IX) |
| **41** | MGNREGA, 2005—replaced from **1 July 2026** by **VB-G RAM G Act, 2025** (125 days' rural work); social pensions |
| **42** | Maternity Benefit Act, 1961 (26 weeks since 2017); Code on Social Security, 2020 |
| **43** | Minimum Wages Act, 1948, subsumed in **Code on Wages, 2019** (four Labour Codes in force **21 Nov 2025**) |
| **44** | Special Marriage Act, 1954; **Uttarakhand UCC, 2024** (in force **27 Jan 2025**); Gujarat UCC, 2026 (enacted, not yet in force) |
| **45** | ICDS (anganwadis), RTE Act, 2009 (via Art. 21A), NEP 2020 ECCE |
| **47** | Prohibition laws in Gujarat, Bihar, Nagaland and Mizoram; Poshan Abhiyaan, PM POSHAN |
| **48A** | Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 (predating 48A), Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980, Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 |
| **50** | CrPC, 1973 and BNSS, 2023 separating judicial and executive magistrates |
| **51** | Panchsheel, UN peacekeeping, multilateral diplomacy, arbitration |

> **In the news:** On **27 January 2025**, Uttarakhand became the first state in independent India to enforce a **Uniform Civil Code**—the **Uniform Civil Code, Uttarakhand, 2024** (passed 7 Feb 2024; presidential assent 11 Mar 2024), governing marriage, divorce, succession and live-in registration while exempting Scheduled Tribes (Goa retains the Portuguese Civil Code of 1867). **Gujarat** followed: its Assembly passed the Gujarat Uniform Civil Code on 24 March 2026 and the President assented on 17 September 2026, though the state had not brought it into force by November 2026.

Article 44 is the directive Mains asks about most, usually as "what holds India back from a Uniform Civil Code?" The case for a code rests on gender justice and equality (Articles 14 and 15) and on national integration, and the Supreme Court has pressed it more than once: in ***Shah Bano* (1985)** it regretted that Article 44 had remained a dead letter, and in ***Sarla Mudgal* (1995)** it asked the government to take a fresh look at it. The case for caution rests on freedom of religion (Articles 25 and 26), the diversity of personal and customary law (which Articles 371A and 371G and the Sixth Schedule expressly protect in parts of the North-East), and the fear that a majority's practices will be imposed as the common code. The **21st Law Commission**, in its 2018 consultation paper on family law, concluded that a uniform code was "neither necessary nor desirable at this stage" and recommended reforming each personal law to remove discrimination instead; the 22nd Law Commission reopened the question by inviting public views in June 2023. A balanced answer supports the goal, and reaches it step by step: codify and reform personal laws, build consensus, and keep the code optional or state-led until that consensus exists.

## 5. Rights versus directives: the constitutional balance

When welfare statutes collide with Fundamental Rights, which yields? Three decades of litigation and four amendments forged the settlement—and a nine-judge bench reshaped Article 39(b) in 2024.

```text
 Champakam (1951)      FRs > DPSPs → 1st Amdt (Art 15(4))
         ↓
 Kerala Ed. Bill (1958) Harmonious construction
         ↓
 Golaknath (1967)      FRs unamendable → 24th & 25th Amdts
         ↓
 Kesavananda (1973)    Basic structure; 31C limb 1 valid,
                       judicial-review bar struck down
         ↓
 42nd Amdt (1976)      Art 31C widened to ALL DPSPs
         ↓
 Minerva Mills (1980)  Extension void; FR–DPSP balance =
                       basic structure
         ↓
 Waman Rao (1981) /    24 Apr 1973 cut-off for 9th Sch.
 I.R. Coelho (2007)    & unamended Art 31C confirmed
         ↓
 Property Owners (2024) Art 31C survives (9:0); not all
                       private property = 39(b) (8:1)
```

In ***Champakam Dorairajan* (1951)**, striking down communal college quotas, the Court ruled that DPSPs run **subsidiary** to Fundamental Rights; Parliament answered with the **1st Amendment (1951)** (Article 15(4), Articles 31A–31B, Ninth Schedule). In ***Re Kerala Education Bill* (1958)**, the Court adopted **harmonious construction**, reconciling Parts III and IV wherever possible.

The clash deepened in ***Golaknath* (1967)**, holding 6:5 that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights even to enact DPSPs. Parliament passed the **24th Amendment (1971)** (confirming amending power via Article 13(4) and mandatory presidential assent) and the **25th Amendment (1971)**, inserting **Article 31C**. Its first limb shielded laws implementing **Article 39(b) or 39(c)** from Articles 14 and 19 (and then 31); its second limb barred judicial review of legislative declarations claiming that nexus.

In ***Kesavananda Bharati* (24 April 1973)**, thirteen judges overruled *Golaknath* but bounded amending power by the **basic structure** doctrine. They upheld Article 31C's **first limb** and invalidated its **second limb** for ousting judicial review. During the Emergency, the **42nd Amendment (1976)** widened Article 31C to shield laws implementing **all** DPSPs against Articles 14 and 19.

In ***Minerva Mills* (31 July 1980)**, the Court struck down that blanket expansion. Comparing Parts III and IV to two wheels of a chariot, Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud laid down the settled rule:

> "The Indian Constitution is founded on the bed-rock of the balance between Parts III and IV. To give absolute primacy to one over the other is to disturb the harmony of the Constitution. This harmony and balance between fundamental rights and directive principles is an essential feature of the basic structure of the Constitution." — Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud, *Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India* (31 July 1980)

***Waman Rao* (1981)** confirmed that pre-24 April 1973 Ninth Schedule laws stayed immune while later additions faced basic-structure review—a boundary cemented in ***I.R. Coelho* (2007)**.

> **In the news:** On **5 November 2024**, a nine-judge bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud in ***Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra*** held **unanimously (9:0)** that pre-42nd **Article 31C** (as upheld in *Kesavananda*) **still survives** after *Minerva Mills*. By **8:1** (Justice Sudhanshu Dhulia dissenting; Justice B.V. Nagarathna concurring on outcome while objecting to criticism of Justice Krishna Iyer), it held that **not all private property** is a "material resource of the community" under **Article 39(b)**, overruling *Sanjeev Coke* (1982) and rejecting Justice Krishna Iyer's *Ranganatha Reddy* (1977) view. Inclusion turns on context—scarcity, community impact and effects of private concentration—affirming economic democracy over rigid dogma.

Today, Fundamental Rights prevail generally, while genuine **Article 39(b)–(c)** laws retain Article 31C's shield from Articles 14 and 19—subject to judicial review and the 2024 criteria. Meanwhile, courts continuously read directives like legal aid, environment and livelihood into Article 21.

## 6. Fundamental Duties: Article 51A

Absent in 1950, Fundamental Duties entered the Constitution during the Emergency via the **42nd Amendment (1976)** on the **Swaran Singh Committee (1976)** report. Inspired by the **Constitution of the USSR** (Japan's democratic constitution also lists duties), **Part IVA** holds a single provision—**Article 51A**—originally with **ten** duties. The **86th Amendment (2002)** added an **eleventh**, clause **(k)**, pairing parental responsibility with Article 21A.

Prelims routinely tests what Parliament **rejected** from the Swaran Singh report: while the committee suggested **eight** duties and Parliament enacted **ten**, it refused **penalties or punishments** for breach, **immunity from judicial review** for penal laws, and a **duty to pay taxes**.

### The eleven duties (Article 51A)

Every **citizen of India** has the duty to:

- **(a)** abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals, institutions, the **National Flag and National Anthem**;
- **(b)** cherish and follow the noble ideals of the freedom struggle;
- **(c)** uphold and protect India's **sovereignty, unity and integrity**;
- **(d)** defend the country and render national service when called upon;
- **(e)** promote harmony and common brotherhood across diversities, and **renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women**;
- **(f)** value and preserve the heritage of our **composite culture**;
- **(g)** protect and improve the **natural environment** (forests, lakes, rivers, wildlife) and have compassion for living creatures;
- **(h)** develop **scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform**;
- **(i)** safeguard **public property** and abjure violence;
- **(j)** strive towards **excellence** in individual and collective activity;
- **(k)** *(86th Amendment, 2002)*—as parent or guardian—provide educational opportunities to a child or ward aged **six to fourteen**.

### Scope, statutes and judicial use

Fundamental Duties bind **citizens only** (not foreigners) and are **non-justiciable**: no writ directly enforces them, and the Constitution itself provides neither a procedure to enforce them nor a penalty for breach. Parliament remains free to back a duty with an ordinary law. The **Justice J.S. Verma Committee (1999)** mapped statutes already backing them: the **Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971** (51A(a)); the **Representation of the People Act, 1951** disqualifying communal appeals (51A(e)); the **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** and criminal provisions on enmity and offences against women (51A(e)); the **Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967** (51A(c)); and the **Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972** and **Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980** (51A(g)).

Courts also invoke Article 51A as an interpretive guide and test of reasonableness. In ***AIIMS Students' Union v. AIIMS* (2001)**, the Court struck down excessive institutional PG reservation (33 per cent overall / 50 per cent discipline-wise), citing **Article 51A(j)** (excellence). In the ***M.C. Mehta*** cases, it paired **Article 51A(g)** with Articles 48A and 21 to protect rivers and forests and mandate environmental education. In ***Shyam Narayan Chouksey***, citing **Article 51A(a)**, it initially required cinemas to play the National Anthem (30 Nov 2016), then modified the order on **9 January 2018** to make playing **optional** while retaining the duty to stand respectfully (exempting persons with disabilities).

Critics observe that Part IVA omits **voting, tax-paying and family planning**, uses vague terms ("scientific temper", "composite culture"), lacks direct penalties and bears Emergency-era origins. Yet rights and duties remain correlatives: civic restraint, fraternity and ecological care sustain the liberties Part III guarantees.

## 7. Rights, directives and duties compared

| Dimension | Fundamental Rights (Part III) | Directive Principles (Part IV) | Fundamental Duties (Part IVA) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Articles & source** | Arts. 12–35; USA | Arts. 36–51; Ireland (via Spain) | Art. 51A(a)–(k); USSR |
| **Origin** | Original (1950) | Original (1950) | 42nd Amdt (1976); 11th by 86th (2002) |
| **Nature & goal** | Mostly negative; political democracy | Positive; social & economic democracy | Civic duties; fraternity & responsibility |
| **Enforceability** | Justiciable (Arts. 32, 226) | Non-justiciable (Art. 37) | Non-justiciable; statutory backing |
| **Scope & sanction** | Citizens & foreigners; judicial invalidation | The State (Art. 36); electoral sanction | **Citizens only**; ordinary laws only |

> **Prelims pointer:** Watch three cross-part traps: (1) Part IV has **no textual classification**, and **Arts. 335, 350A and 351** lie *outside* Part IV; (2) Fundamental Duties bind **citizens only**, omit taxes and voting, and Swaran Singh's penalty/tax/review-bar proposals were rejected; (3) *Property Owners Association* (2024) was **9:0** on Art. 31C's survival and **8:1** on Art. 39(b).

> **Mains angle:** Anchor GS-2 answers on two themes. **FR–DPSP harmony:** trace *Champakam* → *Minerva Mills* → *Property Owners Association* (2024) to show how constitutionalism balances social justice with economic democracy; on UCC (Art. 44), weigh gender justice against tribal and federal sensitivities (Uttarakhand, 2025). **Rights–duties balance:** back civic education and the Verma Committee's statutory path while insisting duties must never become a precondition for enjoying Fundamental Rights.
