## 1. Philosophy, Origin & Features of Directive Principles of State Policy

Whereas Part III of the Constitution (`Articles 12` to `35`) erects legally enforceable barriers against arbitrary State power to secure **political democracy**, **Part IV** (`Articles 36` to `51`) sets out a positive constitutional charter commanding the State to transform an impoverished, deeply stratified post-colonial society into an egalitarian **Welfare State**. Enshrined as the **Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs)**, these sixteen Articles articulate the socio-economic ideals, moral compass, and institutional goals that legislatures and governments across India must steadily translate into law and public policy. Together, Parts III and IV anchor what the Preamble proclaims as India's dual promise: liberty and equality on the one hand, and social, economic, and political justice on the other.

The framers understood with piercing realism that constitutional democracy cannot survive on voting rights and free speech alone if millions remain trapped in illiteracy, hunger, landlessness, caste subordination, and unemployment. Yet they recognised with equal pragmatism that the newly independent Indian republic of 1950—reeling from Partition, princely integration, industrial underdevelopment, and acute fiscal scarcity—lacked the immediate financial capacity and administrative infrastructure to make socio-economic entitlements such as a living wage, universal old-age pensions, or early childhood care immediately enforceable through judicial writs. Their ingenious constitutional solution was to separate citizen-empowering civil liberties (justiciable under Part III) from State-directing socio-economic mandates (non-justiciable yet constitutionally fundamental under Part IV), creating a dynamic roadmap for progressive social revolution.

### Constitutional Origins & Historical Evolution

The architectural lineage of Part IV blends comparative constitutional borrowing with indigenous anti-colonial thought and British Indian statutory practice. Four distinct intellectual and institutional currents converged in the Constituent Assembly to shape Articles `36` to `51`:

1. **The Irish Constitution of 1937 and Its Spanish Roots:** Directly, the Drafting Committee borrowed the concept and phrasing of Directive Principles from **`Article 45` of the Constitution of Ireland (1937)**, titled *"Directive Principles of Social Policy."* Ireland's framers, in turn, had adapted the idea from the **Constitution of the Second Spanish Republic (1931)** (with broader early twentieth-century precedents in the **Weimar Constitution of Germany, 1919**, which codified social and economic obligations of the State). Like India, Ireland faced the challenge of inscribing Catholic-communitarian and welfare goals into a democratic constitution without empowering courts to hijack budgetary priorities.
2. **The Freedom Struggle and the Sapru Committee Report (1945):** Long before the Constituent Assembly met, the **Karachi Resolution of the Indian National Congress (1931)** had fused civil freedoms with economic entitlements—living wages, healthy working conditions, maternity benefits, reduction of land revenue, and State control of key industries. Crucially, in **1945**, the non-partisan **Sapru Committee** (chaired by Sir Tej Bahadur Sapru) made the decisive structural proposal that Indian constitutional rights be split into **two distinct categories**: **justiciable rights** enforceable by courts (which evolved into Part III) and **non-justiciable rights** serving as moral and legislative guides (which evolved into Part IV).
3. **The Instrument of Instructions under the Government of India Act, 1935:** In constitutional form, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar explicitly linked the Directive Principles to the **Instrument of Instructions** issued by the British Crown to the Governor-General and the Governors of the provinces under the **`Government of India Act, 1935`**. The critical democratic transformation, however, lay in *who* issued the instructions and *to whom* they were addressed: under colonial rule, the British monarch instructed imperial governors; under the 1950 Constitution, the sovereign people of India address binding governance instructions to **elected legislatures and executives** at both Union and State levels.
4. **Constituent Assembly Debates & Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's "Novel Feature":** Inside the Constituent Assembly, the **Sub-Committee on Fundamental Rights** (chaired by J.B. Kripalani) under the Advisory Committee (chaired by Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel) drafted both Parts III and IV, with Sir B.N. Rau (Constitutional Advisor) forcefully advocating the distinction between enforceable rights and moral-political directives after consulting Irish constitutionalists including Éamon de Valera and Justice John Hearne. Introducing the Draft Constitution on **4 November 1948**, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar hailed the Directive Principles as a **"novel feature"** of the Indian Constitution—designed to ensure that regardless of which political party captured power through parliamentary elections, every government would remain tethered to the overarching goal of **economic democracy**:

> "If it is said that the Directive Principles have no legal force behind them, I am prepared to admit it. But I am not prepared to admit that they have no sort of binding force at all. Nor am I prepared to concede that they are useless because they have no binding force in law." — B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 4 November 1948

Ambedkar went further to explain the true sanction behind Part IV. In a parliamentary democracy built on universal adult suffrage, a government that contemptuously ignores the Directive Principles may escape a High Court writ, yet it cannot escape the **ballot box**: *"Whoever captures power will not be free to do what he likes with it. In the exercise of it, he will have to respect these Instruments of Instructions which are called Directive Principles. He cannot ignore them. He may not have to answer for their breach in a Court of Law. But he will certainly have to answer for them before the electorate at election time."*

Two decades later, constitutional historian **Granville Austin** distilled the philosophical unity of Parts III and IV into one of the most frequently quoted assessments in Indian constitutional scholarship:

> "The core of the commitment to the social revolution lies in Parts III and IV, in the Fundamental Rights and in the Directive Principles of State Policy. These are the conscience of the Constitution." — Granville Austin, *The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation* (1966)

Equally noteworthy for comparative human rights jurisprudence is the deep alignment between the Indian Constitution and the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), 1948**, adopted by the United Nations General Assembly just as the Constituent Assembly was debating the Draft Constitution. While Part III mirrors the civil and political rights of the UDHR (Articles 3–21), Part IV directly parallels the **economic, social, and cultural rights** of the UDHR (Articles 22–27)—covering social security, work, equal pay, rest and leisure, health, nutrition, and education—alongside the Preamble and the later Fundamental Duties (`Article 51A`, echoing UDHR Article 29 on duties to the community).

### Welfare State vs Police State: Establishing Social and Economic Democracy

The deepest ideological rupture enacted by Part IV is the rejection of the nineteenth-century laissez-faire **"Police State"** in favour of a twentieth-century constitutional **"Welfare State."** Under British colonial governance, the primary functions of the Indian State were strictly minimalist and extractive: maintaining law and order, securing imperial frontiers, collecting land revenue, and protecting commercial contracts, while leaving famine relief, healthcare, education, and caste reform largely to private charity or neglect.

Part IV overturns that philosophy completely. It obligates the Indian Republic to intervene proactively in economic and social life so that wealth does not concentrate in a few hands, workers receive humane conditions and living wages, children are sheltered from exploitation, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and weaker sections are uplifted, and village communities exercise democratic self-rule. As Ambedkar famously warned in his closing address to the Constituent Assembly on **25 November 1949**, on 26 January 1950 India was entering a "life of contradictions": in politics India would have equality (`one man, one vote, one vote one value`), whereas in social and economic life, structural inequality persisted. Part IV is the constitutional mechanism crafted to resolve that contradiction peacefully through democratic legislation rather than violent revolution.

```compare
# Colonial Police State vs Constitutional Welfare State (Part IV Vision)
Police State (Colonial / Laissez-Faire Model) | Welfare State (Part IV Constitutional Model)
Primary Role :: Minimalist — limited to defence, policing, law and order, and revenue extraction | Proactive & developmental — secures social, economic, and political justice for all citizens
Nature of Democracy :: Formal political citizenship (or colonial subjecthood) without socio-economic guarantees | Substantive social and economic democracy reinforcing political liberty under Part III
Economic Philosophy :: Unregulated market autonomy; indifference to concentration of wealth or subsistence wages | Regulated mixed/economic democracy (`Art. 39(b)-(c)`), living wages (`Art. 43`), and social safety nets (`Art. 41`)
Relationship with Citizens :: Negative restraint ("leave the citizen alone" except for tax and order) | Positive stewardship — nutrition (`Art. 47`), healthcare, early education (`Art. 45`), and legal aid (`Art. 39A`)
Constitutional Anchor :: Absent from 1950 framework (echoed colonial governance under pre-1947 regime) | Preamble ("Socialist", Social & Economic Justice) + Part IV (`Articles 36–51`)
```

### Opening Provisions: Article 36 and Article 37

Before enumerating substantive socio-economic goals in Articles `38` through `51`, Part IV opens with two foundational structural Articles—`Article 36` and `Article 37`—which define *who* is bound by the directives and *what* constitutional force they possess.

#### Article 36: Definition of "the State"

`Article 36` provides that *"In this Part, unless the context otherwise requires, 'the State' has the same meaning as in Part III."* Rather than duplicating the four-limb definition already laid down in `Article 12`, `Article 36` incorporates it seamlessly by reference. Consequently, the constitutional obligation to implement Directive Principles does **not** rest solely upon the Union Parliament or State Legislatures; it extends comprehensively to:
- the Government and Parliament of India (Union executive and legislature);
- the Government and Legislature of each of the States (State executives and legislatures);
- all **local authorities** within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India (municipalities, panchayats, district boards, improvement trusts); and
- all **other authorities**—including statutory corporations, public sector undertakings (PSUs), universities, and agencies functioning as instrumentalities of the State (*Ajay Hasia*, 1981; *Pradeep Kumar Biswas*, 2002).

This breadth carries immediate practical significance: a municipal corporation framing urban housing rules, a public sector bank designing rural credit norms, or a State government setting public health budgets is constitutionally guided by Part IV.

#### Article 37: Non-Justiciability Yet "Fundamental in the Governance of the Country"

`Article 37` is the legal fulcrum of Part IV and one of the most heavily tested provisions in UPSC Prelims. Its exact text lays down **three interconnected propositions**:
1. **Non-Justiciability:** *"The provisions contained in this Part shall not be enforceable by any court..."* No citizen can move the Supreme Court under `Article 32` or a High Court under `Article 226` seeking a writ of *mandamus* compelling Parliament or a State Legislature to enact a Uniform Civil Code, enforce prohibition, or create a statutory pension scheme. Likewise, courts cannot declare a statute unconstitutional *solely* on the ground that it fails to implement—or even runs contrary to—a Directive Principle.
2. **Fundamental in Governance:** *"...but the principles therein laid down are nevertheless **fundamental in the governance of the country**..."* Even without direct judicial compulsion, Part IV is not ornamental rhetoric. It forms the permanent constitutional benchmark of legitimate statecraft across ministries, administrative departments, planning bodies, and executive agencies.
3. **Mandatory Duty in Law-Making:** *"...and it shall be the **duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws**."* Notice the imperative auxiliary **"shall."** While the timing, pace, and fiscal design of welfare legislation belong to elected representatives, the constitutional obligation to steer law-making toward Part IV is binding in principle upon every legislature—Union, State, and local.

```keyfacts
# Part IV Architecture at a Glance
Articles 36–51 | Constitutional Locus | Enshrined in Part IV; inspired by Irish Constitution (1937, Art. 45) via Spanish Constitution (1931) & GoI Act 1935 Instrument of Instructions
Article 36 & Article 37 | Scope & Force | Art. 36 imports Art. 12's 4-limb 'State'; Art. 37 makes DPSPs non-justiciable yet 'fundamental in governance' & a law-making duty
No Textual Grouping | Three Scholarly Streams | Socialistic (38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 43, 43A, 47), Gandhian (40, 43, 43B, 46, 47, 48), Liberal-Intellectual (44, 45, 48, 48A, 49, 50, 51)
4 Major Amendments | Post-1950 Additions | 42nd (1976: 39(f), 39A, 43A, 48A), 44th (1978: 38(2)), 86th (2002: recast 45), 97th (2011: 43B); plus 7th (1956) minor tweak to Art. 49
```

### Salient Features & Constitutional Functions of Directive Principles

Synthesising the text of `Articles 36–51`, Constituent Assembly debates, and seven decades of Supreme Court jurisprudence reveals six defining features of India's Directive Principles:

1. **Constitutional Ideals Across Legislative, Executive, and Administrative Action:** Part IV denotes the ideals the State must keep continuously in mind while formulating policies and enacting laws. They function as comprehensive constitutional recommendations covering legislative enactment, executive rulemaking, budgetary allocation, and day-to-day administrative governance.
2. **Modern Democratic Equivalent of the Instrument of Instructions:** Just as the 1935 Act's Instrument of Instructions guided colonial Governors, DPSPs act as standing governance instructions to Indian legislatures and executives—except that their legitimacy flows from popular sovereignty rather than imperial prerogative.
3. **Comprehensive Socio-Economic and Political Programme:** Rather than listing narrow platitudes, Part IV articulates an integrated blueprint for a modern democratic republic—bridging agrarian modernisation, industrial workers' rights, grassroots village self-government, minority and SC/ST uplift, gender-equal family law, judicial independence, ecological conservation, and peaceful international diplomacy.
4. **Non-Justiciable—Neither a Source of Nor a Direct Limitation on Legislative Power:** A subtle constitutional nuance frequently examined by UPSC is that Directive Principles **do not by themselves confer legislative competence** on Parliament or State Legislatures (legislative power comes strictly from `Article 246` and the Seventh Schedule), nor do they operate as a **direct legal limitation** invalidating legislation in the way Part III does. Rather, they are positive **enabling and guiding mandates** that legislatures fulfil using their Seventh Schedule powers.
5. **Crucial Interpretive Aid for Constitutional Courts:** Although non-justiciable directly, Part IV exerts immense *indirect* legal force in courts in three distinct ways:
   - **Testing "Reasonableness" of Restrictions on Fundamental Rights:** Whenever the State restricts a freedom under `Article 19` or creates a statutory classification under `Article 14`, the Supreme Court has consistently held—starting from ***State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara* (1951)** (upholding Bombay prohibition law via `Art. 47`), ***Mohd. Hanif Quareshi* (1958)**, and ***Pathumma v. State of Kerala* (1978)**—that a law enacted to give effect to a Directive Principle carries a **strong presumption of reasonableness and public interest**!
   - **Expanding the Scope of Article 21 (Right to Life):** Since *Maneka Gandhi* (1978), the judiciary has breathed substantive dignity into `Article 21` by reading Part IV directives—free legal aid (`39A`), livelihood (`39(a)`/`41`), elementary education (original `45`), public health (`47`), and environmental/climate protection (`48A`)—directly into enforceable Fundamental Rights.
   - **Resolving Statutory Ambiguity:** Where a statute admits of two interpretations, courts prefer the construction that advances rather than frustrates the Directive Principles (*U.P. State Electricity Board v. Hari Shankar Jain*, 1978).
6. **Political and Electoral Accountability:** Because Part IV expresses the constitutional promise made to citizens, political parties anchor manifestos in DPSP targets, opposition parties measure governmental failures against them, and courts and statutory commissions invoke them to hold administration morally and institutionally accountable.

> [!prelims]
> **High-Yield UPSC Prelims Traps on Philosophy & Features of Part IV:**
> 1. **Direct Limitation on Legislature? NO! (UPSC Prelims 2020):** Directive Principles constitute **neither** a limitation upon the legislative function **nor** a limitation upon the executive function in the strict legal sense of voiding laws; they are *positive non-justiciable mandates* guiding State action. (Fundamental Rights, by contrast, impose legal limitations on both.)
> 2. **Ideal of a "Welfare State" (UPSC Prelims 2015 & 2020):** Explicitly enshrined in **Part IV (Directive Principles of State Policy)**—not in Fundamental Rights or the Seventh Schedule.
> 3. **Where Does "Economic Justice" Appear? (UPSC Prelims 2013):** Both in the **Preamble** AND in the **Directive Principles of State Policy (`Article 38`)**—NOT in Fundamental Rights!
> 4. **Reflection of UDHR, 1948 (UPSC Prelims 2020):** Principles of the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)** are reflected across **ALL THREE**: the **Preamble**, **Directive Principles of State Policy**, AND **Fundamental Duties** (in addition to Fundamental Rights).
> 5. **Purpose of DPSPs (UPSC Prelims 2017):** To establish **social and economic democracy** (whereas Fundamental Rights establish *political democracy*).

> [!remember]
> **Non-Justiciable ≠ Optional or Legally Irrelevant:**
> Never equate `Article 37`'s non-justiciability with constitutional irrelevance. Remember the **Three Shields of Part IV**:
> - **Political Shield:** Democratic accountability at the ballot box (Ambedkar's primary sanction).
> - **Interpretive Shield:** Courts treat laws fulfilling DPSPs as prima facie **"reasonable restrictions"** under `Article 19` and valid classifications under `Article 14` (*Balsara*, *Pathumma*, *Mirzapur Moti Kureshi*).
> - **Constitutional Safe-Harbour Shield:** Laws genuinely implementing `Articles 39(b)` and `39(c)` enjoy direct immunity from `Articles 14` and `19` under **`Article 31C`** (reaffirmed 9–0 in *Property Owners Association*, November 2024).

## 2. Classification & Article-by-Article Exposition (Articles 38–51)

One of the single most important textual rules for UPSC Prelims must be stated up front: **the Constitution of India does NOT classify the Directive Principles into ideological categories.** Part IV simply enumerates `Articles 38` through `51` sequentially without sub-headings. However, because the Constituent Assembly represented a broad ideological coalition uniting Nehruvian democratic socialists, orthodox Gandhians, and modernist constitutional liberals, jurists and standard textbooks (notably M.P. Jain and M. Laxmikanth) group `Articles 38–51` into **three broad ideological streams**: **Socialistic Principles**, **Gandhian Principles**, and **Liberal-Intellectual Principles**.

Crucially, three Articles—**`Article 43`**, **`Article 47`**, and **`Article 48`**—each contain **two distinct ideological limbs** and therefore belong simultaneously to **two categories**:
- **`Article 43`** pairs *living wage and decent standard of life* (**Socialistic**) with *promotion of rural cottage industries* (**Gandhian**).
- **`Article 47`** pairs *raising nutrition, standard of living, and public health* (**Socialistic**) with *prohibition of intoxicating drinks and drugs* (**Gandhian**).
- **`Article 48`** pairs *modern, scientific agriculture and animal husbandry* (**Liberal-Intellectual**) with *preserving cattle breeds and prohibiting cow and calf slaughter* (**Gandhian**).

### Socialistic Principles (Articles 38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 43, 43A, 47)

Reflecting India's commitment to **democratic socialism**—redistributive justice achieved through constitutional legislation rather than authoritarian state monopoly—the Socialistic Principles target structural poverty, economic concentration, worker exploitation, and unequal access to justice and healthcare.

#### Article 38: Securing a Just Social Order & Eliminating Inequalities

`Article 38` is the overarching capstone of the Welfare State, directly echoing the Preamble's promise of **"justice—social, economic and political."** It consists of two distinct clauses:
- **`Article 38(1)` (Original, 1950):** Directs the State to *"strive to promote the welfare of the people by securing and protecting as effectively as it may a social order in which justice, social, economic and political, shall inform all the institutions of the national life."* Notice the breadth of **"all the institutions of the national life"**—spanning not merely parliaments and courts, but markets, universities, panchayats, and administrative agencies.
- **`Article 38(2)` (Inserted by `44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`):** Directs the State, in particular, *"to strive to minimise the inequalities in income, and endeavour to eliminate inequalities in **status, facilities and opportunities**, not only amongst **individuals** but also amongst **groups of people** residing in different areas or engaged in different vocations."* Look carefully at the precision of wording: the Constitution asks the State to **minimise** inequalities in *income* (acknowledging merit and productivity differentials in a mixed economy) while striving to **eliminate** inequalities in *status, facilities, and opportunities*—explicitly addressing regional backwardness (e.g., aspirational districts, North-East, tribal belts) and occupational disparities (e.g., informal vs formal labour, rural vs urban workers).

#### Article 39: Six Foundational Principles of Economic & Social Policy

`Article 39` is the economic heart of Part IV. Its **six clauses `(a)` through `(f)`**—especially `(b)` and `(c)`, which enjoy special constitutional immunity under `Article 31C`—must be mastered individually for both Prelims and Mains:

- **`Article 39(a)` — Adequate Means of Livelihood:** Directs the State to secure *"that the citizens, men and women equally, have the right to an adequate means of livelihood."* Note that `39(a)` applies specifically to **citizens** and explicitly mandates **gender equality** in livelihood access. In ***Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985)**, the Supreme Court invoked `Articles 39(a)` and `41` to read the **right to livelihood** into `Article 21`.
- **`Article 39(b)` — Equitable Distribution of Material Resources:** Directs *"that the ownership and control of the **material resources of the community** are so distributed as best to subserve the common good."* For decades, this clause served as the constitutional anchor for zamindari abolition, land ceilings, coal mine nationalisation, and public spectrum allocation (*Centre for Public Interest Litigation v. Union of India* / 2G Spectrum Case, 2012). In its landmark **5 November 2024** verdict in ***Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra*** (examined in full in Section 3), a 9-judge Constitution Bench clarified (8–1) that **"material resources of the community"** can include *certain* privately owned resources depending on scarcity, community impact, and the Public Trust Doctrine, but does **not** automatically sweep in every private asset.
- **`Article 39(c)` — Preventing Concentration of Wealth:** Directs *"that the operation of the economic system does not result in the **concentration of wealth and means of production** to the common detriment."* Notice the critical qualifier **"to the common detriment"**: the Constitution does not condemn enterprise or scale per se, only predatory monopolies and oligopolistic concentration that harm public welfare—providing the constitutional foundation for the former MRTP Act (1969), the modern **Competition Act, 2002** (Competition Commission of India), progressive taxation, and anti-trust regulation of digital monopolies.
- **`Article 39(d)` — Equal Pay for Equal Work:** Directs *"that there is **equal pay for equal work** for both men and women."* While located in Part IV, the Supreme Court in ***Randhir Singh v. Union of India* (1982)** elevated this principle into an enforceable constitutional mandate by reading `Article 39(d)` alongside `Articles 14` and `16`, holding that paying unequal wages to employees performing identical duties amounts to irrational discrimination. Parliament operationalised it through the *Equal Remuneration Act, 1976*, now subsumed into the **Code on Wages, 2019** (enforced 21 November 2025).
- **`Article 39(e)` — Protection of Workers' Health and Children's Tender Age:** Directs *"that the health and strength of workers, men and women, and the tender age of children are not abused and that citizens are not forced by economic necessity to enter avocations unsuited to their age or strength."* Paired with Fundamental Rights `Articles 23` and `24`, this clause grounds workplace safety norms (Factories Act 1948 → **OSH Code, 2020**), bans on hazardous child labour, and bonded-labour rehabilitation.
- **`Article 39(f)` — Healthy Development of Children (`Substituted by 42nd Amendment, 1976`):** Originally, the 1950 text of `Article 39(f)` simply stated *"that childhood and youth are protected against exploitation and against moral and material abandonment."* The **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`** recast and expanded `39(f)` into its current positive welfare form: *"that **children are given opportunities and facilities to develop in a healthy manner and in conditions of freedom and dignity** and that childhood and youth are protected against exploitation and against moral and material abandonment."* This expanded directive underpins the *Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015*, the *POCSO Act, 2012*, and the *National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR)*.

#### Article 39A: Equal Justice and Free Legal Aid (42nd Amendment, 1976)

Inserted by the **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`**, `Article 39A` directs that *"The State shall secure that the operation of the legal system promotes justice, on a basis of equal opportunity, and shall, in particular, provide **free legal aid**, by suitable legislation or schemes or in any other way, to ensure that opportunities for securing justice are not denied to any citizen by reason of economic or other disabilities."*

In a country where litigation costs and prolonged trials can effectively lock poor citizens out of courtrooms, `Article 39A` transformed access to justice from a privilege into a State obligation. Even before Parliament enacted the **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** (creating **NALSA**, State and District Legal Services Authorities, and statutory **Lok Adalats**), the Supreme Court in ***M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra* (1978)** and ***Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar* (1979)** read `Article 39A` directly into `Article 21`, declaring free legal aid to indigent accused persons an essential ingredient of "reasonable, fair, and just" procedure.

#### Article 41: Right to Work, Education, and Public Assistance

`Article 41` mandates that *"The State shall, **within the limits of its economic capacity and development**, make effective provision for securing the **right to work**, to **education** and to **public assistance** in cases of **unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement**, and in other cases of undeserved want."*

Pay close attention to the unique qualifying phrase—**"within the limits of its economic capacity and development."** Because guaranteeing employment and universal social security entails massive fiscal outlays, the framers deliberately calibrated `Article 41` to scale progressively as India's GDP and tax base expanded. Over time, `Article 41` became the constitutional bedrock for the *National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP)* (old-age, widow, and disability pensions), the *Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016*, and rural statutory work guarantees—first under **MGNREGA (2005)** (100 days) and, effective **1 July 2026**, under the **Viksit Bharat–Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) (VB-G RAM G) Act, 2025** (125 days).

#### Article 42: Just and Humane Conditions of Work & Maternity Relief

`Article 42` directs the State to *"make provision for securing **just and humane conditions of work** and for **maternity relief**."* While `Article 39(e)` protects workers from abusive or unsuitable avocations, `Article 42` compels positive labour standards—regulated working hours, clean ventilation, crèche facilities, occupational health safeguards, and paid maternity protection so that biological motherhood never penalises women's economic participation. Parliament enacted the *Maternity Benefit Act, 1961* (significantly strengthened by the **2017 Amendment** raising paid maternity leave from **12 weeks to 26 weeks** for the first two children and mandating crèches in establishments with 50+ employees), now integrated into the **Code on Social Security, 2020** (enforced November 2025), alongside conditional cash transfers like *Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana (PMMVY)*.

#### Article 43 (Socialistic Limb): Living Wage & Decent Standard of Life

`Article 43` is a composite directive combining a **Socialistic** labour vision with a **Gandhian** rural-industry mandate. Its Socialistic limb requires the State to *"endeavour to secure, by suitable legislation or economic organisation or in any other way, to all workers, agricultural, industrial or otherwise, work, a **living wage**, conditions of work ensuring a decent standard of life and full enjoyment of leisure and social and cultural opportunities."*

In Indian labour jurisprudence—crystallised by the **Committee on Fair Wages (1948)** and endorsed by the Supreme Court in ***Express Newspaper (P) Ltd. v. Union of India* (1958)**—UPSC aspirants must distinguish clearly between three wage tiers:
- **Minimum Wage:** The bare irreducible floor needed not merely for physical subsistence, but also for preserving a worker's efficiency (basic nutrition, shelter, clothing, minimal education/medical needs), payable irrespective of an employer's capacity to pay (statutory baseline under the *Code on Wages, 2019* and *PUDR*, 1982).
- **Fair Wage:** Lies above the minimum wage and below the living wage, determined by the productivity of labour, prevailing regional rates, and the industry's paying capacity.
- **Living Wage (`Article 43` ideal):** The highest constitutional aspiration—providing not only bare essentials, but frugal comfort, children's education, healthcare, old-age insurance, and social and cultural leisure.

#### Article 43A: Workers' Participation in Management of Industries (42nd Amendment, 1976)

Inserted by the **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`**, `Article 43A` instructs that *"The State shall take steps, by suitable legislation or in any other way, to secure the **participation of workers in the management** of undertakings, establishments or other organisations engaged in any industry."* Grounded in industrial democracy—treating workers as co-partners in production rather than mere cogs—it has been partially implemented through Works Committees and Joint Management Councils under the *Industrial Disputes Act, 1947* (now subsumed into the **Industrial Relations Code, 2020**) and worker-director schemes in select public sector enterprises and nationalised banks, though private-sector adoption remains limited.

#### Article 47 (Socialistic Limb): Nutrition, Standard of Living & Public Health

The opening clause of `Article 47` carries unusually forceful constitutional language: *"The State shall regard the **raising of the level of nutrition** and the **standard of living** of its people and the **improvement of public health** as among its **primary duties**..."* Notice that `Article 47` is the **only Directive Principle** that elevates its mandate to a **"primary duty"** of the State. It anchors India's entire public-health and food-security architecture: the *National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013*, *Poshan Abhiyaan* (National Nutrition Mission), *PM POSHAN* (Mid-Day Meal scheme), *National Health Mission*, *Ayushman Bharat (PM-JAY & Ayushman Arogya Mandirs)*, universal immunisation (*Mission Indradhanush*), and judicial recognition of the **Right to Health** under `Article 21` (*Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity*, 1996).

### Gandhian Principles (Articles 40, 43, 43B, 46, 47, 48)

Although Mahatma Gandhi did not sit in the Constituent Assembly—and Dr. Ambedkar sharply criticised romanticising caste-ridden Indian villages during the November 1948 debates—Gandhian members (such as K. Santhanam, Shriman Narayan Agarwal, and Mahavir Tyagi) ensured that six core tenets of Gandhi's Constructive Programme entered Part IV: decentralised *Gram Swaraj*, rural cottage enterprise, co-operative self-help, uplift of depressed classes, temperance, and protection of cattle wealth.

#### Article 40: Organisation of Village Panchayats

`Article 40` directs that *"The State shall take steps to organise **village panchayats** and endow them with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as **units of self-government**."* Initially omitted from the first Draft Constitution—prompting Ambedkar's famously blunt remark that Indian villages were "a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness and communalism"—`Article 40` was inserted on **22 November 1948** following an amendment moved by **K. Santhanam** that Ambedkar graciously accepted.

For four decades, panchayats remained irregular, underfunded, and frequently superseded by State governments despite recommendations from the **Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957)**, **Ashok Mehta Committee (1978)**, **G.V.K. Rao Committee (1985)**, and **L.M. Singhvi Committee (1986)**. Finally, the **`73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992`** (effective 24 April 1993) elevated `Article 40` into a permanent, justiciable third tier of Indian federalism—inserting **Part IX (`Articles 243` to `243O`)** and the **Eleventh Schedule** (29 functional matters), followed by the **PESA Act, 1996** extending village self-rule to Fifth Schedule tribal areas.

#### Article 43 (Gandhian Limb): Promotion of Cottage Industries

Complementing its Socialistic living-wage clause, the second limb of `Article 43` instructs that *"in particular, the State shall endeavour to promote **cottage industries** on an **individual or co-operative basis in rural areas**."* Reflecting Gandhi's vision of *khadi*, *charkha*, and decentralised rural employment to halt distress migration to cities, this directive led to the establishment of the **Khadi and Village Industries Commission (KVIC)** (statutory body under the 1956 Act), All-India Handloom Board, All-India Handicrafts Board, Coir Board, Central Silk Board, and contemporary schemes such as *PM Vishwakarma* (2023) supporting traditional rural artisans.

#### Article 43B: Promotion of Co-operative Societies (97th Amendment, 2011)

Inserted by the **`97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011`** (effective 15 February 2012), `Article 43B` is the most recent addition to Part IV. It directs that *"The State shall endeavour to promote **voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management** of **co-operative societies**."* Alongside inserting `Article 43B`, the 97th Amendment simultaneously upgraded the right to form co-operative societies into a Fundamental Right under `Article 19(1)(c)` and inserted **Part IXB (`Articles 243ZH–243ZT`)** laying down uniform governance rules for co-operatives.

Crucially for Prelims accuracy, in ***Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah* (20 July 2021)**, a three-judge Supreme Court bench (2–1 on severability) struck down **Part IXB** insofar as it applied to **State co-operative societies** because "co-operative societies" is an exclusive State List subject (Entry 32, List II) and Parliament had failed to secure ratification by half the State Legislatures under the proviso to `Article 368(2)`. However, the Court **upheld Part IXB for multi-State co-operative societies**, and—most importantly—left **`Article 19(1)(c)` and `Article 43B` completely untouched and valid**! Thus, `Article 43B` continues in full constitutional force, guiding initiatives such as the Union **Ministry of Cooperation** (created July 2021), PACS computerisation, and the *Multi-State Co-operative Societies (Amendment) Act, 2023*.

#### Article 46: Educational and Economic Uplift of SCs, STs, and Weaker Sections

`Article 46` commands that *"The State shall promote with special care the **educational and economic interests of the weaker sections** of the people, and, in particular, of the **Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes**, and shall protect them from **social injustice and all forms of exploitation**."*

Note two vital dimensions of `Article 46`. First, it extends beyond SCs and STs to **"weaker sections"** generally—providing the explicit constitutional rationale relied upon by Parliament when enacting the **`103rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019`** (10% Economically Weaker Sections [EWS] reservation under `Articles 15(6)` and `16(6)`), subsequently upheld (3–2) by a Constitution Bench in ***Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India* (2022)**! Second, `Article 46` works in tandem with `Articles 15(4)`, `16(4)`, `17`, `330–342A`, and protective statutes including the *Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955*, the *SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989*, the *Forest Rights Act, 2006*, and the *National Commissions for SCs, STs, and Backward Classes* (`Articles 338, 338A, 338B`).

#### Article 47 (Gandhian Limb): Prohibition of Intoxicating Drinks and Drugs

The second clause of `Article 47` expresses Mahatma Gandhi's lifelong campaign for temperance: *"in particular, the State shall endeavour to bring about **prohibition** of the consumption **except for medicinal purposes** of intoxicating drinks and of drugs which are injurious to health."*

In ***State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara* (1951)** and ***Cooverjee B. Bharucha* (1954)**, the Supreme Court invoked `Article 47` to hold that citizens have **no Fundamental Right to trade in liquor** (*res extra commercium*—trade inherently pernicious to public welfare), validating strict State excise control and total prohibition. Yet implementation across India illustrates a classic **fiscal-versus-welfare dilemma**: because alcohol excise constitutes one of the largest independent revenue sources left to States after GST, only **Gujarat, Bihar, Nagaland, and Mizoram** (alongside Lakshadweep UT) currently enforce comprehensive prohibition, while States such as Andhra Pradesh, Haryana, and Tamil Nadu historically experimented with and reversed prohibition owing to illicit hooch tragedies, bootlegging networks, and steep revenue losses.

#### Article 48 (Gandhian Limb): Preservation of Breeds & Prohibition of Cow Slaughter

The second clause of `Article 48` instructs that the State shall take steps *"for preserving and improving the breeds, and **prohibiting the slaughter, of cows and calves and other milch and draught cattle**."* Remarkably, this clause was framed in the Constituent Assembly strictly in **economic and agrarian terms**—not religious dogma—recognising cattle as the backbone of Indian dairy nutrition and plough agriculture (though cultural sentiments strongly backed its passage).

The Supreme Court's doctrinal trajectory on `Article 48` offers a textbook example of how DPSPs reshape Fundamental Rights adjudication:
- In ***Mohd. Hanif Quareshi v. State of Bihar* (1958, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Court held that while banning the slaughter of cows, calves, and *useful* milch/draught cattle was reasonable under `Article 19(1)(g)`, a **total ban** extending to aged, dry, or economically "useless" bulls and bullocks imposed an unreasonable restriction on butchers' right to trade.
- Nearly half a century later, in ***State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat* (2005, 7-Judge Bench, 6–1)**, the Supreme Court **overruled *Quareshi*** on this point and upheld Gujarat's **total ban on the slaughter of bulls and bullocks of all ages**. Reading `Article 48` jointly with **`Article 48A`** (environment) and **`Article 51A(g)`** (compassion for living creatures), the majority reasoned that even aged cattle continue contributing manure, biogas, and ecological value in an agrarian economy, and that a total ban therefore constituted a reasonable restriction in public interest under `Article 19(6)`.

### Liberal-Intellectual Principles (Articles 44, 45, 48, 48A, 49, 50, 51)

Rooted in Enlightenment constitutionalism, secular equality, scientific rationalism, ecological stewardship, and international law, the Liberal-Intellectual Principles guide India toward a modern, rule-bound, outward-looking republic.

#### Article 44: Uniform Civil Code (UCC) — Deep Constitutional & Contemporary Analysis

`Article 44` is brief yet arguably the most politically and constitutionally debated single sentence in Part IV: *"The State shall endeavour to secure for the citizens a **uniform civil code throughout the territory of India**."*

**Meaning & Present Legal Landscape:** India already possesses uniform national laws in virtually all **public and commercial domains**—criminal law (*Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023*, *BNSS, 2023*, *BSA, 2023*), contract law, transfer of property, negotiable instruments, and civil procedure. What remains fragmented along religious and customary lines are **personal laws** governing **marriage, divorce, maintenance, adoption, guardianship, and intestate/testamentary succession**. Hindus (legally including Sikhs, Jains, and Buddhists under the codified Hindu Code Acts of 1955–56), Muslims (*Shariat Application Act, 1937*; *Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939*; *Muslim Women [Protection of Rights on Marriage] Act, 2019*), Christians (*Indian Divorce Act, 1869*; *Indian Succession Act, 1925*), Parsis (*Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act, 1936*), and numerous tribal communities under customary practices are governed by separate statutes or uncodified rules—alongside a secular optional pathway in the **Special Marriage Act, 1954**. A Uniform Civil Code seeks to replace religion-based personal laws with a single, gender-just, secular civil framework applicable equally to all citizens irrespective of faith.

**Constituent Assembly Debates (23 November 1948):** In the Assembly, minority representatives (notably Mohamad Ismail, Naziruddin Ahmad, Mahboob Ali Baig, and Pocker Sahib) moved amendments seeking to exempt personal laws from Draft Article 35 (now `Article 44`), arguing that personal law was integral to religious practice under `Article 25`. Rejecting these amendments, **K.M. Munshi**, **Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar**, and **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** argued that personal laws governing succession and marriage are secular legal matters rather than core religious rituals, and that modern nation-building requires detaching civil status from religious identity. Crucially, however, Ambedkar offered a statesmanlike assurance to calm minority anxieties: **`Article 44` merely directs the State to "endeavour" to secure a UCC; Parliament was not bound to enforce it overnight or coercively**, and a future Parliament might even begin by making the code **voluntary** until society achieved broad consensus.

**The Goa Precedent — Strengths and Caveats:** Often cited as India's lone historical example of a uniform code—and praised by the Supreme Court as a "shining example" in ***Jose Paulo Coutinho v. Maria Luiza Valentina Pereira* (2019)**—**Goa** retains the **Portuguese Civil Code of 1867**, kept in force after Goa's liberation by Parliament's *Goa, Daman and Diu Administration Act, 1962*. It establishes compulsory civil registration of marriages, equal community-of-property rights for spouses (50% marital asset ownership), and mandatory equal inheritance shares for sons and daughters. Yet rigorous Mains analysis notes that **even Goa's code is not completely uniform**: under the *Code of Usages and Customs of Gentile Hindus of Goa (1880)*, Goan Hindu men were historically permitted limited bigamy under specified conditions (if a wife failed to deliver a child by age 25 or a male child by age 30), Catholic church marriages enjoy special canonical annulment rules before ecclesiastical tribunals, and adoption norms vary slightly across communities.

**Judicial Trajectory and Law Commission Reports:**
- ***Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum* (1985):** Upholding a divorced Muslim woman's right to secular maintenance under Section 125 CrPC, CJI Y.V. Chandrachud famously lamented that **`Article 44` had remained a "dead letter"** and urged the State to summon political courage to enact a common civil code to foster national integration.
- ***Sarla Mudgal v. Union of India* (1995) & *Lily Thomas* (2000):** Addressing Hindu men converting to Islam solely to contract bigamous second marriages without divorcing their first wives, the Court requested the Union Government to take a "fresh look" at `Article 44` (clarifying later in *Lily Thomas* that the Court had not issued a binding *mandamus* to legislate).
- ***John Vallamattom v. Union of India* (2003):** Striking down a discriminatory provision in the Indian Succession Act restricting Christian charitable bequests, CJI V.N. Khare reiterated regret over the non-implementation of `Article 44`.
- ***Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017):** Invalidated instant triple talaq (*talaq-e-biddat*) on grounds of manifest arbitrariness under `Article 14`, reinforcing incremental gender-just reform.
- **21st Law Commission of India (August 2018 — *Consultation Paper on Reform of Family Law*):** Chaired by Justice B.S. Chauhan, the 21st Law Commission took a nuanced, landmark position—concluding that a nationwide Uniform Civil Code was **"neither necessary nor desirable at this stage."** Observing that cultural diversity must not be compromised in the pursuit of mere uniformity, it advocated **reforming and codifying each personal law from within** to eradicate gender discrimination—prioritising *"equality within communities"* before seeking *"uniformity between communities."*
- **22nd Law Commission of India (June 2023):** Chaired by Justice Ritu Raj Awasthi, the 22nd Commission reopened the issue by inviting fresh public and religious-organisation views on a UCC, noting that more than three years had elapsed since the 2018 consultation paper.

> [!news]
> **State-Led Uniform Civil Codes (2025–2026): Uttarakhand & Gujarat**
> - **Uttarakhand — First State in Independent India to Enforce a UCC (27 January 2025):** Passed by the Uttarakhand Legislative Assembly on **7 February 2024** (drafted by a committee chaired by retired SC Justice Ranjana Prakash Desai), **`The Uniform Civil Code, Uttarakhand, 2024`** (Act No. 3 of 2024) received Presidential assent on **11 March 2024** (under `Article 254(2)` because family law lies in Entry 5, Concurrent List) and **came into force on 27 January 2025** alongside the *Uttarakhand UCC Rules, 2025* (further amended in 2026). It establishes uniform rules on **marriage and divorce** (minimum age 21 for men, 18 for women; strict monogamy; banning *halala*, *iddat*, and unilateral divorce; mandatory registration), **equal intestate succession** across genders and religions, and **mandatory registration of live-in relationships** (conferring legitimacy and maintenance rights on children born to live-in couples, while attracting criticism on `Article 21` privacy grounds). Crucially, it contains **no chapter on adoption** and **expressly exempts Scheduled Tribes** notified under `Article 342` (plus customary communities protected under Part XXI).
> - **Gujarat & Assam Momentum (2026):** Following Uttarakhand, the Gujarat Legislative Assembly passed the **`Gujarat Uniform Civil Code, 2026`** (Act No. 15 of 2026) on **24 March 2026**, which received Presidential assent on **17 September 2026** and Gazette publication on **1 October 2026** (awaiting state commencement notification and rules as of late 2026). The Assam Legislative Assembly similarly passed a state UCC Bill in May 2026. This **state-by-state trajectory** raises a fresh Mains constitutional question: if different States enact diverging "uniform" codes, does India achieve `Article 44`'s promise of a code *"throughout the territory of India,"* or create a new mosaic of interstate legal fragmentation?

> [!debate]
> **Evaluating a Uniform Civil Code (Article 44): Gender Justice vs Cultural Pluralism**
> - **Case FOR a Uniform Civil Code:**
>   1. **Gender Equality & Constitutional Morality (`Arts. 14, 15, 21`):** Eliminates patriarchal inequities embedded across personal laws—polygamy, unilateral divorce, unequal inheritance shares, restrictive guardianship, and discriminatory coparcenary/maintenance gaps.
>   2. **Secular Citizenship & National Integration:** Separates civil status from religious affiliation, fulfilling Ambedkar's and *Shah Bano*'s vision of equal civic belonging.
>   3. **Legal Simplification & Judicial Efficiency:** Replaces an overlapping maze of community-specific statutes and uncodified customs with a single, transparent code.
> - **Case for CAUTION / Incremental Reform:**
>   1. **Freedom of Religion & Cultural Diversity (`Arts. 25, 26, 29`):** Minority communities apprehend that "uniformity" could translate in practice into majoritarian homogenisation erasing distinct cultural identities.
>   2. **Tribal & Asymmetric Federal Protections:** **`Article 371A`** (Nagaland), **`Article 371G`** (Mizoram), and the **Fifth and Sixth Schedules** constitutionally insulate customary tribal family and land laws—which is why even Uttarakhand's UCC exempted Scheduled Tribes!
>   3. **21st Law Commission Warning:** Uniformity does not automatically guarantee equity; unhurried internal reform within personal laws (like the 2005 Hindu Succession Amendment granting daughters coparcenary rights) often commands deeper social legitimacy than abrupt top-down imposition.
> - **Balanced Mains Way Forward:** Frame UCC not as a majoritarian project but as a **gender-just, rights-centric "Common Family Law"**—built through wide multi-stakeholder consensus, codifying non-discriminatory minimum standards (age of marriage, monogamy, equitable maintenance, equal succession, no-fault divorce), preserving sensitive tribal customary safeguards under Part XXI/Sixth Schedule, and considering an **opt-in / phased parliamentary model** (echoing Ambedkar's 1948 suggestion) rather than fragmented state statutes.

#### Article 45: Early Childhood Care and Education (Recast by 86th Amendment, 2002)

`Article 45` stands as the single greatest triumph of converting a non-justiciable Directive Principle into an enforceable Fundamental Right—while refashioning Part IV to pioneer the next frontier of human development:
- **Original 1950 Text:** Directed the State to endeavour to provide, within a period of **ten years** from the commencement of the Constitution (i.e., by 1960), **free and compulsory education for all children until they complete the age of fourteen years**. Notably, this was the **ONLY Directive Principle originally carrying an explicit time-limit** (10 years)—a sharp Prelims trivia point!
- **Judicial Catalyst:** In ***Mohini Jain v. State of Karnataka* (1992)** and the landmark 5-judge bench verdict in ***Unni Krishnan, J.P. v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (1993)**, the Supreme Court read `Article 45` into `Article 21`, declaring free education up to age **14** an enforceable Fundamental Right.
- **The `86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002` Triad:** Acting on *Unni Krishnan*, Parliament executed a three-part constitutional overhaul across Parts III, IV, and IVA:
  1. Inserted **`Article 21A`** in Part III making free and compulsory education for children aged **6 to 14 years** a Fundamental Right (operationalised via the *RTE Act, 2009*);
  2. **Substituted `Article 45`** in Part IV so that it now directs: *"The State shall endeavour to provide **early childhood care and education** for all children until they complete the age of **six years**"* (the 0–6 pre-school foundational stage!);
  3. Inserted **`Article 51A(k)`** in Part IVA making it a Fundamental Duty of parents/guardians to provide educational opportunities to children aged **6 to 14 years**.

Today, recast `Article 45` drives India's **Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS)** (*Anganwadi* network under *Mission Saksham Anganwadi & Poshan 2.0*) and the **National Education Policy (NEP) 2020**, which formally integrates ages **3–6** into the **5+3+3+4** Foundational Stage (*Balvatika*)—bridging nutrition, cognitive stimulation, and school readiness before Class 1.

```flow
# The 86th Constitutional Amendment (2002) Education Triad
Original Article 45 (1950) | Non-justiciable DPSP: free & compulsory education up to age 14 within 10 years
Unni Krishnan Case (1993) | Supreme Court read Art. 45 into Art. 21, making 0–14 education a Fundamental Right
Part III: Article 21A | Fundamental Right: Free & compulsory education (Ages 6–14; RTE Act 2009) || Part IV: Recast Article 45 | Directive Principle: Early Childhood Care & Education — ECCE (Ages 0–6; ICDS & NEP 2020) || Part IVA: Article 51A(k) | Fundamental Duty: Parents/guardians to provide education opportunities (Ages 6–14)
```

#### Article 48 (Liberal-Intellectual Limb): Scientific Agriculture and Animal Husbandry

The opening clause of `Article 48` instructs the State to *"endeavour to organise **agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines**."* Grounded in food sovereignty and rural modernisation, this directive propelled India's transformation from a famine-prone, PL-480 food-aid recipient into a net agricultural exporter through the **Green Revolution**, **White Revolution (Operation Flood)**, the **Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR)** and State Agricultural Universities, *Soil Health Cards*, *Rashtriya Gokul Mission*, precision micro-irrigation (*PM Krishi Sinchayee Yojana*), and climate-resilient digital agriculture missions.

#### Article 48A: Environmental Protection, Forests, and Wildlife (42nd Amendment, 1976)

Inserted by the **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`** in the wake of the **1972 United Nations Stockholm Conference on the Human Environment** (where Prime Minister Indira Gandhi delivered her celebrated "poverty is the greatest polluter" address), `Article 48A` directs: *"The State shall endeavour to **protect and improve the environment** and to **safeguard the forests and wild life** of the country."*

Simultaneously, the 42nd Amendment shifted **"Forests"** and **"Protection of wild animals and birds"** from the State List to the **Concurrent List** (Entries 17A and 17B) and added citizens' ecological duty under **`Article 51A(g)`**, making India one of the earliest countries in the world to constitutionalise environmental protection. Over four decades, the Supreme Court fused `Article 48A` and `Article 51A(g)` with `Article 21` to forge global-benchmark environmental jurisprudence: **Polluter Pays Principle**, **Precautionary Principle**, **Public Trust Doctrine** (*M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath*, 1997), **Inter-Generational Equity**, the broad dictionary definition of "forest" (*T.N. Godavarman*, 1996—reaffirmed in *Ashok Kumar Sharma*, Feb 2024), and—in a historic **21 March 2024** milestone—***M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India***, recognising a distinct Fundamental Right to be **free from the adverse effects of climate change** under `Articles 21` and `14` read with `48A` and `51A(g)`!

#### Article 49: Protection of Monuments and Heritage of National Importance

`Article 49` declares: *"It shall be the **obligation** of the State to protect every **monument or place or object of artistic or historic interest**, declared by or under law made by Parliament to be of **national importance**, from spoliation, disfigurement, destruction, removal, disposal or export, as the case may be."*

Notice two sharp UPSC Prelims subtleties in `Article 49`:
1. Unlike most DPSPs that say "the State shall endeavour," `Article 49` uses the stronger word **"obligation"**—and its scope is specifically tied to monuments/objects declared to be of **national importance** by or under a **Parliamentary law** (operationalised via the **Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains [AMASR] Act, 1958** administered by the Archaeological Survey of India, plus the *Antiquities and Art Treasures Act, 1972*).
2. The **`7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956`** made a minor technical amendment to `Article 49` (substituting *"declared by Parliament by law"* with *"declared by or under law made by Parliament"*) so Parliament could delegate site-by-site notification to the executive rather than passing a fresh Act for every monument.
3. **Classic Prelims Contrast:** Do NOT confuse **`Article 49`** (DPSP — *State obligation* to protect monuments/places/objects of *national importance*) with **`Article 51A(f)`** (Fundamental Duty — *citizen's duty* to value and preserve the rich heritage of our *composite culture*).

#### Article 50: Separation of Judiciary from Executive

`Article 50` commands: *"The State shall take steps to **separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State**."*

During British colonial rule, the **District Collector / District Magistrate** simultaneously collected revenue, commanded the police, AND tried criminal cases as a magistrate—violating Montesquieu's cardinal rule of judicial independence because the prosecutor and the judge sat in the same chair. While the Constitution separated the Supreme Court and High Courts from executive control immediately in 1950, district-level magistracy separation required comprehensive criminal procedure reform. Parliament fulfilled `Article 50` nationwide through the **`Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC), 1973`** (effective 1 April 1974), and carried the identical separation seamlessly into the **`Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023`** (effective **1 July 2024**, Sections `6` and `14–17`):
- **Judicial Magistrates** (First and Second Class, under Chief Judicial Magistrates and High Courts via `Article 235`) exercise all judicial trial and evidence-appreciation powers.
- **Executive Magistrates** (District Magistrate, Additional DM, SDM, under State Governments) are strictly restricted to administrative and preventive law-and-order powers (such as unlawful assembly orders under BNSS s. 163 / former CrPC s. 144).

In modern constitutional litigation, the Supreme Court has repeatedly invoked `Article 50` alongside the Basic Structure principle of **judicial independence** to curb executive domination over quasi-judicial **Tribunals** (*Madras Bar Association* cases, 2014–2021) and reject executive primacy in judicial appointments (*NJAC Case*, 2015).

#### Article 51: Promotion of International Peace and Security

Closing Part IV, **`Article 51`** is the sole constitutional Article articulating the foundational principles of **India's foreign policy**. Its **four clauses `(a)` to `(d)`** direct the State to endeavour to:
- **`51(a)`:** Promote **international peace and security**;
- **`51(b)`:** Maintain **just and honourable relations between nations**;
- **`51(c)`:** Foster respect for **international law and treaty obligations** in the dealings of organised peoples with one another; and
- **`51(d)`:** Encourage settlement of **international disputes by arbitration**.

**Important Constitutional Mechanics of `Article 51(c)`:** India follows the **dualist tradition** of international law (read alongside `Article 253` and Entry 14 of the Union List). An international treaty ratified by the Union executive does **not** automatically become enforceable domestic Indian law unless Parliament enacts implementing legislation (*Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel*, 1969; *Jolly George Varghese*, 1980). However, guided by `Article 51(c)`, the Supreme Court consistently harmonises domestic law with ratified international conventions wherever no conflicting Indian statute exists—most famously reading the **CEDAW Convention** into `Articles 14, 15, 19, and 21` to frame workplace sexual-harassment guidelines in ***Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997)**, and invoking the **Paris Agreement** alongside `Articles 48A` and `51A(g)` in ***M.K. Ranjitsinh* (2024)**.

Four major Constitutional Amendments reshaped the substantive content of Part IV after 1950 (plus one technical phrasing adjustment in 1956). UPSC Prelims routinely scrambles these Amendment numbers and Article letters—master the exact sequence below:

```timeline

1956 | 7th Constitutional Amendment Act | Minor technical refinement to Article 49 ('declared by or under law made by Parliament') — also inserted Directive outside Part IV: Article 350A (mother-tongue primary instruction).
1976 | 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act | Largest expansion of Part IV (4 changes): substituted & expanded Art. 39(f) (healthy child development in freedom & dignity), and inserted three brand-new Articles — Art. 39A (free legal aid), Art. 43A (workers' participation in industrial management), and Art. 48A (environment, forests & wildlife).

And `Article 43B` was inserted by the **`97th Amendment (2011)`**!
> 2. **Original vs Added Environment/Cattle Articles:** **`Article 48`** (modern agriculture + cow/calf slaughter ban) was in the **original 1950 Constitution**; **`Article 48A`** (environment, forests & wildlife) was inserted by the **`42nd Amendment (1976)`**.
> 3. **Triple Dual-Category Articles:** **`Articles 43`, `47`, and `48`** each belong to **two** ideological streams (`43` = Socialistic + Gandhian; `47` = Socialistic + Gandhian; `48` = Liberal-Intellectual + Gandhian).
> 4. **Classic UPSC PYQ Matches:**
>    - **Separation of Judiciary from Executive (UPSC Prelims 2020):** **`Article 50` (DPSP — Liberal-Intellectual)**—NOT Preamble, NOT Seventh Schedule, NOT Fundamental Duties.
>    - **Promotion of International Peace & Security (UPSC Prelims 2014 & 2017):** **`Article 51` (DPSP — Liberal-Intellectual)**—NOT Preamble, NOT Fundamental Duties, NOT Ninth Schedule.
>    - **Organising Village Panchayats (UPSC Prelims 2012/2015):** **`Article 40` (Gandhian DPSP)**—whereas Uniform Civil Code (`Art. 44`) is Liberal-Intellectual, NOT Gandhian!
>    - **Does the Constitution classify DPSPs into Socialistic, Gandhian, and Liberal-Intellectual?** **NO!** That classification is purely scholarly/analytical.
> 5. **Did the 97th Amendment Judgment (*Rajendra N. Shah*, 2021) strike down `Article 43B`?** **NO!** It invalidated Part IXB only for *State* co-operative societies (preserving multi-State co-ops) while leaving `Article 19(1)(c)` and **`Article 43B`** completely valid.

## 3. Conflict & Harmony Between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

If Parts III and IV together form the "conscience of the Constitution," their relationship across independent India's first three decades was anything but tranquil. Structural friction was baked into the constitutional design from day one: **Fundamental Rights** are enforceable by courts and protect individual liberty, formal equality, and (until 1978) private property against State interference, whereas **Directive Principles** command the State to redistribute land, break economic concentration, reserve opportunities for disadvantaged groups, and regulate private commerce for collective welfare. Whenever Parliament or a State Legislature enacted agrarian ceilings, caste-based reservations, or nationalisation statutes to fulfil Part IV, affected citizens rushed to the Supreme Court invoking Part III.

Over seventy-five years—spanning five distinct constitutional phases, six Constitutional Amendments, and some of the largest benches in Indian judicial history—this adversarial tug-of-war matured into a settled doctrine of **constitutional harmony and balance**, culminating most recently in the landmark nine-judge ruling in ***Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (5 November 2024)**.

### Doctrinal Evolution: From Primacy of Rights to Constitutional Harmony (1951–2024)

#### Phase 1: Strict Subordination of Directive Principles (1951–1957)

The very first constitutional collision occurred barely a year after the Republic was born. In ***State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* (1951)**, the Madras Government defended its caste- and religion-based Communal Government Order reserving seats in State medical and engineering colleges by invoking **`Article 46`** (educational uplift of weaker sections). A seven-judge Supreme Court bench unanimously struck down the reservation order for violating `Article 29(2)` (and `Article 15(1)`), laying down an uncompromising rule of hierarchy: because Directive Principles are expressly non-justiciable under `Article 37`, they **cannot override Fundamental Rights** and must *"conform to and run as subsidiary to the Chapter of Fundamental Rights."*

Crucially, however, the Court added an institutional safety valve: Parliament retained constituent power under `Article 368` to **amend Fundamental Rights** to give effect to Directive Principles. Parliament immediately acted upon that cue—launching a wave of constitutional amendments:
- **`1st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951`:** Inserted **`Article 15(4)`** (enabling educational/social reservations for SCs, STs, and socially and educationally backward classes, directly reversing *Champakam*), and added **`Articles 31A`, `31B`, and the Ninth Schedule** to insulate agrarian and zamindari-abolition reforms from `Articles 14, 19`, and `31`.
- **`4th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1955`:** Shielded adequacy of property compensation from judicial scrutiny and expanded `Article 31A` to cover corporate takeovers and mining leases (answering *Bella Banerjee*, 1954).
- **`17th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1964`:** Widened the definition of "estate" under `Article 31A` to include *ryotwari* land ceilings and placed 44 additional State land-reform laws in the Ninth Schedule.

#### Phase 2: Harmonious Construction (1958–1966)

Recognising that mechanical subordination risked paralysing legitimate welfare legislation, the Supreme Court soon softened *Champakam*'s rigid edge. In ***In Re: The Kerala Education Bill, 1957* (1958)**, Chief Justice S.R. Das enunciated the **Doctrine of Harmonious Construction**: while Fundamental Rights remain legally enforceable and cannot be abrogated by a Directive Principle, courts must not ignore Part IV when interpreting Part III; instead, they must read both chapters harmoniously so that **maximum effect is given to both** wherever possible. That same year, in ***Mohd. Hanif Quareshi v. State of Bihar* (1958)**, the Court held that a harmonious interpretation means the State should implement DPSPs in ways that do not destroy Fundamental Rights—upholding bans on cow slaughter while striking down bans on aged, non-milch bulls under `Article 19(1)(g)` (a specific outcome later reversed in *Mirzapur Moti Kureshi*, 2005).

#### Phase 3: Transcendental Rights vs the Article 31C Counter-Offensive (1967–1972)

This fragile equilibrium shattered in ***I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab* (1967, 11-Judge Bench, 6–5)**. Overruling *Shankari Prasad* (1951) and *Sajjan Singh* (1965), Chief Justice K. Subba Rao declared that Fundamental Rights occupy a **"transcendental and immutable"** position, holding that a constitutional amendment under `Article 368` is "law" under `Article 13(2)` and therefore **Parliament CANNOT amend or abridge any Fundamental Right even to implement a Directive Principle**. When the Court followed *Golaknath* by striking down Bank Nationalisation (*R.C. Cooper*, 1970) and Privy Purse abolition (*Madhav Rao Scindia*, 1970), the confrontation between Parliament and the judiciary reached crisis proportions.

Armed with a two-thirds parliamentary majority after the 1971 election, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi's government enacted two sweeping amendments:
- **`24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971`:** Inserted `Article 13(4)` and recast `Article 368` to affirm expressly that Parliament possesses constituent power to amend *any* part of the Constitution—including Fundamental Rights—and made Presidential assent to constitutional amendment bills mandatory.
- **`25th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971`:** Inserted **`Article 31C`** into Part III, establishing for the first time an explicit constitutional primacy for selected Directive Principles over core Fundamental Rights. `Article 31C` contained **two drastic clauses**:
  1. **First Limb (Substantive Safe Harbour):** No law giving effect to the socialistic Directive Principles in **`Article 39(b)`** (equitable distribution of material resources of the community) or **`Article 39(c)`** (preventing concentration of wealth and means of production) shall be deemed void on the ground that it takes away or abridges **`Article 14`, `Article 19`, or `Article 31`** (with `Article 31` later omitted by the `44th Amendment, 1978`, leaving `Articles 14` and `19`). A State law claiming this shield requires **Presidential assent**.
  2. **Second Limb (Judicial Review Ouster Clause):** No law containing a legislative declaration that it gives effect to `Article 39(b)` or `39(c)` *"shall be called in question in any court on the ground that it does not give effect to such policy."*

#### Phase 4: Basic Structure, the 42nd Amendment & the Chariot Wheels Balance (1973–2007)

In India's most consequential constitutional judgment—***Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (24 April 1973, 13-Judge Bench, 7–6)**—the Supreme Court forged a historic compromise. It **overruled *Golaknath*** and upheld the `24th Amendment`, confirming Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights to advance socio-economic reform—**subject to the Basic Structure Doctrine** (Parliament cannot alter or destroy the Constitution's foundational identity). Applying this test to `Article 31C`, the Court split its verdict cleanly:
- **First Limb UPHELD:** Giving `Articles 39(b)` and `39(c)` primacy over `Articles 14` and `19` was constitutionally valid.
- **Second Limb STRUCK DOWN:** Barring courts from examining whether a statute genuinely bore a reasonable nexus with `Article 39(b)` or `39(c)` destroyed **judicial review**, an indestructible element of the Basic Structure.

During the Internal Emergency, Parliament pushed back again via **Section 4 of the `42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`**, amending `Article 31C` to expand its safe harbour from *only* `Articles 39(b)` and `39(c)` to **"all or any of the principles laid down in Part IV."** Had this expansion survived, *any* ordinary statute invoking *any* Directive Principle could have permanently extinguished equality (`Article 14`) and the six democratic freedoms (`Article 19`).

Four years later, a five-judge Constitution Bench in ***Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India* (31 July 1980, 4–1; Justice P.N. Bhagwati dissenting on `Article 31C`)** struck down Section 4 of the `42nd Amendment` as unconstitutional. Comparing Parts III and IV to the **two wheels of a chariot**—neither of which can be raised above the other without overturning the vehicle—Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud authored the canonical statement of India's constitutional balance:

> "The Indian Constitution is founded on the bed-rock of the balance between Parts III and IV. To give absolute primacy to one over the other is to disturb the harmony of the Constitution. This harmony and balance between fundamental rights and directive principles is an essential feature of the basic structure of the Constitution." — Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud, *Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India* (31 July 1980)

Shortly thereafter, ***Waman Rao v. Union of India* (1981)** confirmed that pre-24 April 1973 Ninth Schedule enactments and the unamended (`Kesavananda`-approved) `Article 31C` remained constitutionally valid, while all post-24 April 1973 additions to the Ninth Schedule faced Basic Structure scrutiny—a bright-line settlement cemented unanimously by nine judges in ***I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu* (2007)**.

Simultaneously, from **1978 onward**, the Supreme Court pioneered a **transformative positive convergence** between Parts III and IV. Abandoning the view that DPSPs were adversaries of liberty, the post-*Maneka Gandhi* judiciary actively **read Part IV directives into `Article 21` (Right to Life with Dignity)** and `Articles 14/16`—transforming free legal aid (`Art. 39A` — *M.H. Hoskot*, 1978; *Hussainara Khatoon*, 1979), equal pay for equal work (`Art. 39(d)` — *Randhir Singh*, 1982), livelihood (`Arts. 39(a), 41` — *Olga Tellis*, 1985), elementary education (original `Art. 45` — *Unni Krishnan*, 1993), public healthcare (`Art. 47` — *Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity*, 1996), and clean environment and climate-change protection (`Art. 48A` — *Subhash Kumar*, 1991; *M.C. Mehta*; ***M.K. Ranjitsinh*, 21 March 2024**) into judicially enforceable Fundamental Rights!

#### Phase 5: Modernising Constitutional Economics — Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra (5 November 2024)

Even after *Minerva Mills* (1980), two massive constitutional riddles remained unresolved for more than four decades, originating from challenges by Mumbai landlords against Chapter VIII-A of the *Maharashtra Housing and Area Development Act (MHADA), 1976* (which empowered the State to acquire dilapidated private "cessed" buildings upon the request of 70% of tenants, invoking `Article 39(b)` and `Article 31C`). Following successive references from a three-judge bench (1992) to five judges (1996) to seven judges (2002), a historic **nine-judge Constitution Bench** delivered its judgment in ***Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (5 November 2024, 2024 INSC 835)**, settling both constitutional questions decisively:

1. **Survival of Pre-42nd Article 31C — Unanimous (9–0):** Petitioners had argued that because the `42nd Amendment` had substituted the text of `Article 31C`, and *Minerva Mills* had invalidated that substituted text without explicitly reviving the old wording, `Article 31C` stood wiped off the constitutional map altogether. All nine judges unanimously **rejected** this argument: when an amending statute substituting an existing constitutional provision is struck down as void, the **doctrine of revival** operates automatically. Therefore, **unamended `Article 31C` (as upheld in *Kesavananda Bharati*, shielding laws implementing `Articles 39(b)` and `39(c)` from `Articles 14` and `19`) CONTINUES TO SURVIVE in full force**.
2. **Scope of "Material Resources of the Community" under Article 39(b) — 8–1 Majority:** The deeper ideological battle turned on whether *every* privately owned asset in India qualifies as a **"material resource of the community"** open to State acquisition and redistribution under `Article 39(b)`. In ***State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy* (1977)**, a seven-judge bench had divided: Justice Untwalia (speaking for four judges) expressed grave doubts that private property generally fell under `Article 39(b)`, whereas **Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer** (speaking for a three-judge minority) famously asserted that *all* resources—public and private alike—belong to the community for socialist distribution. Five years later, a five-judge bench in ***Sanjeev Coke Manufacturing Co. v. Bharat Coking Coal Ltd.* (1982)** erroneously adopted Justice Krishna Iyer's minority view as binding law (echoed in an *obiter* sentence in *Mafatlal Industries*, 1997).

   Writing for a **seven-judge majority** (joined on the `Article 39(b)` outcome by **Justice B.V. Nagarathna** in a separate concurring opinion, with **Justice Sudhanshu Dhulia** filing the lone dissent), **Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud** **overruled *Sanjeev Coke* (1982)** and disapproved Justice Krishna Iyer's sweeping view in *Ranganatha Reddy*. While confirming that **privately owned resources CAN theoretically fall under `Article 39(b)`**, the Court ruled firmly that **NOT EVERY privately owned property automatically constitutes a "material resource of the community."** Instead, courts must apply a **five-factor contextual inquiry** grounded in the **Public Trust Doctrine**:
   - the **nature** of the resource;
   - the **inherent characteristics** of the resource;
   - the **impact** of the resource on the **well-being of the community**;
   - the **scarcity** of the resource; and
   - the **consequences** of such a resource being **concentrated in the hands of private players**.

   Applying these parameters, the Court illustrated that privately owned **forests, ponds, wetlands, ecologically fragile zones, mineral deposits, or spectrum** naturally bear upon community welfare and fall within `Article 39(b)`, whereas ordinary personal assets or private homes do not automatically become State-distributable community property. Justice Nagarathna, while agreeing on this nuanced test, sharply objected to the majority's criticism of Justice Krishna Iyer's socialist judicial philosophy, stressing that earlier judges operated within the socio-economic realities of their era; Justice Dhulia dissented in full, warning that narrowing `Article 39(b)` could weaken the welfare State amid rising economic inequality. Echoing Dr. Ambedkar's Constituent Assembly speeches, the majority concluded that the Indian Constitution deliberately avoided locking the Republic into any rigid economic dogma—neither doctrinaire state socialism nor unfettered laissez-faire—choosing instead a flexible framework of **economic democracy**.

### Comprehensive Comparison: Fundamental Rights vs Directive Principles

Although complementary in constitutional vision, Part III and Part IV differ fundamentally in their legal design, remedies, and institutional mechanics. UPSC Prelims and Mains routinely test these exact contrasts:

## 4. Implementation, Criticism & Directives Outside Part IV

A frequent misconception among beginners is that because Part IV is non-justiciable, it has remained an unfulfilled wish list. In reality, since 1950 successive Union and State governments—guided first by the **Planning Commission (1950–2014)** across twelve Five-Year Plans and subsequently by **NITI Aayog (since 2015)** through the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) India Index and Aspirational Districts Programme—have translated the vast majority of Directive Principles into durable constitutional amendments, landmark statutes, and welfare institutions. Equally critical for UPSC Prelims is recognising the **three non-justiciable constitutional directives situated *outside* Part IV**, alongside evaluating the classic criticisms levelled against Part IV.

It expands the statutory rural household wage-employment guarantee from **100 days to 125 days**, introduces a **60-day seasonal pause** during peak agricultural sowing and harvesting periods to avoid farm-labour shortages, and shifts funding from 100% Central wage payment to a **60:40 Centre–State cost-sharing formula** (90:10 for North-Eastern/Himalayan States and UTs with legislature)—sparking active fiscal-federalism debate in GS-2/GS-3.
> 3. **Climate Right & Forest Safeguards (`Art. 48A`):** In ***Ashok Kumar Sharma v. Union of India* (19 February 2024)**, the Supreme Court directed States/UTs to continue enforcing the broad dictionary meaning of "forest" (*T.N. Godavarman*, 1996) pending final review of the *Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act, 2023* (*Van [Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan] Adhiniyam*); one month later, in ***M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India* (21 March 2024)**, it read `Articles 48A` and `51A(g)` directly into `Articles 21` and `14` to establish the Fundamental Right against the adverse impacts of climate change.

### Directives Outside Part IV (Classic Prelims Trap)

While Part IV (`Articles 36–51`) contains the Directive Principles proper, **three additional non-justiciable constitutional directives** are located in **Parts XVI and XVII**. The Supreme Court has consistently held that these extra-Part IV directives carry equal constitutional significance and interpretive weight—yet UPSC Prelims regularly sets traps asking which directives lie *inside* versus *outside* Part IV:

1. **`Article 335` (Part XVI — Claims of SCs and STs to Services and Posts):** Directs that *"The claims of the members of the **Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes** shall be taken into consideration, **consistently with the maintenance of efficiency of administration**, in the making of appointments to services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union or of a State."* Crucially, after the Supreme Court in *S. Vinod Kumar* (1996) barred relaxed qualifying standards in promotions, Parliament enacted the **`82nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2000`**, inserting a **proviso to `Article 335`** permitting the State to relax qualifying marks in any examination or lower evaluation standards for SC/ST reservations in **matters of promotion** (upheld in *M. Nagaraj*, 2006).
2. **`Article 350A` (Part XVII — Mother-Tongue Instruction at Primary Stage):** Inserted by the **`7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956`** on the recommendation of the States Reorganisation Commission, `Article 350A` directs that *"It shall be the endeavour of **every State and of every local authority within the State** to provide adequate facilities for **instruction in the mother-tongue at the primary stage of education** to children belonging to **linguistic minority groups**; and the **President may issue such directions** to any State as he considers necessary or proper for securing the provision of such facilities."*
3. **`Article 351` (Part XVII — Development of the Hindi Language):** Directs that *"It shall be the duty of the **Union** to promote the spread of the **Hindi language**, to develop it so that it may serve as a medium of expression for all the elements of the **composite culture of India** and to secure its enrichment by assimilating without interfering with its genius, the forms, style and expressions used in Hindustani and in the other languages of India specified in the Eighth Schedule, and by drawing, wherever necessary or desirable, for its vocabulary, **primarily on Sanskrit and secondarily on other languages**."*

> [!prelims]
> **Directives Outside Part IV — Elimination Checklist:**
> - **Strictly Inside Part IV:** ONLY `Articles 36–51`.
> - **Strictly Outside Part IV:** **`Article 335`** (Part XVI — SC/ST claims + efficiency; modified by `82nd Amend, 2000`), **`Article 350A`** (Part XVII — primary mother-tongue instruction for linguistic minorities; inserted by **`7th Amend, 1956`**; binds **States & local authorities**), and **`Article 351`** (Part XVII — Hindi promotion; duty of the **Union** exclusively).
> - **Watch the Trick Question:** If UPSC asks *"Which of the following are part of the Directive Principles of State Policy under Part IV?"* and inserts "Facilities for instruction in mother-tongue at primary stage" (`Art. 350A`), **eliminate it**—it is a constitutional directive *outside* Part IV!

### Criticism and Enduring Utility of Directive Principles

#### Four Classical Criticisms

From the Constituent Assembly floor to comparative constitutional commentary, critics attacked Part IV on four recurring grounds:

1. **No Legal Force ("Pious Superfluities"):** Because `Article 37` bars judicial enforcement, sceptics dismissed Part IV as toothless moral homilies. **Prof. K.T. Shah** famously compared the Directive Principles to *"a cheque on a bank, payable only when the resources of the bank permit."* **Nasiruddin** mocked them as *"no better than the new year's resolutions, which are broken on the second of January."* **T.T. Krishnamachari** derided Part IV as *"a veritable dustbin of sentiment"* allowing every Assembly member to ride a hobby-horse into the Constitution. **K.C. Wheare** called them *"a manifesto of aims and aspirations"* serving as a "moral homily," and **Sir Ivor Jennings** dismissed them as *"pious aspirations."*
2. **Illogically Arranged:** Constitutional scholar **N. Srinivasan** pointed out that Part IV lacks systematic taxonomy or ideological coherence—juxtaposing grand international norms (`Article 51`) beside narrow cultural prescriptions (`Article 48` cow slaughter, `Article 49` monuments), and blending enduring socio-economic principles with transitory Gandhian fads.
3. **Conservative & Ideologically Dated:** **Sir Ivor Jennings** argued that the Directives reflected nineteenth-century British **Fabian Socialism**—remarking that *"the ghosts of Sydney and Beatrice Webb stalk through the pages of the text"*—and warned that binding a twenty-first-century republic to 1940s economic orthodoxy could prove obstructive. *(Notably, the Supreme Court's November 2024 ruling in Property Owners Association directly resolved Jennings' fear by interpreting `Article 39(b)` as flexible economic democracy rather than rigid state socialism!)*
4. **Potential for Constitutional Conflict:** **K. Santhanam** cautioned that Part IV could provoke four institutional clashes: (i) **Centre vs States** (if the Union issues directions under `Article 256` to implement a DPSP and threatens President's Rule under `Article 365` upon non-compliance); (ii) **President vs Prime Minister** (if a President withholds assent to a bill alleging violation of Part IV); (iii) **Governor vs Chief Minister** (in States over contentious directives like prohibition or land reforms); and (iv) **Legislature vs Judiciary**—a conflict that indeed dominated Indian constitutional law from 1951 to 1980.

#### Enduring Utility & Constitutional Significance

Seven and a half decades of lived governance decisively refuted the cynics. Jurists including **L.M. Singhvi** (who hailed DPSPs as *"the life-giving provisions of the Constitution... the stuff of the Constitution and its philosophy of social justice"*), **M.C. Chagla** (who observed that if all directives were fully realised, *"our country would indeed be a heaven on earth"*), and **Sir B.N. Rau** highlighted six lasting strengths of Part IV:
- **Policy Continuity Across Electoral Cycles:** Even when governing coalitions change from left-of-centre to right-of-centre, Part IV provides a bipartisan welfare consensus ensuring stability in both domestic social policy and peaceful foreign policy.
- **Supplementing Fundamental Rights:** Part IV remedies Part III's lack of socio-economic entitlements, ensuring civil liberty is reinforced by material dignity.
- **Judicial Benchmark & Reasonableness Anchor:** Courts rely continuously on Part IV to uphold progressive legislation under `Articles 14` and `19` and to expand human rights under `Article 21`.
- **Guide to All Branches and Tiers of Government:** From Union ministries and NITI Aayog to State legislatures, municipalities, and Gram Panchayats (`Art. 36`), Part IV serves as an authoritative governance compass.
- **Democratic Audit Tool for Citizens and Opposition:** Opposition parties, civil society, and voters hold governments accountable against Part IV benchmarks at election time.
- **Living Transformative Potential:** Because Part IV is phrased as aspirational goals rather than rigid fiscal formulas, it adapts organically across eras—converting original `Article 45` into `Article 21A`, evolving `Article 48A` into climate-change constitutionalism (*M.K. Ranjitsinh*, 2024), and re-balancing `Article 39(b)` for a modern mixed economy (*Property Owners Association*, 2024).

> [!mains]
> **GS-2 Mains Analytical Angle: Auditing Part IV After 75 Years**
> Never write a one-sided Mains answer that merely lists statutes implemented under Part IV. High-scoring GS-2 answers adopt a **two-column empirical audit**:
> - **Substantial Legislative Fulfilment:** Constitutionalised PRIs (`73rd Amend` / `Art. 40`), NALSA & Lok Adalats (`Art. 39A`), universalised minimum wage & social-security framework (`Four Labour Codes, Nov 2025` / `Arts. 42–43`), 125-day rural work guarantee (`VB-G RAM G Act, July 2026` / `Art. 41`), RTE & NEP 2020 ECCE (`Art. 45`), BNSS separation of magistracy (`Art. 50`), and climate-rights jurisprudence (`Art. 48A`).

## 5. Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Article 51A)

Rights and duties are correlative sides of democratic citizenship. A constitution that only empowers citizens to make claims against the State—without reminding them of their reciprocal obligations toward constitutional institutions, fellow citizens, public property, women's dignity, and ecological stewardship—risks eroding the civic fraternity upon which liberty itself depends. Remarkably, the **original Constitution of 1950 contained NO Fundamental Duties** (though it set out the duties of the State under Part IV). The framers assumed that citizens emerging from a moral freedom struggle led by Mahatma Gandhi would spontaneously perform their civic responsibilities without constitutional codification.

Twenty-six years later, during the turbulence of the Internal Emergency (1975–1977), Parliament formally inscribed a citizen's charter of obligations—inserting **Part IVA** containing a single Article, **`Article 51A`**, via the **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`**, later expanded to eleven duties by the **`86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002`**.

### Origins, Comparative Sources & the Swaran Singh Committee (1976)

#### Comparative Constitutional Lineage

Unlike Part III (drawn primarily from the United States) and Part IV (drawn from Ireland), India's **Fundamental Duties were directly inspired by the Constitution of the erstwhile USSR (Soviet Union)**. In comparative constitutional law, major Western liberal democracies—including the **United States, Canada, France, Germany, and Australia**—generally omit a formal catalogue of citizens' duties from their constitutional text, leaving civic obligations to ordinary statutes. Among major democratic constitutions outside the socialist bloc, **Japan** stands out as the prominent exception that expressly codifies citizens' duties (such as work, taxation, and educating children). Internationally, India's Part IVA also resonates with **`Article 29(1)` of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), 1948**, which affirms that *"Everyone has duties to the community in which alone the free and full development of his personality is possible."*

#### Sardar Swaran Singh Committee (1976): Accepted vs Rejected Recommendations

On **26 February 1976**, amid the Internal Emergency, Congress President Dev Kant Borooah constituted a **twelve-member committee chaired by Sardar Swaran Singh** (with A.R. Antulay as Member-Secretary, alongside S.S. Ray, H.R. Gokhale, C.M. Stephen, V.N. Gadgil, Rajni Patel, V.A. Seyid Muhammad, D.P. Singh, D.C. Goswami, V.V. Sathe, and B.N. Mukherjee) to recommend constitutional amendments, including a separate chapter on Fundamental Duties. Stressing that citizens must grow conscious of their duties alongside enjoying their rights, the Committee submitted its proposals in 1976.

The Union Government accepted the core recommendation and enacted the **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`** (effective **3 January 1977**), inserting **Part IVA** and **`Article 51A`** with **ten Fundamental Duties (`51A(a)` to `51A(j)`)**. Tellingly, when the Janata Government later dismantled most Emergency-era excesses through the `44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`, it **deliberately retained Part IVA intact**—confirming an enduring cross-party consensus that citizens' constitutional duties transcended partisan origins.

A perennial UPSC Prelims favourite lies in the exact mismatch between what the **Swaran Singh Committee recommended** and what **Parliament actually enacted** in 1976. While the Committee proposed **eight specific duties**, Parliament enlarged the charter to **ten duties** (adding ideals of the freedom struggle, women's dignity, composite culture, environment/wildlife, scientific temper, and striving for excellence), while **firmly rejecting three stringent proposals** of the Committee:

| Dimension | Swaran Singh Committee Proposal (1976) | Parliament's Decision (`42nd Amendment, 1976`) |
|---|---|---|
| **Separate Chapter on Duties** | Recommended inserting a distinct constitutional chapter on Fundamental Duties | **Accepted:** Inserted **Part IVA** containing a single provision, **`Article 51A`** |
| **Eight Duties Proposed** | (1) Respect & abide by Constitution/laws; (2) Uphold sovereignty, unity & integrity; (3) Respect democratic institutions; (4) Defend country & render national/military service; (5) Abjure communalism; (6) Assist State in implementing DPSPs for socio-economic justice; (7) Abjure violence & protect public property; (8) **Pay taxes according to law** | **7 core ideas adapted & expanded into 10 duties** (`51A(a)–(j)`), adding freedom struggle ideals, women's dignity, composite culture, natural environment, scientific temper & excellence; DPSP-assistance omitted as a standalone duty; **tax-paying REJECTED** |
| **Penalty / Punishment Clause** | Empower Parliament to prescribe **penalties or punishments** for non-compliance or refusal to observe any duty | **REJECTED:** No constitutional penalty clause inserted (though Parliament remains free to pass ordinary laws) |
| **Bar on Judicial Review** | Any law imposing such penalty shall **NOT be challenged in court** on grounds of infringing Fundamental Rights or repugnancy to the Constitution | **REJECTED:** Preserved full judicial review under `Article 13` and `Article 32` |
| **Duty to Pay Taxes** | Make **payment of taxes** an explicit Fundamental Duty | **REJECTED:** Payment of taxes is a statutory legal obligation, NOT a Fundamental Duty under `Article 51A` |

#### The Eleventh Fundamental Duty (86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002)

Twenty-six years after Part IVA was born, the **`86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002`** inserted the **eleventh Fundamental Duty**, clause **`51A(k)`**, obligating every citizen who is a parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to their child or ward aged **six to fourteen years**—completing the constitutional education triad alongside `Article 21A` (Part III) and recast `Article 45` (Part IV).

### Complete Exposition of All Eleven Fundamental Duties (Article 51A(a)–(k))

`Article 51A` begins with the imperative opening: *"It shall be the duty of **every citizen of India**—"* followed by eleven sub-clauses `(a)` through `(k)`. Every single clause carries distinct legal, ethical, and UPSC Prelims significance and must be mastered verbatim in substance:

| Clause | Constitutional Obligation (Summary) | Moral vs Civic | Key Statutory / Judicial Backing |
|---|---|---|---|
| `51A(a)` | Abide by Constitution; respect its ideals, institutions, **National Flag & National Anthem** *(NOT National Song!)* | **Civic** | Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971; *Bijoe Emmanuel* (1986); *Shyam Narayan Chouksey* (2018) |
| `51A(b)` | Cherish and follow noble ideals inspiring our **national struggle for freedom** | **Moral** | Civic & constitutional education; democratic non-violent ethics |
| `51A(c)` | Uphold and protect **sovereignty, unity and integrity** of India | **Civic** | UAPA, 1967; **BNS, 2023** s. `197` (formerly IPC s. `153B`) |
| `51A(d)` | **Defend the country** and render **national service** when called upon | **Civic** | Armed Forces statutes; complements compulsory public service under `Article 23(2)` |
| `51A(e)` | Promote **harmony & common brotherhood** across diversities; **renounce practices derogatory to dignity of women** | **Moral + Civic** | RPA, 1951 s. `123(3)`; Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955; **BNS** s. `196`; Dowry Prohibition, PWDVA 2005, POSH Act 2013 |
| `51A(f)` | Value and preserve rich heritage of our **composite culture** | **Moral** | Cultural conservation & syncretic heritage *(contrast with State duty in `Art. 49`!)* |
| `51A(g)` | Protect & improve **natural environment** (**forests, lakes, rivers, wildlife**); **compassion for living creatures** | **Civic + Moral** | Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972; Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980; PCA Act, 1960; *M.C. Mehta*; *M.K. Ranjitsinh* (2024) |
| `51A(h)` | Develop **scientific temper, humanism, spirit of inquiry and reform** | **Moral** | Anti-superstition State Acts (Maharashtra/Karnataka); NEP 2020 critical pedagogy |
| `51A(i)` | Safeguard **public property** and **abjure violence** | **Civic** | Prevention of Damage to Public Property (PDPP) Act, 1984; *In Re: Destruction of Public & Private Properties* (2009) |
| `51A(j)` | Strive towards **excellence** in all spheres of individual and collective activity | **Moral** | ***AIIMS Students' Union v. AIIMS* (2001)** (struck down excessive institutional PG quota) |
| `51A(k)` | *(86th Amend, 2002)* Parent/guardian to provide education opportunities to child/ward aged **6 to 14 years** | **Civic** | **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009** (Section 10) |

#### Clause-by-Clause Breakdown of Article 51A

- **Clause (a) — Constitutional Fidelity & National Symbols:** *"To abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the **National Flag and the National Anthem**."* Anchors constitutional patriotism—demanding loyalty not to any ruler or party, but to the constitutional framework, Parliament, courts, and sovereign symbols. **Crucial Prelims Trap:** Clause `(a)` explicitly names ONLY the **National Flag** and the **National Anthem** (*Jana Gana Mana*); it does **NOT** mention the **National Song** (*Vande Mataram*) or the National Emblem!
- **Clause (b) — Noble Ideals of the Freedom Struggle:** *"To cherish and follow the noble ideals which inspired our national struggle for freedom."* Elevates the foundational ethos of India's anti-colonial movement—non-violence (*ahimsa*), inclusive secularism, social justice, gender emancipation, and self-reliance (*swadeshi*)—into a living generational duty.
- **Clause (c) — Sovereignty, Unity and Integrity of India:** *"To uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India."* Directly parallels the `42nd Amendment`'s simultaneous insertion of the word **"integrity"** into the Preamble, obligating citizens to resist secessionism, terrorism, and threats to territorial cohesion.
- **Clause (d) — National Defence & National Service:** *"To defend the country and render national service when called upon to do so."* Reaches beyond professional armed forces to affirm that in moments of external aggression or national crisis, every citizen shares a duty to serve—harmonising directly with **`Article 23(2)`**, which permits the State to impose compulsory public service without discrimination on religion, race, caste, or class.
- **Clause (e) — Harmony, Brotherhood & Dignity of Women:** *"To promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood amongst all the people of India transcending religious, linguistic and regional or sectional diversities; to **renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women**."* Notice its vital **dual mandate**: first, operationalising the Preamble's promise of **Fraternity** across India's plural society; second, obligating citizens personally to dismantle patriarchal norms—such as dowry, female foeticide, domestic violence, workplace harassment, and discriminatory customs—complementing State action under `Articles 15(3)`, `39`, and `42`.
- **Clause (f) — Heritage of Composite Culture:** *"To value and preserve the rich heritage of our **composite culture**."* Celebrates India's syncretic civilisational mosaic woven across Vedic, Buddhist, Jain, Islamic, Sikh, Christian, Dravidian, and tribal traditions. **Classic Prelims Contrast:** Do not conflate `51A(f)` (citizen's duty toward *composite culture*) with `Article 49` (State's DPSP obligation to protect *monuments and objects of national importance*).
- **Clause (g) — Natural Environment & Compassion for Living Creatures:** *"To protect and improve the natural environment including **forests, lakes, rivers and wild life**, and to have **compassion for living creatures**."* **Essential Prelims Contrast with `Article 48A`:** While `Article 48A` directs the State to protect the environment, forests, and wildlife, **`Article 51A(g)` explicitly goes further** by naming **lakes and rivers** and adding an ethical mandate of **compassion for living creatures**—a clause heavily relied upon by the Supreme Court in ***Animal Welfare Board of India v. A. Nagaraja* (2014)** (animal cruelty principles) and ***M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India* (21 March 2024)** (right against adverse climate change).
- **Clause (h) — Scientific Temper, Humanism, Inquiry and Reform:** *"To develop the **scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform**."* Drawing directly on Jawaharlal Nehru's *The Discovery of India* (1946)—which defined scientific temper as a fearless, evidence-based way of life open to revision—`51A(h)` makes India virtually unique among world constitutions in constitutionally enshrining rationalism, combating superstition, witch-hunting, fake news, and obscurantism while wedding reason to compassionate **humanism**.
- **Clause (i) — Safeguarding Public Property & Abjuring Violence:** *"To safeguard public property and to abjure violence."* Born from concern over violent agitations burning trains, buses, and civic infrastructure, `51A(i)` distinguishes legitimate peaceful protest (`Article 19(1)(b)`) from destructive mob violence—backed by the *Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act, 1984* and Supreme Court damage-assessment guidelines in ***In Re: Destruction of Public & Private Properties* (2009)**.
- **Clause (j) — Striving Towards Excellence:** *"To strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity so that the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavour and achievement."* Anchors merit, public-service efficiency, institutional quality, and national competitiveness—famously deployed by the Supreme Court in ***AIIMS Students' Union* (2001)** to curb excessive institutional quotas compromising medical excellence.
- **Clause (k) — Parental Duty of Elementary Education (`86th Amendment, 2002`):** *"Who is a **parent or guardian** to provide opportunities for **education** to his child or, as the case may be, ward between the age of **six and fourteen years**."* Distinctively, this is the only Fundamental Duty addressed to a specific subset of citizens (parents and guardians), recognising that `Article 21A` and the RTE Act, 2009 cannot succeed unless families actively enrol and retain children—especially girls—in school rather than pushing them into child labour.

> [!prelims]
> **Seven High-Yield UPSC Prelims Traps on Fundamental Duties (`Article 51A`):**
> 1. **Citizens ONLY:** Unlike `Articles 14, 20, 21`, or `25–28`, Fundamental Duties apply **exclusively to Indian citizens**—NEVER to foreigners.
> 2. **What Is NOT in `Article 51A` (Repeated UPSC PYQ Trap):** **Casting a vote in elections**, **paying taxes**, **practising family planning**, and **respecting the National Song** are **NOT** listed among India's 11 Fundamental Duties!
> 3. **DPSP vs Fundamental Duty Confusion (UPSC Prelims 2015, 2017, 2020, 2021):**
>    - Protecting **monuments of national importance** = **`Article 49` (DPSP)**; preserving **composite culture** = **`Article 51A(f)` (FD)**.
>    - Protecting **village panchayats** (`Art. 40`), **cottage industries** (`Art. 43`), **uniform civil code** (`Art. 44`), **separation of judiciary** (`Art. 50`), and **international peace** (`Art. 51`) = **DPSPs ONLY**, NOT Fundamental Duties!
>    - **Environment, forests & wildlife** appear in **BOTH** `Article 48A` (DPSP) and `Article 51A(g)` (FD)—but **lakes, rivers, and compassion for living creatures** appear **ONLY in `51A(g)`**!
> 4. **Constitutional Enforceability (UPSC Prelims 2017):** A **legislative process has NOT been provided in the Constitution** to enforce Fundamental Duties, nor are they directly enforceable by writs—yet Parliament *can* enact ordinary statutes to enforce them, and duties are NOT strictly "correlative to legal duties" in the sense of automatic judicial sanction.
> 5. **Who Added `51A(k)`?** The **`86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002`** (NOT 42nd, NOT 44th)—applying strictly to ages **6 to 14** (NOT 0–6, which is recast `Article 45`).

### Salient Features & Judicial Application of Fundamental Duties

Six distinctive characteristics define Part IVA in Indian constitutionalism:
1. **Synthesis of Moral and Civic Duties:** Part IVA blends high ethical/moral ideals (cherishing freedom-struggle ideals under `51A(b)`, developing humanism under `51A(h)`) with concrete civic obligations (respecting the Constitution, Flag, and Anthem under `51A(a)`, protecting public property under `51A(i)`).
2. **Codification of Indian Civilisational Values:** Rather than alien transplants, most duties codify traditional values deeply rooted in Indian *dharma*, mythology, religious traditions, and social ethics—respect for nature, compassion for living beings, reverence for women, and communal harmony.
3. **Exclusive to Citizens:** Unlike select Fundamental Rights accessible to all persons, Fundamental Duties bind **citizens of India alone**.
4. **Non-Justiciable Yet Legislatively Enforceable:** Like Part IV, Part IVA contains no direct constitutional enforcement mechanism or penalty; courts cannot issue writs directly punishing a citizen solely for breaching `Article 51A`. However, Parliament is fully competent to enact **ordinary penal or regulatory statutes** operationalising specific duties.
5. **Constitutional Interpretive Aid & Reasonableness Yardstick:** Just as courts use Directive Principles to assess statutory validity, the Supreme Court treats `Article 51A` as an authoritative guide to interpret ambiguous statutes and judge whether a statutory restriction on `Article 14` or `Article 19` is **"reasonable" in public interest** (*Mohan Kumar Singhania*, 1992; *Mirzapur Moti Kureshi*, 2005).
6. **Balance Against Executive Overreach:** Crucially, Indian courts have insisted that Fundamental Duties reinforce constitutional morality—they **cannot be weaponised by the State to override Fundamental Rights** or compel coercive conformity.

#### Landmark Supreme Court Jurisprudence on Article 51A (1986–2024)

Seven pivotal Supreme Court decisions map the exact boundaries of Fundamental Duties in practice:
- ***Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala* (1986 — National Anthem Case):** Three Jehovah's Witness schoolchildren respectfully stood in silence when the National Anthem was sung at school assembly but declined to sing due to religious conscience. Expelled by school authorities, they won reinstatement from the Supreme Court, which held that standing respectfully fully satisfies `Article 51A(a)` and the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971—there is no legal provision compelling anyone to sing against their `Article 19(1)(a)` and `Article 25(1)` conscience.
- ***M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (1983, 1988 & 1991):** Invoking `Article 51A(g)` alongside `Articles 48A` and `21`, the Supreme Court issued binding directions to curb Ganga pollution and instructed the Central Government to introduce **compulsory environmental education** (at least one hour weekly) across schools and colleges, plus public ecological broadcasts via radio and television.
- ***AIIMS Students' Union v. AIIMS* (2001):** In a landmark articulation of Part IVA's status, the Supreme Court declared that Fundamental Duties, though non-enforceable directly, are **equally important** as Fundamental Rights in constitutional interpretation because both share the adjective *"Fundamental."* Invoking **`Article 51A(j)`** (striving towards excellence), the Court struck down an excessive 33% overall / 50% discipline-wise institutional quota for AIIMS postgraduate admissions as sacrificing merit and public healthcare excellence.
- ***Hon'ble Shri Ranganath Mishra v. Union of India* (2003):** Responding to a letter by former CJI Ranganath Mishra treated as a PIL, the Supreme Court directed the Union Government to earnestly examine and implement the **Justice J.S. Verma Committee (1999)** recommendations for sensitising citizens to Fundamental Duties through education.
- ***Shyam Narayan Chouksey v. Union of India* (30 November 2016 → Modified 9 January 2018):** Initially passing an interim order making playing of the National Anthem mandatory in all cinema halls citing `Article 51A(a)`, a three-judge bench led by CJI Dipak Misra **modified its order on 9 January 2018**, making playing the Anthem in cinemas **optional** (rather than compulsory), while affirming that whenever it is played, citizens must stand in respect (expressly exempting persons with disabilities).
- ***M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India* (21 March 2024):** Paired **`Article 51A(g)`** with `Article 48A` to illuminate `Articles 21` and `14`, anchoring India's constitutional **right to be free from the adverse effects of climate change**.
- ***Durga Dutt v. Union of India* (SC Proceedings, 11 September 2024):** Hearing a PIL demanding a comprehensive Parliamentary code enforcing Fundamental Duties, a three-judge bench (Justices Sanjiv Khanna, P.V. Sanjay Kumar, and R. Mahadevan) firmly held that **courts CANNOT issue a writ of mandamus directing Parliament to enact a law** to enforce `Article 51A`. Echoing Attorney General R. Venkataramani's submission that penal coercion is not the answer and that civic responsibility must be nurtured through education and awareness, the bench requested a consolidated synopsis of existing Central and State statutes already operationalising Fundamental Duties.

```cases

Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986) | Art. 51A(a) vs Conscience | Standing respectfully during the National Anthem satisfies Art. 51A(a); forced singing violates Arts. 19(1)(a) & 25(1).
M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1988 / 1991) | Art. 51A(g) Ecological Duty | Paired Art. 51A(g) with Arts. 48A & 21 to mandate Ganga protection and compulsory environmental education in schools.
AIIMS Students' Union v. AIIMS (2001) | Art. 51A(j) Excellence | Declared FDs equally fundamental in constitutional interpretation; struck down excessive 33%/50% institutional PG quotas.
Ranganath Mishra v. Union of India (2003) | Operationalising FDs | Directed the Central Government to earnestly implement the Justice J.S. Verma Committee (1999) educational roadmap.
Shyam Narayan Chouksey (2016 / 9 Jan 2018) | National Anthem in Cinemas | Modified 2016 interim order to make playing the National Anthem in cinema halls optional; retained duty to stand respectfully (exempting PwDs).
Durga Dutt v. Union of India (11 Sept 2024) | 3-Judge Bench · Non-Coercive Ethos | Reaffirmed SC cannot direct Parliament to enact a law enforcing Art. 51A; duties must be nurtured through civic awareness & existing statutes.
```

> **Interpretive Shield vs Coercive Weapon — Seeing the Judicial Balance in Action:**
> Notice how sensitively the Supreme Court calibrates `Article 51A`. When an institution compromises public excellence (*AIIMS Students' Union*, 2001) or polluters degrade shared rivers (*M.C. Mehta*), courts invoke Fundamental Duties to **uphold reasonable regulations and public-interest remedies**. Conversely, when litigants demand compulsory singing (*Bijoe Emmanuel*, 1986) or blanket criminal statutes coercing duties (*Durga Dutt*, Sept 2024), courts protect individual liberty—proving that Fundamental Duties enrich democratic citizenship without turning into authoritarian instruments.

### Justice J.S. Verma Committee Report (1999) & Statutory Enforcement Map

In **1998**, the Government of India appointed a committee chaired by former Chief Justice of India **Justice J.S. Verma** to *"operationalise the suggestions to teach Fundamental Duties to the citizens of the country."* Submitting its landmark report in **October 1999**, the Verma Committee made two lasting contributions: first, it debunked the myth that Fundamental Duties lack legal teeth by **mapping existing Indian statutes that already enforce specific clauses of `Article 51A`**; second, it recommended nationwide civic education through **NCERT** curricula, **NCTE** teacher-training modules, media awareness, and observing **3 January** (the date the `42nd Amendment`'s Part IVA took effect) as **Fundamental Duties Day**.

| Statute Identified by Verma Committee (1999) & Modern Equivalents | Fundamental Duty Enforced | Core Legal Mechanism / Penalty |
|---|---|---|
| **Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971** | `Article 51A(a)` | Criminalises intentional disrespect to the Constitution of India, National Flag, and preventing singing of the National Anthem |
| **Emblems and Names (Prevention of Improper Use) Act, 1950** | `Article 51A(a)` | Prohibits improper commercial or unauthorised use of national emblems, names, and symbols |
| **Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act (UAPA), 1967** | `Article 51A(c)` | Enables banning communal, terrorist, or secessionist organisations threatening India's sovereignty, unity, and integrity |
| **Representation of the People Act (RPA), 1951** (ss. `123(3)`, `123(3A)`) | `Article 51A(e)` | Disqualifies MPs/MLAs indulging in corrupt practices such as soliciting votes on grounds of religion or promoting communal enmity |
| **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** | `Article 51A(e)` | Punishes preaching or practising untouchability and caste-based social disabilities |
| **Indian Penal Code (IPC), 1860** (ss. `153A`, `153B`, `295A`) → **Now Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023** (ss. `196`, `197`, `299`, effective **1 July 2024**) | `Articles 51A(c), 51A(e)` | Penalises promoting enmity between groups (`BNS s. 196`), assertions/false electronic information prejudicial to national integration (`BNS s. 197`, including new clause `197(1)(d)`), and malicious religious outrage (`BNS s. 299`) |

Shortly thereafter, the **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC — Venkatachaliah Commission, 2002)** fully endorsed the Verma Committee's operationalisation roadmap and further recommended amending `Article 51A` to incorporate four new Fundamental Duties: (1) **duty to vote** in elections and participate actively in democratic governance; (2) **duty to pay taxes**; (3) duty to **foster family values and responsible parenthood** regarding children's education, physical, and moral well-being; and (4) **duty of industrial organisations** to provide education to the children of their employees. None of these additions has been enacted to date.

### Criticism, Significance & Rights–Duties Correlativity (Mains Synthesis)

#### Five Classical Criticisms of Fundamental Duties

1. **Incomplete & Non-Exhaustive Catalogue:** Critics observe that `Article 51A` omits core civic duties vital to a modern republic—most notably **casting votes**, **paying taxes**, and **practising family planning** (though Swaran Singh had proposed tax-paying and NCRWC later recommended voting and taxes).
2. **Vague, Ambiguous & Complex Terminology:** Expressions such as *"noble ideals,"* *"composite culture,"* *"scientific temper,"* and *"humanism"* carry varied subjective meanings and are difficult for ordinary citizens to decipher uniformly.
3. **Non-Justiciable ("Code of Moral Precepts"):** Because Part IVA imposes no direct constitutional penalty for non-compliance, sceptics question whether unenforceable homilies alter citizen behaviour on the ground.
4. **Superfluous Addition:** Constitutional traditionalists argue that the listed duties represent basic ethical norms citizens would observe even without formal inscription in the Constitution.
5. **Subordinate Placement & Emergency-Era Origin:** Appending Fundamental Duties as **Part IVA** immediately after Part IV (rather than alongside Part III) symbolically reduces their stature, while critics periodically point to their origin during the 1975–77 Emergency as warranting caution against state paternalism.

#### Six Enduring Significances

1. **Reminder of Civic Reciprocity:** Part IVA reminds citizens that enjoying liberties under Part III carries corresponding obligations toward society, fellow citizens, and the constitutional order.
2. **Deterrent Against Anti-Social & Anti-National Conduct:** Acts as a clear constitutional warning against burning the National Flag, vandalising public transport, spreading communal hate, or degrading ecosystems.
3. **Catalyst for Participatory Democracy:** Encourages citizens to act not as passive spectators awaiting State delivery, but as active co-builders of national excellence, cleanliness, ecological resilience, and gender equality.
4. **Judicial Aid in Constitutional Adjudication:** Assists the Supreme Court and High Courts in determining the constitutionality and reasonableness of welfare, environmental, and merit-preserving legislation (*AIIMS Students' Union*, *Mirzapur Moti Kureshi*, *M.K. Ranjitsinh*).
5. **Enforceable Through Targeted Ordinary Statutes:** As the Verma Committee demonstrated, Parliament can back specific duties with calibrated penal laws without turning Part IVA into a blunt weapon against dissent.
6. **Ethical Resonance with Gandhian Philosophy:** Realises Mahatma Gandhi's celebrated dictum that *"the true source of rights is duty"*—if every citizen discharges duties sincerely, every fellow citizen's rights stand automatically safeguarded.

## 1. Philosophy, Origin & Features of Directive Principles (Part IV, Arts. 36–51)

- **Constitutional Origin:** Borrowed from **`Article 45` of the Irish Constitution (1937)** (rooted in **Spanish Constitution, 1931**). Domestic precursors: **Karachi Congress Resolution (1931)**, **Sapru Committee (1945)** (proposed splitting justiciable Part III vs non-justiciable Part IV), and **Instrument of Instructions** under the **GoI Act, 1935** (now addressed to elected legislatures and executives).
- **Foundational Quotes:** **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar (4 Nov 1948)** called DPSPs a **"novel feature"** with electoral rather than judicial sanction; **Granville Austin (1966)** termed Parts III & IV together **"the conscience of the Constitution."**
- **Core Purpose:** Establishes a **Welfare State** (replacing the colonial Police State) and **social and economic democracy** (whereas Part III secures *political democracy*). Reflects **UDHR (1948)** alongside Preamble, FRs, and FDs (UPSC Prelims 2020).
- **`Article 36`:** Imports `Article 12`'s 4-limb definition of **"the State"** (Union, States, local authorities, other authorities).
- **`Article 37`:** Three rules—(1) **non-justiciable** (not enforceable by any court); (2) **fundamental in the governance of the country**; (3) **duty of the State** to apply them in making laws.
- **Prelims Traps:** DPSPs impose **NO direct legal limitation** on legislative or executive function (Prelims 2020); "Economic Justice" appears in **Preamble + DPSP (`Art. 38`)** (not FRs); courts use DPSPs to test **"reasonableness"** under `Arts. 14 & 19` (*Balsara*, *Pathumma*, *Mirzapur Moti Kureshi*) and expand `Art. 21`.

## 2. Classification & Article-by-Article Exposition (Articles 38–51)

- **No Textual Classification:** The Constitution does **NOT** classify DPSPs; scholars group `Articles 38–51` into **Socialistic, Gandhian, and Liberal-Intellectual**. **Dual-category trap:** **`Arts. 43, 47, 48`** each straddle **two** streams!
- **Socialistic Principles (`38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 43, 43A, 47`):**
  - `38(1)` Social order with social, economic, political justice; **`38(2)` (`44th Amend, 1978`)** minimise income inequalities & eliminate inequalities in status, facilities, opportunities among individuals & groups.
  - `39`: `(a)` adequate livelihood (men/women equally); `(b)` distribution of **material resources of the community** for common good; `(c)` prevent concentration of wealth & means of production; `(d)` **equal pay for equal work** (*Randhir Singh*, 1982); `(e)` protect workers' health & children's tender age; **`(f)` (recast by `42nd Amend, 1976`)** healthy child development in freedom & dignity.
  - **`39A` (`42nd Amend, 1976`):** Equal justice & **free legal aid** (*Hussainara Khatoon*, NALSA).
  - `41`: Right to **work, education, public assistance** (unemployment, old age, sickness, disablement) *within economic capacity*.
  - `42`: Just/humane work conditions & **maternity relief**.
  - `43` (Socialistic limb): **Living wage** (above minimum & fair wage — *Express Newspaper*, 1958), decent life, leisure.
  - **`43A` (`42nd Amend, 1976`):** Workers' participation in industrial management.
  - `47` (Socialistic limb): Raise nutrition, living standards, public health (**only DPSP termed a "primary duty"**).
- **Gandhian Principles (`40, 43, 43B, 46, 47, 48`):**
  - `40`: **Village panchayats** as units of self-government (`73rd Amend, 1992`).
  - `43` (Gandhian limb): Rural **cottage industries** (individual/co-operative).
  - **`43B` (`97th Amend, 2011`):** Voluntary, autonomous, democratic, professionally managed **co-operative societies** (left completely **intact** by *Rajendra N. Shah*, July 2021, which struck down Part IXB only for State co-ops).
  - `46`: Educational/economic interests of **SCs, STs & weaker sections** (cited for `103rd Amend` EWS quota — *Janhit Abhiyan*, 2022).
  - `47` (Gandhian limb): **Prohibition** of intoxicating drinks & injurious drugs except medicinal use (active in Gujarat, Bihar, Nagaland, Mizoram).
  - `48` (Gandhian limb): Preserve cattle breeds & **prohibit slaughter of cows, calves, milch & draught cattle** (*Mirzapur Moti Kureshi*, 2005 7-judge bench overruled *Hanif Quareshi*, 1958, upholding total ban on bulls/bullocks).
- **Liberal-Intellectual Principles (`44, 45, 48, 48A, 49, 50, 51`):**
  - `44`: **Uniform Civil Code (UCC)** throughout India. Goa retains **Portuguese Civil Code (1867)**. SC nudges: *Shah Bano* (1985 — "dead letter"), *Sarla Mudgal* (1995), *John Vallamattom* (2003). **21st Law Commission (2018):** UCC *"neither necessary nor desirable at this stage"* (reform within personal laws); **22nd Law Commission (June 2023)** reopened consultation. **Uttarakhand UCC Act, 2024** (passed 7 Feb 2024, assent 11 Mar 2024, **in force 27 Jan 2025**; exempts Scheduled Tribes; covers marriage, divorce, succession, mandatory live-in registration; no adoption chapter). **Gujarat UCC Act, 2026** (passed 24 Mar 2026, assent 17 Sept 2026, awaiting enforcement notification).
  - **`45` (Recast by `86th Amend, 2002`):** **Early Childhood Care & Education (ECCE)** until age **6** (originally 0–14 within 10 years; `86th Amend` triad = `21A` [6–14 FR] + `45` [0–6 DPSP] + `51A(k)` [6–14 FD]).
  - `48` (Liberal limb): Modern, scientific **agriculture & animal husbandry**.
  - **`48A` (`42nd Amend, 1976`):** Protect/improve **environment, forests & wildlife** (*M.K. Ranjitsinh*, 21 Mar 2024 climate-change right).
  - `49`: State **obligation** to protect **monuments/objects of national importance** (`7th Amend, 1956` minor phrasing tweak).
  - `50`: **Separate judiciary from executive** in public services (Prelims 2020; `CrPC, 1973` → `BNSS, 2023` ss. `6, 14–17`).
  - `51`: **International peace & security**, just relations, respect for **international law/treaties** (dualist India — *Vishaka*, 1997), and **arbitration** (Prelims 2014/2017).
- **Exact Part IV Amendment Summary:** **`42nd (1976)`** = recast `39(f)` + added `39A, 43A, 48A`; **`44th (1978)`** = `38(2)`; **`86th (2002)`** = recast `45`; **`97th (2011)`** = `43B`; (`7th, 1956` = technical edit in `49`).

## 3. Conflict & Harmony Between Fundamental Rights and DPSP

- ***Champakam Dorairajan* (1951):** DPSPs run **subsidiary** to FRs; struck down communal college quotas → **`1st Amendment (1951)`** (`Art. 15(4)`, `31A`, `31B`, Ninth Schedule), followed by `4th (1955)` & `17th (1964)` Amendments.
- ***In Re: Kerala Education Bill* (1958):** Adopted **Doctrine of Harmonious Construction** to give maximum effect to both Parts III and IV.
- ***Golaknath* (1967, 6–5):** Held FRs transcendental & unamendable even for DPSPs → **`24th Amendment (1971)`** (`Art. 13(4)`, mandatory assent) & **`25th Amendment (1971)`** inserting **`Article 31C`** (Limb 1: shields `Art. 39(b)/(c)` laws from `Arts. 14 & 19`; Limb 2: bars judicial review of nexus declarations).
- ***Kesavananda Bharati* (24 April 1973, 13 judges, 7–6):** Overruled *Golaknath*; established **Basic Structure Doctrine**; **upheld `Art. 31C` Limb 1**, **struck down Limb 2** (review bar).
- **`42nd Amendment (1976)` (Sec. 4):** Widened `Art. 31C` from `39(b)–(c)` to **ALL DPSPs** over `Arts. 14 & 19`.
- ***Minerva Mills* (31 July 1980, 4–1):** Struck down `42nd Amend` expansion of `Art. 31C`; CJI Y.V. Chandrachud declared **harmony and balance between Parts III and IV** ("two wheels of a chariot") part of the **Basic Structure**.
- ***Waman Rao* (1981) & *I.R. Coelho* (2007):** **24 April 1973** cutoff for Ninth Schedule Basic Structure review; confirmed unamended `Art. 31C`.
- ***Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (5 November 2024, 9-Judge Bench, `2024 INSC 835`):**
  - **9–0 Unanimous:** Pre-42nd **`Article 31C`** (as upheld in *Kesavananda*) **still survives** post-*Minerva Mills* via doctrine of revival.
  - **8–1 Majority** (CJI D.Y. Chandrachud for 7; Justice B.V. Nagarathna partly concurring while defending Justice Krishna Iyer; Justice Sudhanshu Dhulia lone dissent): **Not every privately owned asset** is a **"material resource of the community"** under **`Article 39(b)`**; overruled ***Sanjeev Coke* (1982)** and disapproved Justice Krishna Iyer's *Ranganatha Reddy* (1977) dicta. Established **5-factor Public Trust test** (nature, characteristics, community well-being impact, scarcity, private-concentration effects) affirming flexible **economic democracy**.
- **Settled Rule Today:** FRs generally prevail; genuine `Art. 39(b)–(c)` laws hold `Art. 31C` immunity against `Arts. 14 & 19` (subject to judicial review & 2024 test); courts continuously read DPSPs into `Art. 21` (*Hoskot*, *Olga Tellis*, *Unni Krishnan*, *M.K. Ranjitsinh* 2024).

## 5. Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Article 51A)

- **Origins:** Absent in 1950; inspired by **USSR Constitution** (**Japan** is the prominent democratic parallel; echoes **UDHR Art. 29**). Recommended by 12-member **Sardar Swaran Singh Committee (1976)**; inserted via **`42nd Amendment (1976)`** (effective **3 January 1977** — Part IVA, `Art. 51A`, **10 duties**; retained by Janata Govt in `44th Amend`). **`86th Amendment (2002)`** added the **11th duty `51A(k)`** (parent/guardian duty for child aged **6–14**).
- **Swaran Singh Rejected Recommendations (3 Classic Prelims Traps):** Proposed **8 duties**; Parliament enacted **10** but **REJECTED**: (1) **penalties/punishments** for non-compliance; (2) **barring judicial review** of penal laws; (3) **duty to pay taxes**.
- **All 11 Duties (`51A(a)–(k)`):** `(a)` Constitution, ideals, institutions, **National Flag & National Anthem** *(NOT National Song)*; `(b)` noble ideals of freedom struggle; `(c)` **sovereignty, unity & integrity**; `(d)` defend country & render national service; `(e)` harmony/common brotherhood & **renounce practices derogatory to women's dignity**; `(f)` heritage of **composite culture**; `(g)` **natural environment** (**forests, lakes, rivers, wildlife**) & **compassion for living creatures**; `(h)` **scientific temper, humanism, inquiry & reform**; `(i)` safeguard **public property** & abjure violence; `(j)` strive towards **excellence**; `(k)` parent/guardian education duty (**ages 6–14**).
- **Key Features & Traps:** Bind **citizens ONLY** (not foreigners); blend moral & civic duties; **non-justiciable** (no constitutional enforcement process/penalty); **omit voting, paying taxes, family planning, National Song**.
- **Verma Committee (1999) Statutory Map (Updated with BNS 2023):** Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act 1971 (`51A(a)`); Emblems & Names Act 1950 (`51A(a)`); UAPA 1967 (`51A(c)`); RPA 1951 s. 123(3)/(3A) (`51A(e)`); Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955 (`51A(e)`); **BNS, 2023** ss. `196` (enmity), `197` (national integration), `299` (formerly IPC `153A/153B/295A`); WPA 1972, FCA 1980 & PCA Act 1960 (`51A(g)`); PDPP Act 1984 (`51A(i)`); RTE Act 2009 s. 10 (`51A(k)`). Recommended **3 January** as Fundamental Duties Day. **NCRWC (2002)** endorsed Verma report & suggested adding duties to vote, pay taxes, family values, and corporate worker-child education.
- **Landmark Cases:** ***Bijoe Emmanuel* (1986)** (standing silently satisfies `51A(a)`); ***M.C. Mehta*** (compulsory environmental education via `51A(g)`); ***AIIMS Students' Union* (2001)** (FDs equally fundamental; struck excessive PG quota via `51A(j)`); ***Ranganath Mishra* (2003)** (implement Verma report); ***Shyam Narayan Chouksey* (9 Jan 2018)** (cinema anthem optional, standing retained); ***M.K. Ranjitsinh* (21 Mar 2024)** (`51A(g) + 48A + 21` climate right); ***Durga Dutt* (11 Sept 2024)** (SC cannot direct Parliament to enact a law enforcing FDs; nurture civic consciousness, never make Fundamental Rights conditional on duties).
