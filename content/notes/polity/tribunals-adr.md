## 1. Constitutional framework for tribunals: Part XIV-A

Specialised disputes—service matters, tax valuations, insolvency, telecom tariffs, and environmental harm—demand domain expertise and faster procedure than regular civil courts provide. Tribunals were **not in the original Constitution of 1950**; Parliament inserted **Part XIV-A (Articles 323A and 323B)** via the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**, on the **Swaran Singh Committee's** recommendation.

| Dimension | Article 323A (Administrative Tribunals) | Article 323B (Tribunals for Other Matters) |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject matter** | **Public service matters ONLY** (Union, State, local/public-authority recruitment & service conditions) | **Other matters:** taxation; foreign exchange/customs; industrial & labour; land reforms; urban property ceiling; **elections to Parliament & State Legislatures**; foodstuffs; **rent & tenancy** (**75th Amendment, 1994**) |
| **Who establishes** | **Parliament ONLY** | **Both Parliament and State Legislatures** (within legislative competence) |
| **Hierarchy** | **NO hierarchy** (one Central, one per State, or Joint for 2+ States) | **Hierarchy permitted** (original + appellate tribunals) |

> **Prelims pointer:** Two frequent traps: (1) **Elections to Parliament and State Legislatures** sit under **Article 323B**, NOT 323A; and (2) **State Legislatures cannot establish Article 323A tribunals**—even **State Administrative Tribunals (SATs)** are established by the **Central Government** on a State's specific request.

### CAT, SATs and JATs (Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985)

Enacted under Article 323A, the **Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985** created the **Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT)** (Principal Bench in New Delhi plus regional benches) covering All India Services, Central civil services, Union civil posts, and **civilian defence employees**. Unbound by the Code of Civil Procedure (CPC), 1908, CAT follows **principles of natural justice** and allows self-representation. The Centre also creates **SATs** (on State request) and **Joint Administrative Tribunals (JATs)** for two or more States, with Chairpersons and members appointed by the President after consulting concerned Governor(s).

> **Prelims pointer:** CAT **EXCLUDES** three groups: **(1) defence forces personnel** (under the Armed Forces Tribunal, 2007), **(2) officers and servants of the Supreme Court**, and **(3) secretarial staff of Parliament**.

## 2. The tribunalisation debate, Supreme Court rulings and the 2026 reform

Early tribunal statutes tried to **exclude High Court writ jurisdiction (Articles 226–227)** so appeals went directly to the Supreme Court. Four decades of litigation pitted executive control against judicial independence.

```text
 1976  42nd Amdt      Added Part XIV-A (Arts 323A & 323B)
          │
 1987  Sampath Kumar  Tribunals as HC substitutes (if indep)
          │
 1997  L. Chandra     Overruled above; Arts 226/227 Basic
       Kumar (7 JJ)   Structure; appeal to HC Div. Bench
          │
 2010  R. Gandhi /    NCLT valid; NTT void (2014); Finance
 2019  Rojer Mathew   Act 2017 rules voided; NTC urged
          │
 2021  MBA-5 / Act    2021 Ordinance voided; 2021 Act kept
       enacted        4-yr term, 50-yr age, 2-name panel
          │
 2025  MBA-6 (Nov)    Voided 2021 Act clauses ("legislative
       (Gavai CJI)    override"); ordered NTC in 4 months
          │
 2026  Tribunals Act  Repeals 2021 Act; 5-member NTC, 5-yr
       (13 Aug 2026)  term, no 50-yr bar (16 tribunals)
```

In ***S.P. Sampath Kumar* (1987)**, the Court provisionally accepted tribunals as High Court substitutes. A **seven-judge bench** overruled it in ***L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997)**: judicial review under **Articles 226, 227, and 32** is **Basic Structure**; tribunals are strictly **supplemental**, never substitutes; and tribunal orders must first be challenged before a **Division Bench of the jurisdictional High Court** before reaching the Supreme Court.

Subsequent verdicts policed executive encroachment: ***Union of India v. R. Gandhi* (2010, five judges)** upheld NCLT/NCLAT while curbing bureaucratic dominance over technical members (and ***Madras Bar Association*, 2014** struck down the National Tax Tribunal); ***Rojer Mathew* (2019, five judges)** voided Finance Act, 2017 tribunal rules, ordered a **Judicial Impact Assessment**, backed an independent **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)**, and referred Money Bill certification to seven judges; and ***Madras Bar Association* (2020 & July 2021)** struck down the 2020 Rules and April 2021 Ordinance (which had abolished nine tribunals including IPAB and FCAT). Yet Parliament enacted the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021**, re-enacting the invalidated **50-year minimum age**, **four-year tenure**, and **two-name Search-cum-Selection Committee (SCSC) panel**.

### *Madras Bar Association* (19 November 2025) and the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026

On **19 November 2025**, **Chief Justice B.R. Gavai and Justice K. Vinod Chandran** struck down those re-enacted appointment, age, tenure, and panel clauses of the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** as an unconstitutional **"legislative override"** violating separation of powers and judicial independence, and directed establishing an independent **NTC** within four months:

> "When Parliament designs or alters the tribunal system, it must do so in a manner consistent with the constitutional requirements of independence, impartiality, and effective adjudication. A law that undermines these foundational values, such as by enabling executive control over appointments, curtailing tenure arbitrarily, or weakening institutional autonomy, does not merely offend an 'abstract principle', it strikes at the core of the constitutional arrangement." — Chief Justice B.R. Gavai, *Madras Bar Association v. Union of India*, 19 November 2025

> **In the news:** Parliament enacted the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026** (passed 10–11 Aug; **assent 13 August 2026**; enforced **25 August 2026**), repealing the 2021 Act. It creates a **five-member, judiciary-led National Tribunals Commission (NTC)** in New Delhi—Chairperson (former SC Judge/HC CJ), **two Judicial Members** (former HC CJ/Judge), and **two Technical Members** (25+ years' experience), serving five years up to age 70—overseeing **16 tribunals** (CAT, NGT, NCLAT, ITAT, CESTAT, NCDRC, AFT, SAT, etc.). It restores a **five-year tenure** (age caps: **70** Chairpersons, **67** Members), **drops the 50-year minimum age bar**, replaces two-name panels with **single-name SCSC recommendations (+ one waitlist)** under a **three-month** appointment limit, and builds a **National Tribunals Data Grid**.

**Persistent critiques (272nd Law Commission Report, 2017 & 2026 debates):** Four gaps linger as of **November 2026**: (1) the Union appoints NTC judicial members after mere *"consultation"* (not concurrence) with the CJI, appoints technical members unilaterally, and retains removal/complaint-screening powers; (2) **GSTAT sits outside the 2026 Act's First Schedule**, staying under Section 110 CGST Act (50-year bar, four-year term, two-name panel); (3) five-year tenure without guaranteed reappointment plus post-tenure appearance bars still deters top advocates; and (4) parent-ministry dependence, vacancies, and direct SC appeals bypassing High Courts persist.

## 3. Quasi-judicial bodies and specialised tribunals

**Quasi-judicial bodies** adjudicate legal rights outside regular courts using **principles of natural justice** (*audi alteram partem*, *nemo judex in causa sua*, reasoned orders) without rigid CPC/Evidence Act formality. They encompass tribunals alongside commissions and regulatory adjudicators.

| Body / Tribunal | Statutory Basis | Domain & Appeal Route |
|---|---|---|
| **ITAT** *(oldest, 1941)* | Income-tax Act, 1961 | Direct tax; appeal to High Court on substantial question of law |
| **CAT / SATs** | Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 | Public services; challenge before **HC Division Bench** (*L. Chandra Kumar*) |
| **NGT (2010)** | NGT Act, 2010 | Civil environmental disputes (7 Acts); appeal to **SC within 90 days** |
| **NCLT / NCLAT** | Companies Act 2013; IBC 2016 | Company law & insolvency; NCLAT appeal to **SC** |
| **GSTAT** *(Sept 2025)* | Sec. 109, CGST Act, 2017 | GST second appeals; Principal Bench → **SC**; State Benches → **HC** |
| **AFT / DRT–DRAT / TDSAT / SAT** | Sectoral Acts (2007, 1993, 1997, 1992) | Armed forces; debt recovery; telecom/cyber; SEBI/IRDAI/PFRDA |
| **Consumer Commissions; NHRC, CIC, SEBI/CCI** | CPA 2019; PHRA 1993; RTI 2005; Competition Act 2002 | Three-tier consumer redressal (NCDRC → SC); human rights, RTI & regulatory adjudication |

### National Green Tribunal (NGT) and GSTAT

Under the **NGT Act, 2010**, India became the **third country** (after **Australia** and **New Zealand**) with a dedicated environmental tribunal. With its Principal Bench in **New Delhi** and four Zonal Benches (**Bhopal, Pune, Kolkata, Chennai**), NGT pairs a judicial Chairperson (retired SC Judge/HC CJ) with 10–20 Judicial and 10–20 Expert Members (five-year non-renewable term).

NGT exercises civil jurisdiction over **seven Scheduled Acts** (Schedule I): **Water Act, 1974**; **Water Cess Act, 1977** *(repealed 2017)*; **Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980** *(renamed Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam in 2023)*; **Air Act, 1981**; **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**; **Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991**; and **Biological Diversity Act, 2002**. Guided by **natural justice** and three statutory principles (**Section 20**)—**sustainable development, precautionary principle, and polluter pays**—it targets disposal within **six months**, with SC appeal within **90 days**. In ***Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai v. Ankita Sinha* (2021)**, the Supreme Court affirmed NGT's ***suo motu*** jurisdiction.

> **Prelims pointer:** Classic trap—NGT has **NO jurisdiction** over the **Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972**, the **Indian Forest Act, 1927**, or the **Forest Rights Act, 2006**!

> **In the news:** Formally launched on **24 September 2025** under President **Justice Sanjaya Kumar Mishra** (former Jharkhand HC CJ), the **GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT)** began adjudicatory hearings on **16 February 2026** via its **Principal Bench in New Delhi** (also functioning as **National Appellate Authority for Advance Rulings [NAAAR]** from **April 2026**) and **31 State Benches across 45 locations**. Each full bench pairs **two judicial** and **two technical members** (one Centre, one State), advancing cooperative federalism and decongesting High Courts.

## 4. Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) and grassroots justice

With **~5.85 crore+ pending cases** across India—**~5.19 crore** in district and subordinate courts, **~65.58 lakh** in High Courts, and **92,755+** in the Supreme Court (NJDG, November 2026 & Lok Sabha reply, August 2026)—**Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR)** is indispensable. Its dual bedrock is **Article 39A** (inserted by **42nd Amendment, 1976**—equal justice and free legal aid) and **Section 89 CPC, 1908** (effective 2002), authorising court referrals to **arbitration, conciliation, judicial settlement (including Lok Adalat), and mediation** (*Afcons Infrastructure*, 2010).

### NALSA, Lok Adalats and Permanent Lok Adalats

The **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** (enforced **9 November 1995**) gave statutory status to **Lok Adalats** (first held at Una, Gujarat, March 1982) and created **NALSA**—with the **CJI** as Patron-in-Chief and the **second-seniormost SC judge** as Executive Chairman—above State (SLSA), District (DLSA), and Taluk authorities.

**Regular Lok Adalats ("People's Courts")** hold civil-court powers, charge **no court fee** (refunding fees paid in referred suits), settle disputes purely by **compromise**, and issue **final, binding, non-appealable civil court decrees** (narrow Art. 226/227 writ review only for fraud/jurisdiction—*Jalour Singh*, 2008). NALSA's quarterly **National Lok Adalats** deliver massive scale—resolving **2.84 crore cases** (March 2026), **2.07 crore** (May 2026), and **2.10 crore cases** worth **₹13,589 crore** (12 September 2026), largely pre-litigation matters.

In **2002**, Parliament added **Chapter VI-A (Sections 22A–22E)** creating **Permanent Lok Adalats (PLAs)** for pre-litigation Public Utility disputes:

| Feature | Regular Lok Adalat (Secs. 19–22) | Permanent Lok Adalat (Secs. 22A–22E, 2002 Amdt) |
|---|---|---|
| **Jurisdiction** | Pending or pre-litigation civil cases & **compoundable** criminal offences (**NO non-compoundable offences**) | **Pre-litigation ONLY** for **Public Utility Services** (transport, postal/telecom, power/water, sanitation, hospitals, insurance; plus notified banking, housing/real estate) |
| **Pecuniary limit** | None fixed | Up to **₹1 crore** (raised from ₹10 lakh on **20 March 2015**) |
| **If conciliation fails** | **Cannot adjudicate**; returns to court | **CAN adjudicate on merits** by majority (Sec. 22C(8)), unless dispute involves an offence |
| **Finality** | Final, binding, non-appealable decree | Final, binding, non-appealable decree; bars parallel suits once invoked |

> **Remember:** Regular Lok Adalats only *conciliate*; **Permanent Lok Adalats can adjudicate on merits** when conciliation fails (for Public Utility Service disputes up to **₹1 crore**).

### Gram Nyayalayas and Family Courts

**Gram Nyayalayas (Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008; enforced 2 October 2009):** Based on the **114th Law Commission Report (1986)**, these are **mobile village courts** at the **intermediate panchayat** level established by States in consultation with High Courts. Presided over by a **Nyayadhikari** (Judicial Magistrate of First Class [JMFC] rank—NOT a lay elder), they try **both civil and criminal** scheduled disputes via **summary procedure**, prioritise conciliation, and follow **natural justice**. Appeals lie within **30 days** to Sessions/District Courts (decided within **six months**).

> **Example:** Against a 5,000+ target, Department of Justice data (**2025–2026**) shows **489 Gram Nyayalayas notified** across 15 States/UTs and only **338 operational** (333 across 11 states as of October 2025, led by Uttar Pradesh [109] and Madhya Pradesh [89]; zero operational in notifying Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, J&K, and Ladakh), hampered by state reluctance, court overlap, and thin central funding (₹2 crore in FY 2025–26).

**Family Courts (Family Courts Act, 1984):** Mandatory in cities/towns with population **exceeding 10 lakh** (optional elsewhere). Handling matrimonial, maintenance, and custody disputes, they prioritise conciliation with counsellors, allow *in-camera* hearings, deny **legal representation as of right** (**Section 13**—court may allow counsel or *amicus curiae*), and provide appeals to a two-judge **High Court** bench within **30 days** (none against consent decrees).

## 5. Arbitration and mediation: statutory evolution (1996–2026)

### Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (and 2023–2026 updates)

Based on the **UNCITRAL Model Law**, the **1996 Act** covers domestic, international commercial, and foreign-award enforcement (New York/Geneva Conventions). Key reforms: **2015 Amendment** (**12-month** timeline + 6-month extension, arbitrator neutrality via Fifth/Seventh Schedules, narrowed "public policy" under Sec. 34); **2019 Amendment + IIAC Act** (Part IA creating the seven-member **Arbitration Council of India [ACI]** to grade institutions, plus the **India International Arbitration Centre [IIAC]**); and **2021 Amendment** (unconditional stay for *prima facie* **fraud/corruption**, dropping Eighth Schedule limits on foreign arbitrators).

Two five-judge Constitution Bench verdicts reshaped practice: ***Cox and Kings Ltd. v. SAP India* (December 2023)** upheld the **Group of Companies doctrine** binding non-signatory corporate affiliates where common intent exists; and ***Gayatri Balasamy v. ISG Novasoft Technologies Ltd.* (30 April 2025, 4:1)**—**CJI Sanjiv Khanna** writing for Justices Gavai, Sanjay Kumar, and Masih; **Justice K.V. Viswanathan** dissenting—held courts under **Sections 34 and 37** possess **limited power to modify arbitral awards** (without revisiting merits) in strictly **four situations**: **(1) severing** independent invalid portions (*omne majus continet in se minus*), **(2) correcting patent clerical, computational, or typographical errors**, **(3) modifying post-award interest** where warranted, and **(4) cautious Article 142** invocation.

> **In the news:** Implementation lags reform. As admitted to Parliament in **February 2026**—and flagged by **CJI Surya Kant** in July and September 2026 as a *"credibility deficit"* (with Indians remaining the third-largest foreign users of SIAC in 2024–2025)—the **Arbitration Council of India (ACI) remains unconstituted as of November 2026**. Likewise, the **Draft Arbitration and Conciliation (Amendment) Bill, 2024** (released **18 October 2024** after the **Dr. T.K. Viswanathan Committee** report, proposing **emergency arbitrators [Sec. 9A]**, opt-in **appellate arbitral tribunals [Sec. 34A]**, ODR hearings, and renaming the law simply the *Arbitration Act*) **remains a consultation draft not yet introduced in Parliament**.

### Mediation Act, 2023 and the Mediation Council of India (2026)

Receiving assent on **14 September 2023**, India's first standalone mediation statute provides **voluntary pre-litigation mediation** (note: **mandatory pre-institution mediation stays intact under Section 12A, Commercial Courts Act, 2015**); a **120-day** completion window (extendable by **60 days**); **online mediation (ODR)** with written consent; **community mediation** by a **three-member panel** for locality disputes; and **Mediated Settlement Agreements (MSAs)** enforceable like court decrees—challengeable within **90 days** (+90 days condonation) solely on **fraud, corruption, impersonation**, or **non-mediable subject matter** (First Schedule excludes criminal prosecutions, taxation, serious fraud, third-party rights).

> **In the news:** The Union Law Ministry formally notified the **Mediation Council of India (MCI)** under Section 31(1) on **27 August 2026** (headquartered in Delhi), and the MCI **commenced operations on 19 October 2026** at the Indian Law Institute, New Delhi, under Chairperson **Dr. Rajiv Mani** to frame regulations, register mediators, and accredit institutions—while key substantive provisions (pre-litigation mediation, statutory timelines, and Section 27 decree enforceability) await phased notification as of **November 2026**. One major gap also persists: the Act does **not implement the UN Singapore Convention on Mediation** for foreign-settled cross-border agreements—India **signed** it on **7 August 2019** but has **still not ratified it**.

| ADR Forum | Process & Binding Nature | Appeal / Challenge | Best Fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Arbitration** (1996 Act) | Adjudicatory; **binding award** | Narrow Sec. 34/37 set-aside & 4-ground modification (*Gayatri Balasamy*, 2025) | High-value commercial & cross-border contracts |
| **Mediation** (2023 Act) | Facilitative; voluntary **MSA = court decree** | 90 days on fraud, corruption, impersonation, non-mediable matter | Commercial, family, workplace & community ties |
| **Conciliation** | Proactive neutral proposes terms; voluntary binding settlement | Narrow validity/fraud grounds (aligned with Mediation Act) | Technical, labour & commercial settlements |
| **Regular Lok Adalat** | Compromise-only; **no fee**; binding only if settled | **No appeal** (narrow Art. 226/227 writ) | Mass pre-litigation, motor accident, cheque & compoundable cases |
| **Permanent Lok Adalat** | Pre-litigation conciliation + **merits adjudication** | Final & binding; no regular appeal | Public Utility Service disputes up to **₹1 crore** |

## 6. Building an ADR-first justice ecosystem: way forward

Shifting India from a **litigation-first** to an **ADR-first** system demands institutional follow-through:
- **Strengthen tribunal autonomy:** Give the CJI real concurrence in NTC appointments under the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026**, bring **GSTAT** under the NTC umbrella, and address five-year tenure disincentives.
- **Close ADR implementation gaps:** Operationalise or reform the **ACI**, enact the Viswanathan-panel **Arbitration Amendment Bill**, scale **IIAC** and the newly functional **MCI**, and **ratify the Singapore Convention on Mediation**.
- **Scale ODR & grassroots justice:** Integrate NITI Aayog's ODR blueprint with e-Courts Phase III for high-volume small disputes, incentivise states to operationalise notified **Gram Nyayalayas**, and apply ***Gayatri Balasamy* (2025)** with strict judicial restraint to preserve arbitral finality.

> **Mains angle:** Link GS-2 answers on tribunals and ADR to **ease of doing business**, **Article 39A**, and **separation of powers**—contrasting **2025–2026 milestones** (**GSTAT**, ***Madras Bar Association* Nov 2025** → **Tribunals Reforms Act 2026** & NTC, ***Gayatri Balasamy* April 2025**, **MCI** operationalisation in **Oct 2026**, 2+ crore National Lok Adalat settlements) against remaining gaps (unconstituted **ACI**, pending Draft Arbitration Bill, unratified **Singapore Convention**, GSTAT outside NTC, only 338 operational **Gram Nyayalayas**).
