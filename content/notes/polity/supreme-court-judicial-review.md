## 1. Organisation, Composition and Appointment of Judges (Part V, Articles 124–128)

At the summit of India's constitutional architecture stands the **Supreme Court of India**, an institution deliberately engineered by the Constituent Assembly to perform a wider and heavier constitutional mandate than almost any other apex court in the democratic world. Governed by **Part V, Chapter IV (`Articles 124` to `147`)**, the Supreme Court simultaneously discharges four distinct institutional roles: it is the **final interpreter and custodian of the Constitution**, the **exclusive federal umpire** arbitrating disputes between the Union and the States, the **direct protector and guarantor of Fundamental Rights** (`Article 32`), and the **highest general court of appeal** across all civil, criminal, revenue, and statutory disputes in the Republic.

As Sir Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar famously remarked in the Constituent Assembly, *"The Supreme Court in the Indian Union has more powers than any Supreme Court in any part of the world."* While the Supreme Court of the United States confines itself chiefly to federal constitutional questions and federal statutes, and the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom cannot invalidate an Act of Parliament, the Supreme Court of India unites the constitutional review power of an American apex court with the comprehensive appellate jurisdiction of a British final court of appeal—and adds to both an original federal jurisdiction, an advisory remit, and an extraordinary equitable mandate under `Article 142`.


### Single Integrated Judiciary and Historical Origin

A defining structural feature of Indian federalism is the absence of a dual court system. In the **United States**, the federal principle extends all the way through the judicial branch: federal courts enforce federal legislation and the federal Constitution, whereas each state maintains its own autonomous judicial hierarchy—culminating in a State Supreme Court—to enforce state laws and state constitutions. India's framers consciously rejected this American bifurcation and adopted instead the **single integrated judicial system** pioneered under the **Government of India Act, 1935**.

Under India's single integrated pyramid, there is no separate class of Union courts and State courts. One unified chain of courts—the Supreme Court at the apex, the twenty-five High Courts at the intermediate state level, and the District and Subordinate Courts at the base—interprets and enforces **both** Central laws enacted by Parliament and State laws enacted by State Legislatures. Uniformity of constitutional interpretation, procedural coherence across India's vast territory, and equal protection of citizens' rights regardless of state boundaries were the decisive reasons behind this choice. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar explained the architectural rationale with characteristic precision while introducing the Draft Constitution:

> "The Indian Federation though a Dual Polity has no Dual Judiciary at all. The High Courts and the Supreme Court form one single integrated Judiciary having jurisdiction and providing remedies in all cases arising under the constitutional law, the civil law or the criminal law." — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, 4 November 1948

Historically, the Supreme Court of India was formally inaugurated on **28 January 1950**—two days after the Constitution commenced—in the Chamber of Princes inside the Parliament House, before moving to its iconic Tilak Marg complex in New Delhi in 1958. **Justice Harilal Jekisundas (H.J.) Kania** took office as the first Chief Justice of India alongside seven companion judges. Institutionally, the new apex court absorbed the mantle of two predecessor tribunals:

- **The Federal Court of India (1937–1950):** Established under the Government of India Act, 1935, it had exercised limited original federal jurisdiction and narrow constitutional appellate jurisdiction from High Courts, yet remained subordinate to London in civil and criminal appeals.
- **The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council (London):** Until 1949, the Privy Council served as the ultimate appellate tribunal for British India. Through the **Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act, 1949** (enacted by the Constituent Assembly acting as the Dominion Legislature), India severed appellate subordination to Britain, transferring all pending appeals and supreme appellate authority to the new Supreme Court (`Article 135`).

```flow

Supreme Court of India (Part V, Arts. 124–147) | Apex constitutional court, federal umpire & final court of appeal; law declared binds ALL courts (Art. 141)
25 High Courts (Part VI, Arts. 214–231) | Enforce Union & State laws; writ power for FRs & legal rights (Art. 226); judicial & administrative superintendence (Arts. 227, 235)
District & Subordinate Courts (Part VI, Arts. 233–237) | Civil Courts (District Judge) || Criminal Courts (Sessions Judge & Judicial Magistrates — separated from Executive under Art. 50) || Specialised Statutory Tribunals (Arts. 323A–323B — subject to HC/SC review per L. Chandra Kumar, 1997)
```

### Sanctioned Strength and Statutory Evolution (Article 124(1))

**`Article 124(1)`** originally declared that there shall be a Supreme Court of India consisting of a Chief Justice of India and, *"until Parliament by law prescribes a larger number, of not more than seven other Judges."* That single clause embeds one of the most frequently tested UPSC Prelims rules: the Constitution established an **initial strength of 8 judges (1 CJI + 7 puisne judges)**, and vested **Parliament—by ordinary legislation, NOT an `Article 368` constitutional amendment**—with the authority to increase the sanctioned strength as litigation volumes grew. Notice also a subtle textual boundary: `Article 124(1)` empowers Parliament to prescribe a *"larger number"*; it does not contemplate shrinking the Court below its original constitutional baseline.

Parliament first exercised this statutory power by enacting the **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956**, which raised the count of puisne judges from 7 to 10 (total 11). Over the next seven decades, Parliament amended this 1956 parent statute seven times to keep pace with expanding population, constitutional rights jurisprudence, and appellate filings. The **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019** raised the strength from 31 to **34 (1 CJI + 33 puisne judges)**—a milestone figure that governed the Court for seven years until Parliament enacted a historic expansion in **August 2026** raising the sanctioned ceiling to **38 (1 CJI + 37 puisne judges)**.

### Appointment of Judges and Evolution of the Collegium System (Article 124(2))

No issue in Indian constitutional law has generated sharper institutional friction between the Executive and the Judiciary than the method of appointing Supreme Court and High Court judges. The bare constitutional text of **`Article 124(2)`** appears deceptively concise:

1. Every Judge of the Supreme Court shall be **appointed by the President** by warrant under his hand and seal, **after consultation** with such of the Judges of the Supreme Court and of the High Courts in the States as the President may deem necessary for the purpose.
2. **Proviso to `Article 124(2)`:** In the case of appointment of a Judge **other than the Chief Justice**, the **Chief Justice of India shall always be consulted** (mandatory consultation).

In the Constituent Assembly, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar steered a deliberate middle course among three global models. He rejected unilateral executive appointment (the then British model) as dangerous to judicial independence; he rejected American-style Senate confirmation hearings as likely to drag judicial appointments into partisan politics; and—crucially—he equally rejected amendments demanding the *"concurrence"* of the Chief Justice of India, warning against concentrating an unreviewable veto in a single individual:

> "I personally feel no doubt that the Chief Justice is a very eminent person. But after all, the Chief Justice is a man with all the failings, all the sentiments and all the prejudices which we as common people have, and I think to allow the Chief Justice practically a veto upon the appointment of Judges is really to transfer the authority to the Chief Justice which we are not prepared to vest in the President or the Government of the day." — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, 24 May 1949

#### Pre-Collegium Conventions and the Supersession Crises (1950–1977)

For the first twenty-three years of the Republic (1950–1973), an informal consensus sustained harmonious appointments: the Union Executive almost invariably accepted the CJI's recommendations for puisne judges, and **the senior-most judge of the Supreme Court was automatically appointed as Chief Justice of India** by convention. That bipartisan equilibrium shattered twice during the 1970s amid escalating executive–judiciary conflict over property rights, constitutional amendments, and the Emergency:

- **First Supersession (April 1973):** One day after the 13-judge Constitution Bench delivered *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (24 April 1973) affirming the Basic Structure doctrine, the Union Government superseded three senior judges who had formed part of the *Kesavananda* majority—**Justices J.M. Shelat, K.S. Hegde, and A.N. Grover**—and appointed **Justice A.N. Ray** (fourth in seniority, who had sided with the government) as the 14th Chief Justice of India. All three superseded judges resigned in protest.
- **Second Supersession (January 1977):** Following the infamous Emergency ruling in *ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla* (1976), where **Justice H.R. Khanna** authored his historic lone dissent defending `Article 21` personal liberty, the government bypassed Justice Khanna and appointed **Justice M.H. Beg** as Chief Justice of India. Justice Khanna resigned immediately.

These two episodes—coupled with punitive mass transfers of sixteen High Court judges during the Emergency and the controversial Law Ministry circular of March 1981 seeking advance consent from additional HC judges for transfer—convinced India's legal community that relying purely on executive self-restraint could no longer guarantee an independent judiciary. Out of this constitutional anxiety emerged the **Four Judges Cases**, which transformed the word *"consultation"* in `Article 124(2)` into the modern **Collegium system**—a term that **nowhere appears in the text of the Constitution**.

#### The First Judges Case: S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981)

Decided by a seven-judge Constitution Bench (by a 4–3 majority led by Justice P.N. Bhagwati), ***S.P. Gupta v. Union of India* (1981)** addressed the transfer of High Court judges and non-confirmation of additional judges following the Law Minister's 1981 circular. Interpreting `Articles 124(2)` and `217(1)` literally, the majority ruled that **"consultation" does NOT mean "concurrence"**; it denotes only a full, effective, and meaningful exchange of views based on identical material facts. Consequently, the **ultimate primacy rested with the Union Executive**, which could reject the CJI's recommendation for "cogent reasons." For the next twelve years (1981–1993), the Central Government enjoyed decisive ascendancy in judicial appointments.

#### The Second Judges Case: SCAORA v. Union of India (1993)

Dissatisfied with mounting executive influence over the Bench, a nine-judge Constitution Bench in ***Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association (SCAORA) v. Union of India* (1993)** overruled *S.P. Gupta* by a **7–2 majority** (lead opinion by Justice J.S. Verma). Reading `Article 124(2)` purposively alongside the structural imperative of judicial independence, the Court radically redefined the appointment framework:

- **Consultation Equals Concurrence & Judicial Primacy:** In an integrated participatory process, the opinion of the **Chief Justice of India enjoys primacy**—meaning no appointment to the Supreme Court or a High Court can be made unless it conforms with the CJI's opinion.
- **Birth of the Collegium (CJI + 2):** Crucially, the majority prevented individual arbitrariness (answering Ambedkar's 1949 fear) by clarifying that the "opinion of the CJI" is **not his personal individual opinion**, but an **institutional opinion formed collectively** after consulting the **two senior-most judges of the Supreme Court** (creating the original 3-member Collegium).
- **Codification of the CJI Seniority Rule:** To eliminate future 1973/1977-style supersessions permanently, the Court declared that the **senior-most judge of the Supreme Court** considered fit to hold the office **shall be appointed as Chief Justice of India**.
- **Binding Reiteration:** The Executive could send a recommended name back once for reconsideration with cogent material; however, if the Collegium **unanimously reiterated** the recommendation, the President was constitutionally bound to make the appointment.

#### The Third Judges Case: In Re Special Reference No. 1 of 1998

When President K.R. Narayanan sought clarification under `Article 143(1)` after then CJI M.M. Punchhi allegedly forwarded recommendations without consulting colleagues properly, a **unanimous nine-judge Constitution Bench** (authored by Justice S.P. Bharucha) refined and institutionalised the Collegium architecture into the exact form operating today:

- **Plurality of Judges (CJI + 4 for SC):** For Supreme Court appointments (and transfer of High Court judges), the Collegium was expanded to a **five-member body: the CJI plus the four senior-most judges of the Supreme Court**. (If the successor CJI is not already among those four senior-most judges, the successor CJI must also be included as a sixth consultee so continuity is maintained.)
- **High Court Appointments (CJI + 2):** For appointments to High Courts, the SC Collegium comprises the **CJI plus the two senior-most SC judges** (acting upon proposals initiated by the respective High Court Collegium of the HC Chief Justice + two senior-most HC judges).
- **Mandatory Consultee Judge Rule:** In recommending an elevation to the Supreme Court, the Collegium must also obtain the written views of the **senior-most SC judge hailing from the candidate's parent High Court** (if not already on the Collegium).
- **Two-Dissent Veto Rule:** The **sole individual opinion of the CJI does not constitute valid consultation** and is not binding on the government. Moreover, if **even two judges** of the five-member SC Collegium express an **adverse opinion** against a nominee, the CJI **shall not forward that recommendation** to the Centre.
- **Written Record & Narrow Review:** Every opinion must be recorded in writing and transmitted to the Union Government; judicial review of an appointment is tightly restricted to lack of eligibility or failure to follow the mandatory consultative process (never the subjective merits of selection).

#### The 99th Amendment, NJAC Act (2014) and the Fourth Judges Case (2015)

Mounting bipartisan criticism that the Collegium operated as an unaccountable, self-perpetuating club ("judges appointing judges") culminated in Parliament unanimously enacting the **`99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014`** (ratified by more than half of the State Legislatures and assented to on 31 December 2014) alongside the **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act, 2014**. Inserting **`Articles 124A, 124B, and 124C`**, the amendment replaced the Collegium with a six-member constitutional body—the **NJAC**—consisting of:

1. The **Chief Justice of India** (ex-officio Chairperson);
2. **Two senior-most Judges** of the Supreme Court next to the CJI;
3. The **Union Minister of Law and Justice** (ex-officio); and
4. **Two "Eminent Persons"** nominated for a non-renewable three-year term by a selection committee comprising the Prime Minister, the CJI, and the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha (or leader of the single largest opposition party), with a mandate that **at least one** eminent person belong to the SC, ST, OBC, minority community, or be a woman.

Crucially, the NJAC Act contained a **two-member veto clause** (`Section 5(2)` and `6(6)`): the Commission could not recommend any candidate if **any two members** objected—meaning the Union Law Minister plus one lay eminent person, or both eminent persons together, could veto the unanimous choice of all three Supreme Court judges!

> [!case]
> **Fourth Judges Case — *Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India* (16 October 2015, 5-Judge Bench, 4–1)**
> - **Bench:** Justices J.S. Khehar (lead opinion), Madan B. Lokur, Kurian Joseph, Adarsh Kumar Goel (majority), and **Justice J. Chelameswar (sole dissent)**.
> - **Majority Holding (4–1):** Struck down **both** the `99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014` and the NJAC Act, 2014 as *ultra vires* the **Basic Structure** of the Constitution. Held that **judicial primacy in appointments is an integral, inseparable part of Judicial Independence**; inclusion of the Union Law Minister (representing the single largest litigant before the courts—the Government of India) risked reciprocity and executive interference, while the vague term *"eminent persons"* paired with the two-member veto could paralyse judicial consensus. Consequently, the pre-existing **Collegium system automatically revived**.
> - **Justice J. Chelameswar's Historic Dissent:** Emphasised that *"transparency is a vital factor in constitutional governance"*, criticised Collegium proceedings as *"opaque and inaccessible to both the public and history"*, and warned that equating *"primacy of the judiciary"* with *"independence of the judiciary"* had no warrant in the Constituent Assembly Debates.
> - **Consequential Order (December 2015):** Acknowledging genuine flaws in Collegium functioning, the same Bench directed the Union Government to draft a revised **Memorandum of Procedure (MoP)** in consultation with the CJI focusing on four pillars: **eligibility criteria, transparency, a permanent secretariat, and a complaints mechanism**.

#### Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) Deadlock, 2025 Transparency Push and Diversity Deficit

More than a decade after the December 2015 order, the revised **Memorandum of Procedure (MoP)** remains stalled between the Centre and the SC Collegium over two sticking points: the Centre's proposed **"national security and public interest" veto clause** against reiterated nominees, and control of the **permanent Collegium Secretariat**. Without binding timelines, the Union Executive routinely exercises a *de facto* **"pocket veto"** by sitting on reiterated files or **segregating (cherry-picking)** names from unified batches—distorting inter-se seniority.

Two structural critiques of the Collegium—**opacity** and **under-representation**—reached an inflection point in **2025–2026**. Following the March 2025 High Court cash-discovery row, the Supreme Court under outgoing CJI Sanjiv Khanna enacted two landmark self-regulatory disclosures:

- **Mandatory Public Asset Disclosures (1 April 2025):** The **Full Court** resolved that **asset statements of all SC judges (including the CJI)** shall be mandatorily published on the Court's website—upgrading the 1997 confidential and 2009 voluntary resolutions.
- **Appointment Process & Collegium Records (5 May 2025):** The SC uploaded the complete HC/SC appointment workflow alongside tabular records of HC Collegium approvals (**9 November 2022 – 5 May 2025**), detailing Bar vs Service source, recommendation/notification dates, **social category (SC/ST/OBC/Minority/Woman)**, and **judicial-family ties** (confronting *"Uncle Judge"* concerns).

Yet diversity across the higher judiciary remains starkly skewed. Since **Justice M. Fathima Beevi** (October 1989), only **13 women** have ever served on the Supreme Court. As of **November 2026**, only **3 of 37 sitting SC judges** are women—**Justice B.V. Nagarathna** (Collegium member, slated to be India's **first woman CJI** in **September 2027** for 36 days), **Justice V. Mohana** (2 June 2026, second woman elevated directly from the Bar after Justice Indu Malhotra), and **Justice Sunita Agarwal** (6 October 2026). Parliamentary data in early 2026 further showed that of **847 High Court judges** appointed during 2018–January 2026, only **33 were SC, 17 ST, 104 OBC, 46 minorities, and 130 women**.

> [!debate]
> **Should India Retain the Collegium System or Move to an Independent Appointments Commission?**
> - **Defence of the Collegium:** In a polity where the Union and State Governments are parties to nearly half of all constitutional and appellate litigation, keeping appointment primacy outside executive control protects courts from partisan packing and preserves the Basic Structure. Moreover, peer evaluation by senior judges best assesses forensic competence and courtroom integrity, and the April–May 2025 disclosures prove the Collegium can reform from within.
> - **Critique & Case for an Independent Commission:** India is virtually the only major constitutional democracy where sitting judges hold final self-appointment primacy; both the **Law Commission (214th Report)** and **Justice Chelameswar's 2015 dissent** highlighted the absence of democratic legitimacy, written eligibility benchmarks, formal background-vetting machinery, and social diversity. A balanced constitutional compromise—echoing the UK's **Judicial Appointments Commission (JAC)** or South Africa's **Judicial Service Commission**—would retain a judicial majority while embedding transparent applications, independent vetting, and strict statutory timelines for the Executive.

### Qualifications, Oath, Tenure, Salaries and Post-Retirement Restrictions (Articles 124–125)

The Constitution deliberately keeps formal eligibility rules crisp while insulating service conditions from political pressure.

#### Qualifications for Appointment (Article 124(3))

A person is qualified for appointment as a Judge of the Supreme Court only if they are a **citizen of India** and satisfy **any one** of the following three alternative criteria:

1. Has been for **at least five years a Judge of a High Court** (or of two or more High Courts in succession); **OR**
2. Has been for **at least ten years an Advocate of a High Court** (or of two or more High Courts in succession)—computing periods during which the person held judicial office after becoming an advocate as part of this ten-year stretch; **OR**
3. Is, in the opinion of the President, a **"distinguished jurist."**

Three sharp Prelims traps cluster inside `Article 124(3)`: first, the Constitution prescribes **NO minimum age** for appointment as a Supreme Court judge (unlike 25/30/35 years for MPs, Governors, and the President); second, the **"distinguished jurist"** channel—intended by framers like H.V. Kamath to bring eminent legal academics onto the Bench—has **NEVER been used even once** since 1950; and third, the **"distinguished jurist" criterion exists ONLY for the Supreme Court and is NOT available for High Court appointments** under `Article 217(2)`!

#### Oath or Affirmation (Article 124(6) and Third Schedule)

Before entering upon office, every Supreme Court judge must make and subscribe an oath or affirmation before the **President of India** (or some person appointed in that behalf by the President—commonly the CJI for puisne judges) in the exact form set out in the **Third Schedule** (Form IV). In this oath, a judge solemnly swears to:

- Bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution of India as by law established;
- Uphold the **sovereignty and integrity of India** (added by the **`16th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963`**);
- Duly and faithfully and to the best of their ability, knowledge, and judgment perform the duties of the office **without fear or favour, affection or ill-will**; and
- **Uphold the Constitution and the laws.**

#### Tenure, Resignation and Age Determination (Articles 124(2) and 124(2A))

The Constitution does **not** fix a minimum tenure for a Supreme Court judge—explaining why several CJIs have served for only a few weeks or months (the shortest being Justice Kamal Narain Singh for 17 days in 1991, and the longest being Justice Y.V. Chandrachud for over seven years, 1978–1985). Instead, `Article 124(2)` makes three clear tenure stipulations:

1. **Retirement Age:** A judge holds office until attaining the age of **65 years** (contrasting with **62 years** for High Court judges under `Article 217(1)`).
2. **Age-Dispute Resolution (`Article 124(2A)`):** Inserted by the **`15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963`**, `Article 124(2A)` specifies that the age of a Supreme Court judge shall be determined by **such authority and in such manner as Parliament may by law provide**. Contrast this immediately with High Court judges under `Article 217(3)`, where age disputes are decided directly by the **President after consultation with the CJI**, whose decision is final!
3. **Voluntary Resignation (`Art. 124(2)` proviso (a)):** A judge may resign at any time by writing under their hand addressed to the **President of India**. As a five-judge Constitution Bench established in ***Union of India v. Gopal Chandra Mishra* (1978)**, a judge's unilateral resignation takes effect *ex proprio vigore* (by its own force) immediately upon communication—or from a specified prospective date—without needing "acceptance" by the President. (A judge may withdraw a prospective resignation only *before* the specified effective date arrives.)

#### Salaries, Allowances, Pension and Service Conditions (Article 125)

Under **`Article 125`**, the salaries, privileges, allowances, leave of absence, and pensions of Supreme Court judges are determined from time to time by **Parliament by law**—currently governed by the **Supreme Court Judges (Salaries and Conditions of Service) Act, 1958**, as amended by the **High Court and Supreme Court Judges (Salaries and Conditions of Service) Amendment Act, 2018** (effective 1 January 2016 following the 7th Central Pay Commission):

- **Chief Justice of India:** **₹2,80,000 per month** (fixed basic pay, plus sumptuary allowance, rent-free official residence, medical, car, and travel entitlements).
- **Other Supreme Court Judges:** **₹2,50,000 per month** (equal to the Chief Justice of a High Court; puisne High Court judges draw ₹2,25,000 per month).
- **Pension:** Retired judges receive a monthly pension equal to **50 per cent** of their last drawn salary, plus post-retirement domestic help and secretarial allowances.
- **Constitutional Protection:** Neither the privileges, allowances, leave rights, nor pension of a judge may be **varied to their disadvantage after appointment** (`Article 125(2)` proviso). The **sole constitutional exception** allowing salary reduction is a Proclamation of **Financial Emergency under `Article 360(4)(b)`**. Moreover, all salaries, allowances, and pensions are **charged upon the Consolidated Fund of India** (`Article 112(3)(d)`).

### Acting Chief Justice, Ad-hoc Judges and Retired Judges (Articles 126–128)

To ensure that the Supreme Court never stalls due to sudden vacancy, absence, illness, or shortfall of quorum on Constitution Benches, the Constitution embeds three distinct temporary-judge mechanisms across `Articles 126`, `127`, and `128`. UPSC Prelims frequently swaps who appoints whom and whose consent is required across these three provisions.

- **Acting Chief Justice of India (`Article 126`):** When the office of the CJI is **vacant**, or when the CJI is **absent** or otherwise **unable to perform the duties** of the office, those duties are performed by such one of the **other Judges of the Supreme Court** as the **President may appoint** for the purpose. Notice that **the President**—NOT the outgoing CJI—makes this appointment (conventionally selecting the senior-most sitting SC judge).
- **Ad-hoc Judges (`Article 127`):** If at any time there is a **lack of a quorum** of permanent Judges of the Supreme Court to hold or continue any session of the Court, the **Chief Justice of India** may request in writing the attendance of a **sitting Judge of a High Court** who is duly qualified for appointment as a Supreme Court judge. Two mandatory preconditions apply: **(a) previous consent of the President**, and **(b) consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court concerned**. While attending Supreme Court sittings, the ad-hoc judge must prioritise SC duties over other duties and exercises **all the jurisdiction, powers, and privileges of a Judge of the Supreme Court**.
- **Attendance of Retired Judges (`Article 128`):** At any time, the **Chief Justice of India** may request a **retired Judge of the Supreme Court**, a retired Judge of the Federal Court, or a **retired Judge of a High Court** duly qualified for appointment as an SC judge, to sit and act as a Judge of the Supreme Court for a temporary period. Two mandatory preconditions apply: **(a) previous consent of the President**, and **(b) consent of the retired judge concerned**. The retired judge receives allowances determined by the **President** and enjoys full jurisdiction, powers, and privileges while sitting—**HOWEVER, `Article 128` explicitly stipulates that they shall NOT otherwise be deemed to be a Judge of the Supreme Court!** (Notice the corresponding High Court provision for retired judges is `Article 224A`, whereas ad-hoc judges under `Art. 127` exist *only* for the SC; High Courts instead have *Additional* and *Acting* judges under `Art. 224`!)

```compare
# Acting CJI (Art. 126) vs Ad-hoc Judges (Art. 127) vs Retired Judges (Art. 128)
Acting CJI (Article 126) | Ad-hoc Judge (Article 127) | Retired Judge (Article 128)
Triggering Situation :: CJI office vacant, or CJI absent / unable to perform duties | Lack of quorum of permanent SC judges to hold/continue a session | Temporary caseload/institutional need at any time
Appointing / Requesting Authority :: President of India appoints | Chief Justice of India requests in writing | Chief Justice of India requests
Eligible Pool :: Any sitting Judge of the Supreme Court | Sitting Judge of a High Court qualified for SC appointment | Retired SC Judge, retired Federal Court Judge, or qualified retired HC Judge
Consent / Consultation Needed :: None specified in text (convention: senior-most SC judge) | Previous consent of President + consultation with HC Chief Justice | Previous consent of President + consent of the retired judge
Constitutional Status :: Performs full duties of CJI until CJI resumes/is appointed | Full SC jurisdiction, powers & privileges; SC sittings take priority | Full SC powers & President-fixed allowances; NOT deemed to be an SC judge
```

### Seat of the Supreme Court, Regional Benches Debate and Constitution Bench (Articles 130, 145)

#### Seat of the Supreme Court and the Regional Benches Debate (Article 130)

Under **`Article 130`**, *"The Supreme Court shall sit in Delhi or in such other place or places, as the Chief Justice of India may, with the approval of the President, from time to time, appoint."* Two legal features make `Article 130` a perennial Prelims and Mains favourite: first, the **initiative lies with the CJI** (acting with Presidential approval—not Parliament unilaterally); second, the provision is purely **optional and enabling**, meaning no court or citizen can compel the CJI or the President via writ to establish an outside bench.

For decades, legal reformers have debated invoking `Article 130`—or amending the Constitution—to overcome the acute geographic inequity of having India's sole final court of appeal clustered in New Delhi. Empirical studies show that a disproportionately high share of Special Leave Petitions originate from High Courts close to Delhi (Delhi, Punjab & Haryana, Allahabad, Uttarakhand), while litigants from southern, eastern, and northeastern states face prohibitive travel and legal costs.

- **Pro-Regional Benches / National Court of Appeal:** Both the **125th Law Commission Report (1988)** and the landmark **229th Law Commission Report (2009)**, along with repeated Parliamentary Standing Committee reports (2004, 2005, 2006, 2023) and proposals by former Vice-Presidents and Attorneys General (notably K.K. Venugopal), advocated splitting the apex court's dual role: retaining a **Constitutional Division exclusively in New Delhi** while establishing **four Regional Cassation Benches (or a National Court of Appeal)** in **Delhi (North), Chennai/Hyderabad (South), Kolkata (East), and Mumbai (West)** to hear routine civil, criminal, and statutory appeals.
- **Supreme Court Full Court Resistance:** The Supreme Court Full Court has consistently rejected regional bench proposals (in resolutions passed in **1999, 2001, 2004, 2006, and 2010**), warning that splitting the apex court would erode its **unitary institutional character**, produce conflicting regional interpretations of national statutes, undermine cohesion among judges, and lower the prestige of the Court. In ***V. Vasanthakumar v. H.C. Bhatia* (2016)**, a three-judge Bench referred the question of establishing a **National Court of Appeal** to a Constitution Bench (still pending adjudication), while proponents note that post-2020 **hybrid virtual hearings** partially mitigate geographic barriers without fragmenting the Bench.

#### Rules of Procedure, Constitution Bench and Master of the Roster (Article 145)

**`Article 145`** empowers the Supreme Court, with the **approval of the President**, to make rules regulating its own practice and procedure (currently the **Supreme Court Rules, 2013**). Three core sub-clauses deserve exact Prelims mastery:

1. **Constitution Bench Quorum (`Article 145(3)`):** The **minimum number of Judges** who are to sit for the purpose of deciding any case involving a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution**, or for hearing any Presidential Reference under **`Article 143`**, shall be **five**. In practice, larger Constitution Benches of 7, 9, 11, or 13 judges are convened whenever an earlier bench of equal or smaller strength must be reconsidered—the largest in Indian history being the **13-judge Bench in *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)** (followed by 11 judges in *Golaknath* 1967 and *T.M.A. Pai* 2002, and 9 judges in *Indra Sawhney* 1992, *S.R. Bommai* 1994, *I.R. Coelho* 2007, *K.S. Puttaswamy* 2017, *Property Owners Association* Nov 2024, and the ongoing Sabarimala reference).
2. **Open-Court Pronouncement (`Article 145(4)`):** No judgment shall be delivered by the Supreme Court save in **open court**, and no `Article 143` report shall be made save in accordance with an opinion delivered in open court.
3. **Majority Rule and Right to Dissent (`Article 145(5)`):** All judgments and opinions are delivered with the **concurrence of a majority** of the Judges present at the hearing; however, every judge who disagrees retains an unfettered constitutional right to deliver a **dissenting judgment or opinion**—a tradition that has produced some of India's finest constitutional beacons (such as Justice Fazl Ali in *A.K. Gopalan*, Justice H.R. Khanna in *ADM Jabalpur*, and Justice Subba Rao in *Kharak Singh*).

Alongside `Article 145`, an unwritten but immensely powerful administrative convention designates the Chief Justice of India as the **"Master of the Roster"**—enjoying exclusive administrative authority to constitute benches and assign cases. Reaffirmed decisively in ***Campaign for Judicial Accountability and Reforms v. Union of India* (2017)**, ***Asok Pande v. Supreme Court of India* (2018)**, and ***Shanti Bhushan v. Supreme Court of India* (2018)** (which rejected a plea to vest roster allocation in the five-member Collegium), the doctrine holds that even though the CJI is only *primus inter pares* (first among equals) on the judicial side, administrative discipline requires a single head to manage listings. Nevertheless, the unprecedented **12 January 2018 press conference** by four senior-most judges (Justices Chelameswar, Gogoi, Lokur, and Kurian Joseph) alleging selective listing of politically sensitive matters sparked an enduring Mains debate on automating roster allocation and institutionalising transparent rules for listing Constitution Benches.

> [!prelims]
> **Six Classic UPSC Prelims Traps in Articles 124–130:**
> 1. **Strength increase:** Parliament raises SC judge strength by **ordinary law** (`Art. 124(1)`), **NOT** an `Article 368` constitutional amendment (now **38 judges** via the August 2026 Act).
> 2. **No minimum age:** The Constitution fixes a retirement age of **65 years**, but prescribes **NO minimum age** and **NO fixed minimum tenure**.
> 3. **Distinguished Jurist:** Available **ONLY for Supreme Court** appointments (`Art. 124(3)`), **NEVER for High Courts** (`Art. 217(2)`)—and **never used** so far.
> 4. **Age dispute split:** For SC judges, Parliament prescribes the age-determination authority by law (`Art. 124(2A)`, 15th Amend); for HC judges, the **President decides after consulting the CJI** (`Art. 217(3)`).
> 5. **Who appoints temporary judges:** **Acting CJI (`Art. 126`)** is appointed by the **President**; **Ad-hoc (`Art. 127`)** and **Retired (`Art. 128`)** judges are requested by the **CJI** with the President's prior consent.
> 6. **Oath schedule:** SC judges take oath under the **Third Schedule** before the President (unlike the President's own oath in `Article 60` and Governor's in `Article 159`).

## 2. Removal and Independence of the Supreme Court

Judicial independence is not a personal privilege bestowed upon judges; it is the indispensable structural shield that protects ordinary citizens and federal units against executive overreach and legislative majoritarianism. Yet absolute insulation without any mechanism to address corruption, incapacity, or grave misconduct would turn independence into unaccountable autocracy. The Constitution resolves this classic tension by pairing an **extraordinarily rigorous parliamentary removal process** (`Article 124(4)–(5)`) with **ten interlocking constitutional safeguards** that keep sitting judges free from political inducement or intimidation.

### Constitutional Grounds and Statutory Procedure for Removal (Article 124(4)–(5))

Unlike Ministers, Governors, or the Attorney General, a Judge of the Supreme Court does **not** hold office during the pleasure of the President. Under **`Article 124(4)`**, a Judge of the Supreme Court can be removed from office **ONLY by an order of the President**, passed **after an address by each House of Parliament** supported by a **Special Majority** has been presented to the President **in the same session** for such removal, strictly on one or both of **ONLY two constitutional grounds**:

1. **Proved misbehaviour**; or
2. **Incapacity**.

Neither "misbehaviour" nor "incapacity" is exhaustively defined in the Constitution. In ***Krishna Swami v. Union of India* (1992)** and the Justice P.B. Sawant Inquiry Committee report (1992), "misbehaviour" was understood as wilful abuse of judicial office, wilful misconduct, corruption, lack of integrity, or conduct that brings the judicial office into serious disrepute—far beyond mere errors of judgment or honest legal mistakes. "Incapacity" signifies permanent physical or mental infirmity rendering a judge unfit to discharge judicial duties. Crucially, the word **"proved"** before "misbehaviour" means Parliament cannot remove a judge on mere political allegations; the charges must first be established through an independent statutory investigation.

> [!remember]
> **"Removal" vs "Impeachment" — Classic UPSC Prelims Distinction:**
> In everyday journalism, removing a Supreme Court or High Court judge is casually called *"impeachment."* For UPSC Prelims, remember strictly that **the word "impeachment" does NOT appear in `Article 124(4)` (SC) or `Article 217(1)(b)` (HC)**—both use the word **"removed."** In the entire Constitution of India, the term **"impeachment"** is reserved **exclusively for the President of India (`Article 61`)**! Furthermore, Presidential impeachment requires **2/3rd of the TOTAL membership** of each House (and can span sessions), whereas judicial removal requires **>50% of total membership + ≥2/3rd present and voting** in **each House in the SAME session**, preceded by a statutory three-member inquiry under the 1968 Act.

#### Step-by-Step Procedure Under the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968

Exercising its enabling power under **`Article 124(5)`**, Parliament enacted the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968**, which lays down the exact seven-step statutory pathway governing the removal of both Supreme Court judges and High Court judges (via `Article 218`):

1. **Initiation of Removal Motion (`Section 3(1)`):** A notice of motion for presenting an address to the President may be introduced in **either House of Parliament**, signed by **at least 100 members in the Lok Sabha** OR **at least 50 members in the Rajya Sabha**.
2. **Gatekeeping by Presiding Officer:** The notice is submitted to the **Speaker (Lok Sabha)** or the **Chairman (Rajya Sabha)**, who—after consulting such persons and considering such relevant material as deemed fit—**may either admit the motion or refuse to admit it**. (If notices are given in both Houses on the *same day*, a joint committee is formed only if both admit; if given on different days, the earlier notice takes precedence.)
3. **Constitution of the Three-Member Inquiry Committee (`Section 3(2)`):** Once the motion is admitted, the Speaker or Chairman keeps the motion pending and constitutes a **three-member Inquiry Committee** comprising:
   - **(a)** The **Chief Justice of India or a sitting Judge of the Supreme Court** (Chairperson);
   - **(b)** A **Chief Justice of a High Court**; and
   - **(c)** A **distinguished jurist** (in the opinion of the Speaker/Chairman).
4. **Quasi-Judicial Investigation (`Sections 3–4`):** The Committee frames definite written charges, supplies them to the judge, provides a reasonable opportunity for cross-examination and legal defence, and exercises civil-court powers to summon witnesses and documents. (If "incapacity" is alleged, the Committee arranges a medical board examination.)
5. **Submission of Report (`Section 4(2)`):** Upon completing the investigation, the Committee submits its report to the Speaker or Chairman, who lays it before the House.
   - If the Committee finds the judge **NOT guilty** of any misbehaviour or incapacity, **no further steps are taken** and the motion ends permanently (`Section 6(1)`).
   - If the Committee finds the judge **guilty** of any charge, the motion—together with the Committee's report—is taken up for consideration by the House (`Section 6(2)`).
6. **Parliamentary Vote by Special Majority in the Same Session (`Article 124(4)`):** The motion must be passed by **each House of Parliament separately** in the **same session** by a **Special Majority**—meaning **(i) a majority of the total membership of that House** (>50% of total strength) **AND (ii) a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting**. (Notice there is **NO Joint Sitting** for judicial removal!)
7. **Presidential Order of Removal:** Once the address is presented by both Houses in the same session, the **President passes an order removing the judge** from office.

```flow
# Removal Process of a Supreme Court Judge (Art. 124(4) & Judges Inquiry Act, 1968)
Notice of Motion Signed | Minimum 100 Lok Sabha MPs OR Minimum 50 Rajya Sabha MPs (ground: ONLY 'proved misbehaviour' or 'incapacity')
Presiding Officer's Discretion | Speaker (LS) or Chairman (RS) evaluates material — may REFUSE at threshold OR ADMIT motion
3-Member Inquiry Committee (1968 Act) | SC Judge / CJI (Chair) || High Court Chief Justice || Distinguished Jurist
Committee Findings Submitted to House | If NOT Guilty: Motion terminates permanently || If GUILTY: Motion + Report taken up for House vote
Special Majority in BOTH Houses (Same Session) | >50% of Total Membership AND ≥2/3rd of Members Present & Voting in LS & RS separately
Presidential Order of Removal | Address presented to President in the same session → President issues formal Order of Removal
```

Two landmark Constitution Bench decisions clarified the delicate legal intersection between the 1968 statutory inquiry and Parliament's sovereign vote:

- ***Sub-Committee on Judicial Accountability v. Union of India* (1991, 5-judge bench):** Held that a valid removal motion admitted by the Speaker does **NOT lapse upon the dissolution of the Lok Sabha** (an exception to `Article 107`!). The Court drew a sharp two-stage line: up to the Inquiry Committee's finding of guilt, the process is **statutory and quasi-judicial** (subject to judicial review); once the guilty report enters the House floor, the process becomes **parliamentary**. Courts will not interfere *during* the parliamentary vote, though a **final Presidential removal order** remains open to limited judicial review if obtained through patent procedural illegality or violation of constitutional safeguards.
- ***Sarojini Ramaswami v. Union of India* (1992, 5-judge bench):** Held that a judge cannot demand a copy of the Inquiry Committee's report *before* it is submitted to the Speaker to pre-emptively litigate and stall Parliament; the judge receives the report at the stage of parliamentary consideration and can defend themselves on the floor of the House (in person or through counsel).

### Historical Record of Removal Proceedings and the 2025–2026 Accountability Debate

The constitutional bar for removal was set deliberately high—so high, in fact, that **NO Judge of the Supreme Court (and NO Judge of any High Court) has EVER been removed by Parliament** since 1950. Every serious proceeding in India's constitutional history has either collapsed on the parliamentary floor, been rejected at the admission threshold, or been short-circuited by the judge's strategic resignation mid-inquiry:

1. **Justice V. Ramaswami (Supreme Court, 1991–1993) — Only SC Judge to Face a Floor Vote:** Following audit disclosures of extravagant official-residence expenditure during his stint as Chief Justice of the Punjab & Haryana High Court, 108 Lok Sabha MPs initiated a removal motion in February 1991 (admitted by Speaker Rabi Ray). The three-member Inquiry Committee chaired by SC Justice P.B. Sawant found **11 of 14 charges proved**. Yet when the Lok Sabha voted on **10–11 May 1993** (after Kapil Sibal defended Justice Ramaswami in a landmark four-hour floor address), **196 MPs voted in favour, 0 voted against, and 205 Congress MPs abstained**—causing the motion to fall short of the mandatory >50% total-membership threshold! Justice Ramaswami retired in 1994, exposing the danger of party whips rescuing a judge despite proven financial impropriety.
2. **Justice Soumitra Sen (Calcutta High Court, 2011):** Indicted for financial misappropriation prior to elevation, he became the **first judge in Indian history against whom the Rajya Sabha passed a removal motion** (189–17 in August 2011). Before the Lok Sabha could vote, he resigned on **1 September 2011**, bringing the proceeding to an end.
3. **Justice P.D. Dinakaran (Karnataka / Sikkim High Court, 2011):** Faced land-grabbing and disproportionate-assets charges before a Rajya Sabha Inquiry Committee chaired by Justice Aftab Alam; resigned in **July 2011** before the panel finalised its report.
4. **CJI Dipak Misra (Supreme Court, April 2018):** Seven opposition parties submitted a notice signed by **64 Rajya Sabha MPs** alleging five grounds of misbehaviour (including arbitrary roster allocation and medical-college bribery echoes). On **23 April 2018**, Rajya Sabha Chairman M. Venkaiah Naidu **refused to admit the notice** at the threshold under `Section 3(1)` of the 1968 Act—the first time a presiding officer exercised refusal power against a sitting CJI. Two Congress MPs withdrew their Supreme Court challenge after a five-judge Bench refused to disclose the administrative order constituting the bench.
5. **Other High Court Motions (2015–2017):** Notices moved against Justices J.B. Pardiwala (Gujarat HC, 2015—remarks expunged), S.K. Gangele (MP HC, 2015–2017—exonerated by inquiry panel), and C.V. Nagarjuna Reddy (AP/Telangana HC, 2016–2017—failed signature threshold after withdrawals) never reached a House vote.

> [!news]
> **Justice Yashwant Varma Removal Inquiry and the Post-Resignation Accountability Gap (2025–2026):**
> - **Trigger & In-House Probe (March–July 2025):** Following a fire on **14–15 March 2025** at then Delhi HC judge **Justice Yashwant Varma's** official residence (30, Tughlaq Crescent, New Delhi), charred bundles of ₹500 notes were reportedly found in a storeroom. Repatriated to **Allahabad HC** (without judicial work) and indicted by an SC in-house panel, he declined to resign.
> - **Statutory Inquiry Panel (Aug 2025 – May 2026):** On **12 August 2025**, Lok Sabha Speaker Om Birla admitted a bipartisan removal notice signed by **146 MPs** (submitted 21 July 2025) and constituted a 3-member Judges Inquiry Committee under the 1968 Act (reconstituted w.e.f. **6 March 2026**: **SC Justice Aravind Kumar, Bombay HC CJ Shree Chandrashekhar, and Senior Advocate B.V. Acharya**).
> - **Mid-Inquiry Resignation & Tabled Report (Apr–Nov 2026):** Justice Varma abruptly **resigned on 9 April 2026** after the SC rejected his challenge to the panel. Completing its probe, the Committee submitted its report on **18 May 2026**; **tabled in Parliament on 12 August 2026**, it found all three charges (unexplained cash, failure to secure evidence, evasive replies) **"proved."** In a letter circulated to MPs in **November 2026**, Justice Varma cited `Art. 217(1)(a)` and *Gopal Chandra Mishra* (1978) to argue proceedings became **"infructuous"** post-resignation—exposing a major accountability gap when judges quit mid-inquiry.

#### The In-House Inquiry Procedure and the Need for Graded Accountability

Because `Article 124(4)` removal is a cumbersome "nuclear option" reserved for extreme misconduct, the Supreme Court adopted the **Restatement of Values of Judicial Life (7 May 1997)**—a 16-point ethical charter—and an administrative **In-House Procedure (1999, formally published in 2014)** to handle complaints against sitting HC and SC judges confidentially. Under this procedure, if a three-judge in-house committee finds serious misconduct short of removal, the CJI may advise the judge; if grave enough for removal, the CJI advises the judge to resign or retire, withdraws judicial work if the judge refuses, and forwards the report to the President and Prime Minister for parliamentary action (exactly the sequence triggered in the Justice Yashwant Varma case in 2025!).

However, as the **2025–2026** controversy demonstrated, the in-house mechanism lacks statutory teeth, transparent public timelines, and **intermediate sanctions** (censure, fine, temporary suspension, or pension cuts). Parliament previously attempted to codify judicial ethics and establish a National Judicial Oversight Committee through the **Judicial Standards and Accountability Bill, 2010** (passed by Lok Sabha in March 2012), but the Bill **lapsed upon the dissolution of the 15th Lok Sabha in 2014**—leaving a statutory vacuum between absolute impunity and parliamentary removal.

### The Ten Pillars of Judicial Independence

To insulate Supreme Court judges from both executive favour and legislative reprisal, the Constitution weaves **ten distinct structural safeguards** across Parts IV and V. Every single pillar is regularly tested in both UPSC Prelims (matching safeguards to exact Articles) and GS-2 Mains answers.

| # | Pillar of Independence | Constitutional Provision | How It Insulates the Supreme Court |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Mode of Appointment** | `Article 124(2)` + Collegium | Executive cannot unilaterally pack the Bench; judicial primacy via CJI + 4 Collegium (*2nd, 3rd & 4th Judges Cases*) curbs partisan appointments. |
| **2** | **Security of Tenure** | `Article 124(4)–(5)` | Judges do **not** hold office at the President's pleasure; removable **only** by Special Majority in both Houses (same session) on 2 proved grounds. |
| **3** | **Fixed Service Conditions** | `Article 125` | Salaries, allowances, privileges, leave, and pensions are fixed by Parliament and **cannot be reduced mid-term**, except during an `Art. 360` Financial Emergency. |
| **4** | **Expenses Charged on CFI** | `Arts. 112(3)(d), 146(3)` | Salaries, pensions, and administrative expenses of SC judges and staff are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**—**non-votable** by Parliament (though discussable). |
| **5** | **Conduct Non-Discussable** | `Articles 121, 211` | Neither Parliament nor any State Legislature can discuss the conduct of any SC or HC judge in discharge of duties, **except** upon a formal removal motion. |
| **6** | **Ban on Post-Retirement Practice** | `Article 124(7)` | Retired SC judges are **totally barred** from pleading or acting before **any court or authority** anywhere in India, preventing favouritism toward future clients or law firms. |
| **7** | **Power to Punish for Contempt** | `Article 129` | Inherent authority as a Court of Record to punish civil and criminal contempt preserves institutional dignity and enforces orders without relying on executive goodwill. |
| **8** | **Freedom to Appoint Own Staff** | `Article 146` | The CJI appoints all officers and servants of the SC and prescribes their conditions of service without interference from the Union Executive or UPSC. |
| **9** | **Jurisdiction Cannot Be Curtailed** | `Article 138` | Parliament is empowered to **enlarge** SC jurisdiction on Union List matters or by special agreement, but can **NEVER curtail** constitutionally granted jurisdiction. |
| **10** | **Separation from Executive** | `Article 50` (DPSP) | Directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive in public services—operationalised via **CrPC 1973 / BNSS 2023**, removing judicial powers from executive magistrates. |

#### The Post-Retirement Government Appointments Debate

Look closely at Pillar 6 (**`Article 124(7)`**): while it imposes an absolute bar on retired Supreme Court judges *pleading or acting* as advocates before any Indian court or authority (far stricter than **`Article 220`**, which permits retired permanent High Court judges to practice before the **Supreme Court** and **other High Courts**!), it does **NOT** bar retired SC judges from accepting **post-retirement appointments offered by the Executive**—such as chairpersons of statutory tribunals, the National Human Rights Commission (NHRC), the Law Commission, Governors of States, or nominated Members of the Rajya Sabha!

Over the past decade, rapid post-retirement acceptances of high political or executive offices—including former CJI P. Sathasivam (Governor of Kerala, 2014), former CJI Ranjan Gogoi (Rajya Sabha nomination within four months of retirement, March 2020), and former Justice S. Abdul Nazeer (Governor of Andhra Pradesh within six weeks of retirement, February 2023)—have rekindled a fierce ethical debate. As far back as **1958**, the **14th Law Commission Report** (chaired by India's first Attorney General, M.C. Setalvad) warned that post-retirement executive patronage erodes public faith in impartiality, observing that the government is the largest litigant before the Supreme Court. Echoing that concern in Parliament in 2012, former Union Minister Arun Jaitley famously cautioned that *"the clamour for post-retirement jobs is also adversely affecting the impartiality of the judiciary... pre-retirement judgements are influenced by a desire for a post-retirement job."*

Because several statutes (such as the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 and tribunal enactments) *require* former SC judges as chairpersons, an absolute ban on all post-retirement roles is impractical unless statutory designs change. Instead, legal scholars and former CJIs (including Justice R.M. Lodha) advocate a **mandatory two-year cooling-off period** before accepting any executive, gubernatorial, or political office, coupled with either raising judicial retirement age or offering **full last-drawn salary as pension** with an independent, non-executive selection panel for statutory commissions.

> [!mains]
> **Mains Analytical Framing — Independence Without Insularity:**
> Top-scoring GS-2 answers on judicial independence avoid treating independence and accountability as zero-sum enemies. Anchor your argument on **three pillars**: (1) **Constitutional Shield** (`Arts. 121, 124(4), 125, 146` — indispensable because the State is the largest litigant); (2) **Accountability Deficits** (opacity of appointments, the resignation loophole exposed by the **2025–2026 Justice Yashwant Varma** inquiry, and post-retirement executive postings eroding *perceived* impartiality); and (3) **Balanced Democratic Alignment**—citing the five-judge Constitution Bench verdict in ***CPIO, Supreme Court of India v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal* (November 2019)**, which declared that the **office of the CJI is a "public authority" under the RTI Act, 2005**, affirming that *"judicial independence does not mean freedom from accountability; transparency strengthens, rather than weakens, public confidence in the courts."*

## 3. Complete Jurisdiction and Powers of the Supreme Court (Articles 129–145)

The Constitution confers upon the Supreme Court an extraordinary sweep of jurisdictions unmatched by any comparable apex court. Whereas the Supreme Court of the United States hears barely 60 to 80 constitutional and federal cases a year through discretionary *certiorari*, and the UK Supreme Court cannot test parliamentary statutes for constitutional validity, India's Supreme Court operates simultaneously as a **federal court of first instance**, a **direct Fundamental Rights court**, a **general court of civil, criminal, and constitutional appeal**, a **plenary tribunal overseer**, a **presidential legal advisor**, a **Court of Record**, and a **court of nationwide equity**. Every major jurisdiction carries sharp constitutional exclusions and procedural thresholds that form the bedrock of UPSC Prelims questions.

| # | Jurisdiction / Institutional Power | Governing Article(s) | Nature of Jurisdiction | Core Scope & Critical Constitutional Exclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Original & Exclusive** | `Article 131` (and `Art. 71`) | Original **AND** Exclusive | Inter-governmental federal legal disputes (Centre–State / State–State); **excludes** pre-Constitution treaties, `Art. 262` river waters, Finance Commission, commercial/damage claims & private citizens. `Art. 71`: President/VP poll disputes. |
| **2** | **Writ Jurisdiction** | `Article 32` | Original, **NOT Exclusive** (concurrent with HC `Art. 226`) | Five prerogative writs **ONLY for Fundamental Rights** (Part III); itself a Fundamental Right; cannot be refused at pure discretion. |
| **3** | **Appellate Jurisdiction** | `Articles 132, 133, 134, 134A` | Appellate (from High Courts) | Constitutional (`132`), Civil (`133` — ₹20,000 limit removed by `30th Amend`), Criminal (`134` — as of right on acquittal reversed to death; 1970 Act added life/10+ yrs); HC certificate via `134A` (`44th Amend`). |

### Original and Exclusive Jurisdiction (Articles 131 and 71)

As the sole umpire of India's federal compact, the Supreme Court exercises **original** (meaning disputes originate directly in the SC, not via appeal) and **exclusive** (meaning **no other court in India—not even a High Court**—can entertain them) jurisdiction under **`Article 131`**. This federal jurisdiction covers three exact combinations of parties:

1. **Between the Government of India and one or more States**;
2. **Between the Government of India and any State or States on one side, and one or more other States on the other**; or
3. **Between two or more States**.

Two threshold conditions govern every `Article 131` suit. First, the dispute must involve a **question (whether of law or fact) on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends**—purely political quarrels or policy disagreements lacking a constitutional or statutory legal right are barred. Second, the parties must be constituent units of the federation in their constitutional capacity: **suits filed by private citizens, corporations, or municipalities** against the Centre or a State do **NOT** lie under `Article 131` (as settled in ***State of Bihar v. Union of India*, 1970**, which held that even a government-owned PSU like Hindustan Steel Ltd. is not a "State" for `Article 131`).

#### Six Express and Doctrinal Exclusions from Article 131

UPSC Prelims routinely tests whether a given federal controversy falls inside or outside `Article 131`. Remember all **six categories excluded** from the Supreme Court's original federal jurisdiction:

1. **Pre-Constitutional Treaties and Covenants (`Article 131` Proviso):** Any dispute arising out of any treaty, agreement, covenant, engagement, *sanad*, or similar instrument executed **before the commencement of the Constitution (26 January 1950)** and continuing in operation, or providing that jurisdiction shall not extend to it. (Such disputes may, however, be referred by the President for an advisory opinion under `Article 143(2)`!)
2. **Inter-State River Water Disputes (`Article 262`):** Parliament is empowered by `Article 262(2)` to bar the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and all other courts over disputes concerning the use, distribution, or control of inter-state river waters—a bar Parliament enacted in `Section 11` of the **Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956** (though SC can still exercise `Article 136` appellate review over a tribunal's final award, as clarified in the Cauvery litigation).
3. **Matters Referred to the Finance Commission (`Article 280`):** Recommendations and fiscal allocations within the Finance Commission's constitutional remit lie outside `Article 131`.
4. **Adjustment of Certain Expenses and Pensions (`Article 290`):** Financial adjustments between the Union and a State regarding shared court/commission expenses or pre-1950 pensions are settled by agreement or an arbitrator appointed by the CJI, not via an `Article 131` suit.
5. **Ordinary Disputes of a Commercial Nature:** Routine commercial or contractual disagreements between the Centre and a State (unrelated to constitutional sovereignty or federal legal rights) are excluded.
6. **Recovery of Damages by a State Against the Centre:** A State cannot invoke `Article 131` merely to sue the Union for ordinary tortious or commercial damages.

An unresolved constitutional question under `Article 131` concerns whether a **State Government can challenge the constitutional validity of a Central statute** (enacted by Parliament) through an `Article 131` original suit. In ***State of Madhya Pradesh v. Union of India* (2011)**, a two-judge Bench answered in the negative, holding that statutory challenges belong to writ petitions under `Articles 32` or `226`. Three years later, in ***State of Jharkhand v. State of Bihar* (2014)**, a coordinate Bench disagreed and referred the issue to a larger Constitution Bench—leaving States (including Kerala on the Citizenship Amendment Act, 2019 and Punjab on farm laws) to invoke `Article 131` while definitive adjudication awaits.

#### Original and Exclusive Jurisdiction Over Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections (Article 71)

Distinct from federal suits under `Article 131`, **`Article 71(1)`** vests the Supreme Court with **original and exclusive jurisdiction** to inquire into and decide **all doubts and disputes arising out of or in connection with the election of a President or Vice-President**, and makes its decision **final**. Crucially, if the Supreme Court declares the election of a President or Vice-President void, acts already performed by that person in office prior to the declaration are **not invalidated** (`Article 71(2)`). Moreover, **`Article 71(4)`** (inserted by the `11th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1961`) bars challenging such an election merely on the ground of any **vacancy** among the members of the electoral college. (Contrast this with MP and MLA election disputes, which originate in the concerned **High Court** under the Representation of the People Act, 1951—NOT the Supreme Court!)

> **Landmark Contemporary Article 131 Federal Suits (2024):**
> - **Fiscal Federalism — *State of Kerala v. Union of India* (1 April 2024, `2024 INSC 253`):** Challenging the Union Ministry of Finance's imposition of a **Net Borrowing Ceiling** (including off-budget borrowings by state entities like KIIFB) under `Article 293`, Kerala filed an original suit under `Article 131`. While refusing interim relief for ₹26,226 crore in extra borrowing, a Division Bench led by Justice Surya Kant held that `Article 293` had never previously received authoritative constitutional interpretation and **referred the suit to a five-judge Constitution Bench** under `Article 145(3)`.
> - **Police Federalism — *State of West Bengal v. Union of India* (10 July 2024, `2024 INSC 502`):** West Bengal filed an `Article 131` suit contesting the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) registering *suo motu* FIRs inside the State after West Bengal withdrew its **general consent** under `Section 6` of the Delhi Special Police Establishment (DSPE) Act, 1946 in November 2018. Rejecting the Union's preliminary objection that the CBI is an independent agency distinct from the Government of India, a Bench led by Justice B.R. Gavai held the **`Article 131` suit maintainable**, noting that administrative superintendence over the DSPE vests in the Central Government and framing the federal controversy for trial on merits.

### Writ Jurisdiction (Article 32)

The Constitution makes the Supreme Court the direct **"protector and guarantor of Fundamental Rights."** Under **`Article 32`**, any aggrieved person—or any public-spirited citizen via PIL—may move the Supreme Court directly for the enforcement of any right conferred by **Part III**, and the Court may issue directions, orders, or the five classic prerogative writs: **Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, and Quo Warranto** (examined doctrinally in full in Chapter 5, *Fundamental Rights*).

Three constitutional characteristics distinguish the Supreme Court's `Article 32` writ jurisdiction sharply from both `Article 131` and High Courts' `Article 226` jurisdiction:

- **Original, Yet NOT Exclusive:** Like `Article 131`, `Article 32` is **original** (a citizen need not go through lower courts or High Courts first). Unlike `Article 131`, however, it is **NOT exclusive**: it operates **concurrently** with the High Courts' writ jurisdiction under `Article 226`.
- **Narrower Subject-Matter Scope Than High Courts (`Art. 226`):** The Supreme Court can issue writs under `Article 32` **ONLY for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights**—never for ordinary statutory or legal rights (unless Parliament confers enlarged writ power for other purposes under **`Article 139`**, a power Parliament has not generally exercised). By contrast, a High Court under `Article 226` can issue writs for **both Fundamental Rights AND "for any other purpose"** (ordinary legal rights), making HC writ jurisdiction wider in subject-matter scope though narrower in territorial reach!
- **Itself a Fundamental Right (Non-Discretionary Shield):** Because `Article 32` sits inside Part III—hailed by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar as *"the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it"*—the Supreme Court **cannot refuse** to exercise `Article 32` jurisdiction purely at its discretion when a clear breach of a Fundamental Right is shown (unlike `Article 226`, which is discretionary). Nevertheless, to prevent docket flooding, the Court frequently invokes self-imposed judicial discipline (*Kanubhai Brahmbhatt*, 1987; *P.N. Kumar*, 1987) directing petitioners to seek efficacious relief from the High Court under `Article 226` first unless urgent nationwide or systemic constitutional issues demand apex intervention. Under **`Article 32(3)`**, Parliament may by law empower any other court within its local jurisdiction to exercise `Article 32` writ powers (unused so far), and under **`Article 32(4)`**, the right cannot be suspended except during a National Emergency strictly in accordance with **`Article 359`** (and never for `Articles 20` and `21` after the `44th Amendment`).

### Appellate Jurisdiction (Articles 132–134A, 135 and 139A)

As the apex court of an integrated judicial hierarchy, the Supreme Court is primarily a court of appeal hearing challenges against judgments of High Courts across India. Its regular appellate jurisdiction falls into **four distinct constitutional tracks** (Constitutional, Civil, Criminal, and Extraordinary Special Leave), supported by certificate, continuity, and transfer provisions.

```flow
# Appellate and Discretionary Pathways to the Supreme Court
High Court / Tribunal Order | Aggrieved Party Seeks Apex Judicial Remedy
Constitutional Appeal (Art. 132) | Any HC final order (civil/criminal/other) + Art. 134A certificate of substantial constitutional question || Civil Appeal (Art. 133) | HC civil order + Art. 134A certificate of substantial question of general importance needing SC decision || Criminal Appeal (Art. 134) | As of right if HC reverses acquittal to Death (or Life/10+ yrs via 1970 Act), OR via Art. 134A fitness certificate
Special Leave Petition (Art. 136) | Plenary DISCRETIONARY override from ANY court or tribunal (final OR interlocutory) — EXCEPT Armed Forces courts/tribunals (Art. 136(2))
```

#### 1. Appeals in Constitutional Matters (Article 132)

Under **`Article 132`**, an appeal lies to the Supreme Court from **any judgment, decree, or final order of a High Court**—whether in a **civil, criminal, or other proceeding** (such as revenue, writ, or election matters)—if the High Court certifies under `Article 134A` that the case involves a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution**. Once such a certificate is granted, any party may appeal to the SC on the ground that the constitutional question was wrongly decided. Note that appeals under `Article 132` lie **only against final orders**, never interlocutory orders (for which a litigant must invoke `Article 136`).

#### 2. Appeals in Civil Matters (Article 133)

Under **`Article 133`**, an appeal lies to the Supreme Court from any judgment, decree, or final order in a **civil proceeding** of a High Court if the High Court certifies under `Article 134A` **both** of the following twin conditions:

1. That the case involves a **substantial question of law of general importance**; **AND**
2. That in the opinion of the High Court, the said question **needs to be decided by the Supreme Court**.

Originally, `Article 133(1)` permitted civil appeals as of right whenever the disputed property value exceeded **₹20,000**—a colonial pecuniary filter that rewarded wealthy litigants even in legally trivial property disputes. Recognising its elitist bias, Parliament enacted the **`30th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1972`**, which **abolished the ₹20,000 monetary ceiling completely** and replaced it with the qualitative "substantial question of law of general importance" standard! Note also **`Article 133(3)`**: no appeal lies under `Article 133` from the judgment of a **single judge of a High Court** (the litigant must first exhaust intra-court Letters Patent Appeal to a Division Bench, unless Parliament provides otherwise).

#### 3. Appeals in Criminal Matters (Article 134 and the 1970 Enlargement Act)

Because criminal convictions directly imperil personal liberty and life (`Article 21`), **`Article 134`** structures criminal appeals along two distinct lanes—one **as of right** (requiring NO High Court certificate) and one **by certificate**:

- **Criminal Appeal AS OF RIGHT (No HC Certificate Required):** Lies to the Supreme Court in two constitutional scenarios (`Article 134(1)(a)–(b)`):
  1. When a High Court has, on appeal, **reversed an order of acquittal** of an accused person and **sentenced them to death**; OR
  2. When a High Court has **withdrawn for trial before itself** any case from a subordinate court and, in such trial, convicted the accused and **sentenced them to death**.
- **Parliamentary Enlargement (`Article 134(2)` & 1970 Act):** Exercising `Article 134(2)`, Parliament enacted the **Supreme Court (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Act, 1970**, expanding criminal appeals **as of right** to cover cases where a High Court reverses an acquittal (or withdraws a trial) and sentences the accused to **imprisonment for life OR imprisonment for ten years or more**.
- **Criminal Appeal BY CERTIFICATE (`Article 134(1)(c)`):** Lies whenever the High Court certifies under `Article 134A` that the case is a **fit one for appeal** to the Supreme Court.

Observe the crucial Prelims asymmetry: if a High Court **reverses a conviction and acquits** an accused person (even in a murder trial), the State has **NO constitutional appeal as of right** under `Article 134`—it must either seek a fitness certificate under `Article 134(1)(c)` or petition for discretionary Special Leave under `Article 136`!

#### 4. Certificate for Appeal (Article 134A), Continuity (Article 135) and Case Transfers (Article 139A)

- **Certificate for Appeal (`Article 134A`):** Inserted by the **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`** to eliminate weeks of post-judgment procedural delay, `Article 134A` directs every High Court, immediately upon pronouncing a judgment covered by `Articles 132(1)`, `133(1)`, or `134(1)(c)`, to determine—either **on its own motion** (*suo motu*) OR upon an **oral application made immediately** by or on behalf of the aggrieved party—whether to grant a certificate for appeal to the Supreme Court.
- **Federal Court Continuity (`Article 135`):** Preserved for the Supreme Court all pre-1950 appellate jurisdiction formerly exercisable by the Federal Court of India under existing laws whenever `Articles 133–134` did not otherwise apply.
- **Withdrawal and Transfer of Cases (`Article 139A`):** Inserted by the **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`** and refined by the **`44th Amendment Act, 1978`**, `Article 139A` equips the Supreme Court with two vital caseload-consolidation powers:
  1. **`Article 139A(1)` (Withdrawal):** Where cases involving the **same or substantially the same questions of law of general importance** are pending before the SC and one or more High Courts (or before two or more High Courts), the SC—either on its own motion, on application by the **Attorney General of India**, or on application by any party (added by the `44th Amendment`)—may **withdraw all such cases to itself** and dispose of them (or decide the common legal question and return the cases to the High Courts).
  2. **`Article 139A(2)` (Transfer):** The Supreme Court may, if it deems it expedient for the **ends of justice**, transfer any case, appeal, or other proceeding pending before any High Court to any other High Court (parallel to its statutory civil/criminal transfer powers under `Section 25 CPC` and `Section 446 BNSS, 2023` / `Section 406 CrPC`).

### Special Leave Petition (Article 136)

Sitting above all structured appellate gates is **`Article 136`**, the single widest and most frequently invoked doorway to the Supreme Court. It declares that *"Notwithstanding anything in this Chapter, the Supreme Court may, in its discretion, grant special leave to appeal from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order in any cause or matter passed or made by any court or tribunal in the territory of India."*

Four defining legal attributes separate `Article 136` from `Articles 132–134`:

1. **Purely Discretionary Residual Power:** Special Leave **cannot be claimed as a matter of right** by any litigant; it is an extraordinary reserve power meant to cure grave injustice or decide questions of overarching public importance (*Pritam Singh v. State*, 1950).
2. **Covers BOTH Final and Interlocutory Orders:** Unlike `Articles 132–134` (which require a "final order"), `Article 136` applies equally to **interim, interlocutory, bail, stay, and procedural orders**.
3. **Sweeps Across ALL Courts and Tribunals:** It reaches not just High Courts, but **any court or quasi-judicial tribunal** anywhere in India (industrial tribunals, CAT, NCLAT, NGT, Election Commission acting quasi-judicially, or Speaker acting as Tenth Schedule tribunal under *Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992)—bypassing even High Courts in exceptional cases.
4. **The Sole Constitutional Exclusion (`Article 136(2)`):** Special Leave **does NOT apply** to any judgment, determination, sentence, or order passed or made by **any court or tribunal constituted by or under any law relating to the Armed Forces** (such as military courts-martial!). Appeals from the Armed Forces Tribunal (AFT) reach the Supreme Court only via statutory appeal under `Sections 30–31` of the Armed Forces Tribunal Act, 2007 (though High Courts retain `Article 226` writ jurisdiction over AFT orders, affirmed in *Union of India v. Parashotam Dass*, 2023).

#### The SLP Docket Overload Crisis (Verified 2026 Pendency Data)

In Constituent Assembly design, `Article 136` was envisioned as an exceptional safety valve invoked sparingly. In practice, however, liberal admission of routine civil, bail, rent, and service-matter SLPs—accounting for well over **80 per cent of all Supreme Court filings**—has transformed India's constitutional apex into an overburdened routine court of third appeal. According to National Judicial Data Grid (NJDG) statistics placed before the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha by the Union Ministry of Law and Justice in **July–August 2026**, India's judicial backlog reached approximately **5.65 crore pending cases** nationwide (**4.99 crore** in district/subordinate courts, **64.67 lakh** in the 25 High Courts, and **92,755 to 95,954 cases** in the Supreme Court as of late July 2026—up sharply from 70,239 in 2021 and 82,496 in 2024, and hovering near **one lakh** by October 2026). Notably, over **10,000 Supreme Court cases have been pending for more than 10 years**, including 558 for over 20 years and 26 for over 30 years.

### Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143) and All Sixteen Presidential References

Modelled directly on `Section 213` of the **Government of India Act, 1935** (and analogous to the Canadian Supreme Court's reference jurisdiction), **`Article 143`** empowers the **President of India** (acting on the aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers) to seek the Supreme Court's authoritative legal opinion on matters of public importance without waiting for adversarial litigation. Notably, this consultative power is **completely absent in the United States**, where the US Supreme Court refused President George Washington's 1793 request for legal advice and has steadfastly held that Article III's *"Cases and Controversies"* clause prohibits advisory opinions.

**`Article 143` contains TWO sharply distinct sub-clauses**—a perennial favourite for UPSC Prelims traps:

- **`Article 143(1)` — Questions of Law or Fact of Public Importance (Discretionary Duty):** If at any time it appears to the President that a question of law or fact has arisen, or is likely to arise, which is of such a nature and of such public importance that it is expedient to obtain the opinion of the Supreme Court, the President may refer it to the Court, and the Court **"may"**, after such hearing as it thinks fit, report its opinion to the President. Because of the word **"may"**, the Supreme Court **may legitimately decline to answer** an `Article 143(1)` reference if it is vague, purely hypothetical, political, or serves no constitutional purpose—a discretion the Court actually exercised twice: in the **1993 Ayodhya Reference** (***Dr. M. Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India*, 1994**, declining to answer the purely historical-factual question of whether a Hindu temple existed on the disputed site) and in the **1982 Jammu & Kashmir Resettlement Bill Reference** (returned unanswered in **2001** after 19 years because the Bill had already been enacted into law!).
- **`Article 143(2)` — Pre-Constitutional Treaties and Covenants (Mandatory Duty):** Notwithstanding the proviso to `Article 131` (which excludes pre-1950 treaties, agreements, covenants, engagements, and *sanads* from the SC's original federal jurisdiction), the President may refer a dispute of that very kind to the Supreme Court, and the Court **"shall"**, after such hearing as it thinks fit, report its opinion to the President. Here, the word **"shall"** makes it **constitutionally mandatory** for the Supreme Court to tender its opinion—it cannot decline!

#### Legal Effect of an Advisory Opinion

Under **BOTH `Article 143(1)` AND `Article 143(2)`**, the reference must be heard by a Constitution Bench of **at least five judges (`Article 145(3)`)**, and the opinion delivered in open court (`Article 145(4)`). Crucially, under **both clauses**, the Supreme Court's advisory opinion is **purely consultative and NOT legally binding** on the President or the Government—nor does it operate strictly as a decree between litigating parties. Nevertheless, as the seven-judge Bench noted in ***In Re: Special Courts Bill, 1978*** and the five-judge Bench reaffirmed in ***Special Reference No. 1 of 2012*** (2G Natural Resources Reference), an advisory opinion by a Constitution Bench carries immense moral, institutional, and persuasive weight—and in practice, Union Governments have consistently respected and implemented them (for instance, enacting the `9th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1960` to implement *Berubari Union*, and expanding the Collegium to CJI + 4 after the *Third Judges Case, 1998*). Moreover, an `Article 143` reference **cannot be used as an appellate review to overturn a binding *inter partes* decree**, though the Court may clarify the broader constitutional principle (*ratio decidendi*) flowing from an earlier judgment (*Special Reference No. 1 of 2012* and *Special Reference No. 1 of 2025*).

> **Sixteenth Presidential Reference — *In Re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills by the Governor and the President of India* (Special Reference No. 1 of 2025, 20 November 2025, 5-Judge Bench)**
> - **Trigger & Context:** On **8 April 2025**, a two-judge Bench in *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu* had fixed strict 1-to-3-month timelines for Governors (`Art. 200`) and the President (`Art. 201`) to act on State Bills, and invoked `Article 142` to declare ten long-pending Tamil Nadu Bills **"deemed assented."** On **13 May 2025**, President Droupadi Murmu referred **fourteen constitutional questions** to the Supreme Court under `Article 143(1)`.
> - **Bench & Unanimous Opinion (20 November 2025):** A five-judge Constitution Bench led by outgoing 52nd CJI **B.R. Gavai** (with Justices Surya Kant, Vikram Nath, P.S. Narasimha, and A.S. Chandurkar) delivered a landmark unanimous advisory opinion policing the boundaries of separation of powers:
>   1. **No Rigid Judicial Timelines:** Because the framers consciously omitted fixed time limits in `Articles 200` and `201` (using elastic phrases like *"as soon as possible"*), courts cannot judicially write rigid numerical deadlines into the constitutional text.
>   2. **No "Deemed Assent" via `Article 142`:** Invoking `Article 142` to treat a Bill as "deemed assented" usurps the constitutional function of the Governor/President, violating **separation of powers**; `Article 142` can **never supplant express constitutional provisions**.
>   3. **Non-Justiciability of Merits & Pre-Enacted Bills:** The dialogic legislative process under `Arts. 200–201` is non-adjudicatory; courts cannot review the *substantive merits* of a Governor's or President's decision to assent, return, or reserve a Bill—nor adjudicate the constitutionality of a Bill *before* it becomes law (save under `Art. 143`).
>   4. **Reservation of Repassed Bills & No Mandatory `Art. 143` Reference:** A Governor still retains constitutional authority to reserve a reconsidered/repassed Bill if federal or constitutional implications warrant, and the President is not bound to seek an `Article 143` opinion for every reserved Bill.
>   5. **Narrow Judicial Check Retained — Limited Mandamus:** Rejecting absolute gubernatorial impunity, the Bench clarified that a Governor cannot sit on a Bill indefinitely ("pocket veto" paralysis); in cases of **"prolonged, unexplained, and indefinite" inaction**, constitutional courts may issue a **limited writ of mandamus** directing the Governor to take *a* decision within a reasonable period, without dictating *which* constitutional option to pick.

### Court of Record, Contempt Power, Binding Law and Complete Justice (Articles 129, 141, 142, 144)

Beyond traditional dispute adjudication, four structural Articles—`129`, `141`, `142`, and `144`—clothe the Supreme Court with sovereign institutional authority across the entire Indian legal system.

#### 1. Court of Record and Contempt of Court (Article 129)

**`Article 129`** declares that *"The Supreme Court shall be a court of record and shall have all the powers of such a court including the power to punish for contempt of itself."* Being a **Court of Record** entails **two distinct legal powers**:

- **Perpetual Evidentiary Record:** The judgments, proceedings, and acts of the Supreme Court are recorded for perpetual memory and testimony; these records possess conclusive evidentiary value and cannot be questioned for authenticity when produced before any court in India.
- **Inherent Power to Punish for Contempt:** The Supreme Court possesses summary, inherent constitutional power—which no ordinary statute can strip away—to punish contempt not only of **itself**, but also of **High Courts, subordinate courts, and tribunals** throughout India (settled by a three-judge Bench in ***Delhi Judicial Service Association v. State of Gujarat*, 1991**, arising out of police humiliation of a Chief Judicial Magistrate in Nadiad).

While `Article 129` confers the constitutional power (corresponding to `Article 215` for High Courts), the Constitution itself does **NOT define "contempt of court"** (unlike `Article 19(2)`, which lists contempt of court as a reasonable restriction on free speech). Parliament codified the definition, procedure, and defences in the **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** (enacted on the recommendations of the **H.N. Sanyal Committee, 1961**), dividing contempt into two categories:

- **Civil Contempt (`Section 2(b)`):** Wilful disobedience to any judgment, decree, direction, order, writ, or other process of a court, OR wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court. Its purpose is coercive and remedial—compelling compliance with judicial orders.
- **Criminal Contempt (`Section 2(c)`):** The publication (whether by words spoken or written, signs, visible representations, or otherwise) of any matter or the doing of any act which:
  1. **Scandalises** or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower the authority of, any court; **OR**
  2. **Prejudices**, or interferes or tends to interfere with, the due course of any judicial proceeding; **OR**
  3. **Interferes** or tends to interfere with, or obstructs or tends to obstruct, the administration of justice in any other manner.

Crucially, the 1971 Act carves out clear statutory defences: innocent publication/distribution, fair and accurate reporting of judicial proceedings, fair and reasonable criticism of a judicial act (as distinct from imputing dishonest motives to a judge), and—inserted by the **Contempt of Courts (Amendment) Act, 2006** (`Section 13(b)`)—**justification by TRUTH as a valid defence**, provided the court is satisfied that the truth statement was made in the **public interest** and the request to invoke the defence is ***bona fide***. Punishment under the 1971 Act is capped at simple imprisonment up to **six months** or a fine up to **₹2,000** (or both)—though under `Article 129`, the SC's constitutional power is not strictly bounded by the statutory fine ceiling (still subject to proportionality).

> [!debate]
> **Criminal Contempt ("Scandalising the Court") vs Freedom of Speech (`Article 19(1)(a)`):**
> - **Case for Retention:** Protecting public confidence in an impartial judiciary is vital for the Rule of Law; since judges cannot descend into media arenas to debate litigants, wilful scurrilous attacks imputing corruption or bias without proof can destabilise judicial authority (e.g., *In Re: Prashant Bhushan*, 2020, where a nominal ₹1 fine was imposed; *In Re: Vijay Kurle*, 2020).
> - **Case for Reform / Narrowing:** Critics—including former SC judges like Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer (who called contempt law *"vague and wandering"*) and Justice Markandey Katju—note that the **United Kingdom abolished the offence of "scandalising the court" in 2013** (Crime and Courts Act, 2013) on the UK Law Commission's advice that it was an archaic relic chilling fair public criticism. The **274th Law Commission Report (2018)** declined to abolish criminal contempt in India given high pendency and attacks on subordinate judges, but urged exercising the power **sparingly** only when actual obstruction of justice is threatened.

#### 2. Law Declared Binding on All Courts (Article 141) and Aid of Authorities (Article 144)

**`Article 141`** anchors the common-law doctrine of *stare decisis* (binding judicial precedent) at the constitutional level: *"The law declared by the Supreme Court shall be binding on all courts within the territory of India."* Three surgical Prelims clarifications govern `Article 141`:

1. **Binds ALL Courts EXCEPT the Supreme Court Itself:** In the landmark seven-judge Bench decision in ***Bengal Immunity Co. Ltd. v. State of Bihar* (1955)**, the Supreme Court held that the phrase *"all courts within the territory of India"* does **NOT** include the Supreme Court itself! Thus, while a smaller bench is bound by a larger bench's precedent, a larger Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court retains full freedom to **depart from or overrule its own earlier decisions** whenever constitutional fidelity demands it—enabling historic course-corrections such as *Golaknath* → *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), *A.K. Gopalan* → *Maneka Gandhi* (1978), *ADM Jabalpur* → *K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017), and *P.V. Narasimha Rao* (1998) → ***Sita Soren v. Union of India* (4 March 2024, 7-judge unanimous bench)** ending legislative immunity for bribery!
2. **Only *Ratio Decidendi* Binds:** What binds subordinate courts is the underlying legal principle essential to the decision (*ratio decidendi*), **NOT** passing remarks (*obiter dicta*), consent orders, summary dismissals of SLPs without reasons, or rulings delivered *per incuriam* (in ignorance of a binding statute or larger bench judgment).
3. **Enforcement Backing (`Article 144`):** Complementing `Article 141`, **`Article 144`** commands that *"All authorities, civil and judicial, in the territory of India shall act in aid of the Supreme Court"*—binding police, district collectors, Union/State bureaucracies, and every court to execute Supreme Court orders faithfully.

#### 3. Power to Do "Complete Justice" (Article 142)

**`Article 142(1)`** vests the Supreme Court with an extraordinary, uniquely Indian plenary power of equity: *"The Supreme Court in the exercise of its jurisdiction may pass such decree or make such order as is necessary for doing **complete justice** in any cause or matter pending before it,"* and every such decree or order is enforceable throughout the territory of India (`Article 142(2)` additionally empowers the Court to secure attendance of persons, discovery of documents, and investigation/punishment of contempt). Unlike High Courts (which possess **no equivalent to `Article 142`**), the Supreme Court uses this constitutional reserve to bridge technical procedural gaps when rigid law would otherwise defeat substantial justice.

- **Transformative and Contemporary Invocations of `Article 142`:**
  - ***Union Carbide Corporation v. Union of India* (1989 & 1991):** Approved the $470 million Bhopal Gas Tragedy compensation settlement to bring immediate relief to lakhs of victims.
  - ***Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997):** Used `Articles 141` and `142` to issue binding workplace sexual-harassment guidelines in a statutory void until Parliament passed the POSH Act, 2013.
  - ***M. Siddiq v. Mahant Suresh Das* (Ayodhya Verdict, 9 November 2019, 5-judge unanimous bench):** Directed the allotment of a 5-acre alternative plot in Ayodhya to the Sunni Central Waqf Board to ensure restorative justice alongside the title decree.
  - ***A.G. Perarivalan v. State of Tamil Nadu* (18 May 2022):** Invoked `Article 142` to release a Rajiv Gandhi assassination convict after the Tamil Nadu Governor sat on the State Cabinet's `Article 161` remission recommendation for over two years.
  - ***Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan* (1 May 2023, 5-judge Constitution Bench):** Held that the Supreme Court may directly dissolve a marriage under `Article 142` on the ground of **irretrievable breakdown of marriage** (a ground not explicitly listed in the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955) and **waive the mandatory 6-to-18-month cooling-off period** under `Section 13B(2)` HMA when reconciliation is impossible—subject to careful scrutiny of alimony, child welfare, and equity.
  - ***Kuldeep Kumar v. U.T. Chandigarh* (Chandigarh Mayoral Election Case, 20 February 2024, 3-judge bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud):** Caught on video camera, the Returning Officer had deliberately defaced eight valid ballots to tilt the election. Calling the act a *"mockery of democracy"*, the Court invoked `Article 142` to set aside the invalidation, recount the genuine ballots in court, and **directly declare the rightful candidate elected Mayor** without ordering a fresh poll!
- **Constitutional Boundaries on `Article 142`:** Precisely because `Article 142` is so potent, the Supreme Court itself has laid down two iron guardrails preventing it from becoming an unruly judicial weapon:
  1. **Cannot Override Express Substantive Statutes:** In ***Supreme Court Bar Association (SCBA) v. Union of India* (1998, 5-judge bench)**, overruling *Vinay Chandra Mishra* (1995), the Court declared that `Article 142` is **curative and supplementary**—it **cannot supplant express substantive statutory provisions** (hence the SC could not suspend an advocate's practising licence under `Article 142`, bypassing the statutory disciplinary domain of the Bar Council of India under the Advocates Act, 1961).
  2. **Cannot Override Express Constitutional Provisions or Separation of Powers:** In ***Special Reference No. 1 of 2025* (20 November 2025, 5-judge bench)**, the Court held unequivocally that `Article 142` **cannot be used to manufacture "deemed assent"** to State Bills bypassing the Governor or President under `Articles 200–201`, stressing that equitable power must operate *within*—never *against*—the constitutional distribution of powers.

### Review Jurisdiction (Article 137) and Curative Petitions

In every mature legal system, litigation must attain finality (*interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium*—the doctrine of *res judicata*). Yet judges are human, and a final apex verdict marred by a glaring factual oversight, a forgotten binding statute, or undisclosed bias can inflict irreversible injustice. To balance **finality** against **fallibility**, Indian constitutional law provides two tightly guarded post-judgment remedies: the constitutional **Review Petition (`Article 137`)** and the judicially invented **Curative Petition (*Rupa Ashok Hurra*, 2002)**.

#### 1. Review Petition (Article 137)

Under **`Article 137`**, subject to any parliamentary law or rules made under `Article 145`, the Supreme Court has explicit constitutional power to **review any judgment pronounced or order made by it**. Governed by **Order XLVII of the Supreme Court Rules, 2013**, review is **NOT an appeal in disguise** and never allows re-arguing settled points merely because another view is plausible:

- **Limitation Period:** Must be filed within **30 days** of the pronouncement of the judgment or order (condonable only on sufficient cause).
- **Three Narrow Grounds:**
  1. **Discovery of new and important matter or evidence** which, despite exercising due diligence, was not within the petitioner's knowledge or could not be produced when the decree was passed;
  2. **Mistake or error apparent on the face of the record** (a patent, self-evident error—not one requiring elaborate re-argument); or
  3. **Any other sufficient reason** strictly analogous to the first two grounds (in criminal cases, restricted strictly to an error apparent on the face of the record).
- **Bench & Mode of Hearing:** A Review Petition goes before the **same Bench** (as far as practicable) that delivered the original judgment, and is ordinarily decided **in chambers by circulation** without oral arguments by lawyers.
- **Crucial Death-Penalty Exception (***Mohd. Arif @ Ashfaq v. Registrar, Supreme Court*, 2014, 5-Judge Bench**):** Recognising that *"death is different"* and irreversible under `Article 21`, the Constitution Bench carved out a mandatory exception: **every Review Petition involving a death sentence MUST be heard in open court** by a bench of **at least three judges** (with limited oral arguments, generally up to 30 minutes)!

#### 2. Curative Petition (Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra, 2002)

What if even a Review Petition is dismissed, yet a litigant discovers a catastrophic defect—such as a flagrant violation of **natural justice** (an affected party was never served notice or heard) or an undisclosed **judicial bias** (a judge failed to disclose a personal interest in the dispute)? Confronted with that constitutional dilemma, a unanimous **five-judge Constitution Bench** in ***Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra* (10 April 2002)** exercised the Court's inherent powers rooted jointly in **`Articles 137` and `142`** to craft an extraordinary **"second and final" remedy**—the **Curative Petition**—available **ONLY after the dismissal of a Review Petition**.

To ensure that Curative Petitions remain the *"rarest of rare"* remedy rather than an endless third round of litigation, *Rupa Ashok Hurra* imposed five stringent procedural gates (codified in **Order XLVIII of the SC Rules, 2013**):

1. **Narrow Substantive Grounds:** Available solely to **prevent abuse of the process of court** or to **cure a gross miscarriage of justice** (specifically breach of *audi alteram partem* / natural justice, undisclosed apprehension of judicial bias, or grave manifest illegality).
2. **Prior Review Exhaustion:** The petitioner must specifically aver that the exact grounds urged were already taken in the Review Petition and dismissed by circulation.
3. **Mandatory Senior Advocate Certification:** The petition **MUST be accompanied by a certificate from a Senior Advocate** confirming that the strict *Rupa Ashok Hurra* requirements are fulfilled (unlike ordinary Review Petitions filed through an Advocate-on-Record).
4. **Special Circulation Bench:** Every Curative Petition is first circulated in chambers to a bench consisting of the **three senior-most Judges of the Supreme Court (including the CJI) PLUS the Judges who passed the original judgment** (if still sitting).
5. **Open-Court Threshold & Exemplary Costs:** Only if a **majority** of those judges concludes that the matter deserves hearing is it listed in open court (as far as possible before the same bench); if found frivolous or vexatious, the Court imposes **exemplary costs** on the petitioner.

Across more than two decades, Curative Petitions have succeeded in barely a handful of extraordinary cases—most famously ***Navneet Kaur v. State (NCT of Delhi)* (2014)** (commuting Devender Pal Singh Bhullar's death sentence due to inordinate mercy-petition delay and schizophrenia) and, in a landmark commercial-public-interest ruling on **10 April 2024**, ***Delhi Metro Rail Corporation (DMRC) v. Delhi Airport Metro Express Pvt. Ltd. (DAMEPL)*** (`2024 INSC 292`), where a three-judge Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud allowed DMRC's curative petition, set aside the SC's own 2021 judgment, and **vacated an arbitral liability exceeding ₹8,000 crore** against the public-sector metro operator on the ground that the earlier judgment had overlooked patent illegality and inflicted a *"grave miscarriage of justice."*

```compare
# Review Petition (Art. 137) vs Curative Petition (Rupa Ashok Hurra, 2002)
Review Petition | Curative Petition
Constitutional / Legal Basis :: Expressly provided in Article 137 + Order XLVII, SC Rules 2013 | Judicially crafted under Articles 137 & 142 (Rupa Ashok Hurra, 2002) + Order XLVIII
Stage & Time Limit :: Filed directly after original judgment; within 30 days | Filed ONLY after dismissal of a Review Petition; within reasonable time

Mandatory Certification :: Filed through an Advocate-on-Record (AoR) | Mandatory certificate by a Senior Advocate verifying Hurra criteria
Bench Composition :: Same Bench that delivered the original judgment (as far as practicable) | Three senior-most SC judges + Judges of the original Bench (if available)
Hearing Mode :: Chambers by circulation (OPEN COURT mandatory for death penalty — Mohd. Arif, 2014) | Chambers first; listed in open court ONLY if a majority finds prima facie merit
```

### Advocates in the Supreme Court

Practising before the Supreme Court is governed by the **Advocates Act, 1961** and **Order IV of the Supreme Court Rules, 2013**, which classify advocates appearing before the apex court into **three strictly distinct categories**—a niche but recurring topic in M. Laxmikanth and UPSC Prelims:

1. **Senior Advocates (`Section 16(2)` of the Advocates Act, 1961):** Advocates designated as "Senior" by the **Supreme Court or any High Court** by virtue of their ability, standing at the Bar, or special knowledge and experience in law. Designation carries both prestige (distinctive rectangular flap gown and pre-audience priority) and strict professional restrictions: a Senior Advocate **cannot file a *vakalatnama* or appear in the Supreme Court without an Advocate-on-Record** (nor in a High Court without an instructing junior advocate), cannot directly accept briefs or draft pleadings/affidavits from clients, and cannot conduct routine procedural mentions.
   - **Major 2025 Procedural Overhaul (***Jitender @ Kalla v. State (Govt. of NCT of Delhi)*, 13 May 2025, `2025 INSC 667`**):** Earlier, in *Indira Jaising-1* (2017) and *Indira Jaising-2* (2023), the SC had instituted a **100-point rubric** evaluated by a five-member Permanent Committee (CJI + 2 senior judges + AG + 1 Bar member) interviewing candidates. Finding that the rigid point system had become mechanical and vulnerable to manipulation, a three-judge Bench (Justices A.S. Oka, Ujjal Bhuyan, and S.V.N. Bhatti) on **13 May 2025 discarded the 100-point assessment framework completely**! Under the revised 2025 guidelines, applications (plus *suo motu* deserving names with consent) are screened for basic eligibility (**minimum 10 years' practice**; minimum one annual exercise) by a Permanent Secretariat and placed directly before the **Full Court** for collective holistic decision-making, while strictly barring individual judges from personally recommending candidates.
2. **Advocates-on-Record (AoR):** The institutional backbone of Supreme Court filings! **ONLY Advocates-on-Record are entitled to file any appearance, pleading, petition, affidavit, or act for a party in the Supreme Court of India.** To qualify as an AoR under the SC Rules, an advocate must: (a) complete at least **four years of legal practice**; (b) undergo **one year of continuous training** under a senior AoR of at least ten years' standing; (c) pass the rigorous **Supreme Court AoR Examination** (covering practice/procedure, drafting, professional ethics, and leading cases); and (d) maintain an office within **16 kilometres of the Supreme Court** in Delhi with a registered clerk.
3. **Other Advocates:** Any advocate whose name is entered on the roll of **any State Bar Council** maintained under the Advocates Act, 1961. They are entitled to **appear and argue** any matter before the Supreme Court on behalf of a party, **HOWEVER, they are NOT entitled to file any document or matter** directly before the Court unless instructed/filed through an Advocate-on-Record!

> [!prelims]
> **Five High-Yield UPSC Prelims Traps on SC Jurisdiction and Advocates:**
> 1. **Article 131 Exclusions:** Inter-state river water disputes (`Art. 262`), pre-Constitution treaties, Finance Commission referrals, commercial disputes, and suits by **private citizens** are ALL excluded from `Article 131`.
> 2. **Article 136(2) Exclusion:** Special Leave Petitions apply to **every** court and tribunal (final *and* interlocutory orders), **EXCEPT military tribunals and courts-martial** constituted under Armed Forces laws.
> 3. **Article 143(1) vs 143(2):** Under `143(1)` (public importance), the SC **may decline** to answer (*Ismail Faruqui*, 1994); under `143(2)` (pre-Constitution treaties), the SC **must** answer—yet the opinion is **NOT legally binding** under **EITHER** clause!
> 4. **Article 141 Precedent Trap:** Law declared by the SC binds all Indian courts **EXCEPT the Supreme Court itself** (*Bengal Immunity*, 1955).
> 5. **Advocate-on-Record Monopoly:** Any enrolled Indian advocate may *argue* before the Supreme Court, but **ONLY an Advocate-on-Record (AoR)** can *file* a petition or appearance in the SC (neither a Senior Advocate nor an ordinary advocate can file directly!).

## 4. Separation of Powers and Checks and Balances

Every constitutional democracy confronts an enduring structural paradox: a State must possess sufficient concentrated power to govern a complex society, yet unchecked concentration of power inevitably extinguishes individual liberty. The **doctrine of separation of powers** addresses this paradox by dividing sovereign authority into three distinct functions—**legislative** (making laws), **executive** (executing laws and administering policy), and **judicial** (interpreting laws and adjudicating disputes)—and assigning them to separate institutional organs so that no single body becomes lawmaker, enforcer, and judge simultaneously.

### Classical Doctrine vs Functional Checks and Balances

Intellectual roots of divided governance trace back to **Aristotle's *Politics*** (which distinguished deliberative, magisterial, and judicial elements) and **John Locke's *Two Treatises of Government* (1689)**. However, the doctrine received its definitive modern formulation from French jurist **Baron de Montesquieu** in his monumental treatise ***De l'Esprit des Lois* (*The Spirit of the Laws*, 1748)**, inspired by his reading of the British constitutional balance:

> "When the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person, or in the same body of Magistrates, there can be no liberty; because apprehensions may arise, lest the same monarch or senate should enact tyrannical laws, to execute them in a tyrannical manner. Again, there is no liberty, if the judicial power be not separated from the legislative and executive." — Baron de Montesquieu, *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748), Book XI, Chapter VI

In its strictest, "pure" theoretical form, separation of powers demands three watertight rules: (1) the same person must not serve in more than one organ of government; (2) no organ may interfere with or control the working of another; and (3) no organ may exercise a function belonging to another. Yet constitutional practice quickly proved that absolute, hermetically sealed separation is unworkable in a modern regulatory welfare state. Legislatures lack the technical time to draft thousands of granular statutory rules (necessitating **delegated legislation**), administrative agencies must resolve specialised disputes (necessitating **tribunals**), and zero coordination between lawmakers and executives breeds institutional paralysis.

Democracies therefore pair **functional differentiation** with the complementary principle of **Checks and Balances**—expressed memorably by James Madison in *Federalist No. 51* (*"Ambition must be made to counteract ambition"*). Even the **United States**, the classic exemplar of rigid separation (where a separately elected President and Cabinet sit outside Congress), embeds robust mutual checks: Presidential veto over Bills, Senate confirmation of federal judges and treaties, Congressional impeachment of the President and judges, and judicial review by Article III courts.

### Separation of Powers Under the Indian Constitution

Unlike the American Constitution—whose Articles I, II, and III explicitly vest legislative, executive, and judicial powers in three distinct branches—**India's Constitution contains NO single express clause proclaiming a rigid separation of powers**. During the Constituent Assembly Debates in December 1948, **Prof. K.T. Shah** moved an amendment (Draft `Article 40-A`) seeking an explicit constitutional mandate separating all three branches. **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** opposed and defeated the proposal because India had consciously chosen a **Westminster parliamentary executive** (`Article 75(3)`), where the Prime Minister and Ministers *must* sit inside the Legislature and remain collectively accountable to the Lok Sabha—making American-style personnel separation structurally impossible.

Instead, India adopted **functional differentiation fortified by five specific constitutional buffers**:

- **`Article 50` (DPSP):** Directs the State to take steps to **separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State**—operationalised nationwide through the **Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC), 1973** (carried forward in the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita — BNSS, 2023**, effective 1 July 2024), which bifurcated **Judicial Magistrates** (under High Court control) from **Executive Magistrates** (District Magistrates / SDMs performing law-and-order roles).
- **`Articles 53(1)` and `154(1)`:** Vest executive power distinctly in the President and Governors, while leaving legislative power to Parliament and State Legislatures (`Articles 245–246`) and judicial power to the integrated courts (`Articles 124–147, 214–237`).
- **`Articles 121` and `211`:** Prohibit Parliament and State Legislatures from discussing the conduct of any Supreme Court or High Court judge in the discharge of judicial duties, **except** upon a formal removal motion.
- **`Articles 122` and `212`:** Prohibit courts from inquiring into the validity of parliamentary or state legislative proceedings on the ground of any alleged **irregularity of procedure**—though substantive illegality or unconstitutionality remains open to judicial review (***Raja Ram Pal v. Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha*, 2007**).
- **`Article 361`:** Grants the President and State Governors personal immunity from court answerability for official acts and immunity from criminal arrest during their term, without shielding unconstitutional State action from judicial scrutiny (*Rameshwar Prasad*, 2006).

#### Deliberate Functional Overlaps in India's Constitutional Design

Alongside those buffers, the Constitution purposefully weaves functional intersections across all three organs to sustain agile parliamentary governance and reciprocal oversight:

- **Executive Performing Legislative & Quasi-Judicial Functions:** Promulgation of **Ordinances (`Articles 123, 213`)** when legislatures are not in session (checked by ***D.C. Wadhwa*, 1987** and the seven-judge Bench in ***Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar*, 2017**, declaring repeated re-promulgation without placing Ordinances before the House a *"fraud on the Constitution"*); **delegated legislation** (rules, regulations, by-laws); President and Governors acting as integral components of legislatures (`Articles 79, 168`) exercising Bill-assent powers (`Articles 111, 200–201`); **pardoning powers (`Articles 72, 161`)** (subject to narrow judicial review for *mala fides* or arbitrariness, ***Epuru Sudhakar*, 2006**); adjudication of MP/MLA disqualifications on binding ECI advice (`Articles 103, 192`); and executive-appointed administrative tribunals.
- **Legislature Performing Judicial & Executive-Linked Functions:** Impeachment of the President (`Article 61`); removal of Supreme Court and High Court judges (`Articles 124(4), 217`); adjudication of **parliamentary privileges and contempt (`Articles 105, 194`)** (limited by ***Sita Soren*, 2024**, ending bribery immunity); Tenth Schedule anti-defection adjudication by Speakers/Chairmen (held reviewable as quasi-judicial tribunals in ***Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992**); and ministerial membership inside the Houses (`Article 75(3)`).
- **Judiciary Performing Rule-Making & Administrative Functions:** Framing Supreme Court rules of practice (`Article 145`); exclusive administrative control over court staff (`Articles 146, 229`) and subordinate judiciary (`Article 235`); declaring binding law with legislative-like nationwide force (`Article 141`); and issuing equitable decrees for "complete justice" (`Article 142`).

```flow
# Checks and Balances Across India's Three Constitutional Organs
Union & State Legislatures (Arts. 79, 245, 368) | Enact statutes, amend Constitution, control Budget, hold Cabinet accountable (Art. 75(3)), remove Judges (Art. 124(4)) & impeach President (Art. 61)
Executive Branch (Arts. 53, 74, 123, 154) | Implements laws, drafts Bills, issues Ordinances (checked by legislature), appoints Judges (checked by Collegium), grants pardons (Arts. 72, 161) || Integrated Judiciary (Arts. 13, 32, 124–147, 226) | Strikes down ultra vires laws & arbitrary executive orders via Judicial Review, guards Basic Structure & enforces Fundamental Rights

```

### Landmark Supreme Court Jurisprudence on Separation of Powers

Across seventy-five years of constitutional adjudication, the Supreme Court has distilled India's separation-of-powers jurisprudence into one lucid governing rule: **incidental functional overlap is constitutionally permissible, but NO organ may assume an essential function belonging to another organ or destroy the system of checks and balances.**

The classic judicial exposition came from Chief Justice B.K. Mukherjea in ***Rai Sahib Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab* (1955)**, upholding the Punjab Government's executive textbook-publishing policy even without a specific enabling statute so long as it did not infringe Fundamental Rights or existing laws:

> "The Indian Constitution has not indeed recognized the doctrine of separation of powers in its absolute rigidity but the functions of the different parts or branches of the Government have been sufficiently differentiated and consequently it can very well be said that our Constitution does not contemplate assumption, by one organ or part of the State, of functions that essentially belong to another." — Chief Justice B.K. Mukherjea, *Rai Sahib Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab* (1955)

Every subsequent milestone built directly upon *Ram Jawaya Kapur*, culminating in a remarkable pair of back-to-back rulings in **November 2025** that policed **both** sides of the constitutional *Lakshman Rekha*:

#### The Doctrine of Legislative Abrogation vs Unconstitutional Legislative Override

A subtle separation-of-powers boundary often tested in Mains GS-2 is **how far Parliament may go after the Supreme Court strikes down a law**. In ***NHPC Ltd. v. State of Himachal Pradesh* (6 September 2023)**, the Court crystallised the distinction between **valid legislative abrogation** and **invalid legislative override**:

- **Valid Legislative Abrogation (Curing the Basis):** Parliament or a State Legislature may enact a fresh validating law (even retrospectively) that genuinely removes the underlying statutory or factual defect upon which the earlier judgment rested, thereby altering the legal foundation of the dispute.
- **Invalid Legislative Override (Usurping Judicial Power):** A legislature **cannot directly overrule a judicial verdict by bare legislative declaration** or re-enact the exact unconstitutional clauses *"notwithstanding any judgment of any court"* without curing the constitutional flaw—because that converts the legislature into an appellate court over the judiciary!

That exact principle animated the dramatic **twin verdicts of 19–20 November 2025** delivered by Benches led by outgoing 52nd CJI B.R. Gavai. On **19 November 2025** (***Madras Bar Association v. Union of India*, `2025 INSC 1330`**), the Supreme Court checked the **Legislature and Executive**, striking down re-enacted provisions of the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** (covering 4-year tenures, 50-year minimum age, and executive-dominated selection panels) because Parliament had verbatim replicated clauses of the 2021 Ordinance already declared unconstitutional in *Madras Bar Association-V* (July 2021)—calling it *"legislative override in the strictest sense."* Directly responding to this verdict's mandate, Parliament enacted the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026** (assented **13 August 2026**), repealing the 2021 Act and creating a five-member **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)** to oversee 16 key tribunals. Less than twenty-four hours later, on **20 November 2025** (***Special Reference No. 1 of 2025***), a five-judge Constitution Bench checked the **Judiciary itself**, holding that courts cannot rewrite `Articles 200–201` with numerical deadlines or use `Article 142` for *"deemed assent."* Together, those back-to-back rulings demonstrated India's constitutional separation of powers working symmetrically in both directions.

### Parliamentary Sovereignty (UK) vs Judicial Supremacy (USA) vs Indian Constitutional Synthesis

Constitutional democracies historically gravitate toward two opposite poles of institutional authority—**British Parliamentary Sovereignty** and **American Judicial Supremacy**—while India forged a distinctive middle synthesis grounded in **Constitutional Supremacy**.

1. **United Kingdom — Parliamentary Sovereignty:** Classicised by jurist **A.V. Dicey**, the UK operates under an unwritten (uncodified) constitution where **Parliament is legally omnipotent**: it can make, amend, or repeal any law whatsoever, and **no court in the UK can strike down an Act of Parliament** as unconstitutional. Even under the **Human Rights Act, 1998**, British courts cannot invalidate an incompatible statute; they may issue only a non-binding *"declaration of incompatibility"* inviting Parliament to amend the law. Importantly, Britain's **Constitutional Reform Act, 2005** ended the centuries-old fusion of the House of Lords and the Lord Chancellor by establishing an independent **UK Supreme Court** (operational **October 2009**)—strengthening judicial separation **without** ending Diceyan parliamentary sovereignty!
2. **United States — Judicial Supremacy:** Operating under a brief written federal Constitution (7 Articles, 27 Amendments) and rigid separation of powers, the US Supreme Court—beginning with ***Marbury v. Madison* (1803)**—asserted sweeping **judicial review** amplified by the broad **"due process of law"** guarantees of the **Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments**. Because US courts review not only procedural fairness but also the *substantive reasonableness* of federal and state laws (historically striking down socio-economic welfare legislation during the *Lochner* era), the American system exemplifies **judicial supremacy**.
3. **India — Constitutional Supremacy (The Indian Synthesis):** India's framers deliberately rejected *both* extremes. On one hand, India has a written federal Constitution with justiciable Fundamental Rights (`Article 13`), preventing British-style legislative omnipotence: the Supreme Court can invalidate any Union or State law violating legislative competence, Part III, or express text, and can strike down constitutional amendments breaching the **Basic Structure**. On the other hand, Parliament enjoys wide amending power under `Article 368`, may cure statutory defects via validating laws, and originally operated within a narrower textual review perimeter than the United States. Neither Parliament nor the Supreme Court is sovereign in India; **the Constitution alone is supreme**.

```compare
# UK Parliamentary Sovereignty vs US Judicial Supremacy vs Indian Constitutional Synthesis
United Kingdom (Parliamentary Sovereignty) | United States (Judicial Supremacy) | India (Constitutional Supremacy)
Constitutional Type :: Unwritten / uncodified, unitary, flexible | Brief written federal Constitution (rigid amendment) | Detailed written federal Constitution (blend of rigid & flexible)
Executive–Legislature :: Westminster fusion; Cabinet drawn from Parliament | Rigid presidential separation; Cabinet outside Congress | Westminster fusion (Art. 75(3)) + functional checks
Judicial Review of Statutes :: None — courts CANNOT strike down Acts of Parliament (only HRA 1998 declarations) | Sweeping review via 'due process of law' (5th & 14th Amendments) | Express textual review (Arts. 13, 32, 226, 246) + Basic Structure (Art. 368)
Judicial Separation Milestone :: Constitutional Reform Act, 2005 created UK Supreme Court (2009) | Independent Art. III courts from 1789; Senate confirms judges | Single integrated judiciary; Art. 50 separation; Collegium primacy
Ultimate Sovereign :: Parliament (Diceyan legal sovereignty) | Written Constitution interpreted with strong Judicial Supremacy | The Constitution of India (harmonised synthesis of Parliament & Judiciary)
```

#### The Evolution From "Procedure Established by Law" to Substantive "Due Process"

A pivotal chapter in India's synthesis occurred around **`Article 21`**. While drafting the Constitution, Constitutional Advisor **Sir B.N. Rau** consulted US Supreme Court Justice **Felix Frankfurter**, who warned that American *"due process of law"* had allowed conservative judges to veto progressive welfare and labour statutes. Heeding that advice, the Constituent Assembly borrowed Japan's narrower phrase **"procedure established by law"** for `Article 21`.

Initially, in ***A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* (1950)**, the Supreme Court interpreted *"procedure established by law"* literally—holding that `Article 21` protected individuals solely against **arbitrary executive action**, not against harsh or unfair **legislative procedure**, provided Parliament had formally enacted a statute. That positivist deference collapsed morally during the 1975–77 Emergency (*ADM Jabalpur*, 1976). In its transformative seven-judge Constitution Bench decision in ***Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978)**, the Court overruled *Gopalan*'s siloed reading and interlocked **`Articles 14, 19, and 21` into the "Golden Triangle"**: any legal procedure depriving a person of life or personal liberty must not merely exist on the statute book, but must be **"fair, just, and reasonable"**—neither arbitrary, fanciful, nor oppressive. Reinforced by ***K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017)** and ***Shayara Bano* (2017)**, *Maneka Gandhi* effectively imported **substantive due process** into Indian constitutional review—moving India closer to American substantive scrutiny on civil liberties while retaining legislative space on socio-economic policy.

> [!prelims]
> **Three Comparative Constitution Traps Frequently Tested in UPSC Prelims:**
> 1. **Article 21 Exact Text:** The Indian Constitution **explicitly uses "procedure established by law"** (borrowed from **Japan**)—it does **NOT** contain the phrase *"due process of law"* (which entered Indian jurisprudence judicially via ***Maneka Gandhi*, 1978**).
> 2. **UK Supreme Court Trap:** Britain's **Constitutional Reform Act, 2005** created the **UK Supreme Court (2009)** and stripped the Lord Chancellor's judicial headship, **WITHOUT** conferring power to strike down Acts of Parliament or ending Parliamentary Sovereignty.
> 3. **Indian Separation Clause Trap:** There is **NO single Article** declaring watertight separation of all three organs in India; **`Article 50` (DPSP)** mandates separating ONLY the **judiciary from the executive in public services**—not separating the executive from the legislature!

## 5. Judicial Review, Judicial Activism, Overreach and PIL

If Separation of Powers defines the structural geometry of India's Republic, **Judicial Review** is the constitutional engine that enforces those boundaries—and **Judicial Activism** and **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)** are the transformative vehicles through which India's Supreme Court democratised access to justice for millions of marginalised citizens. Mastering the exact textual anchors, evolving doctrinal tests, legitimate boundaries, and contemporary reforms across all four concepts is indispensable for both Prelims accuracy and GS-2 Mains distinction.

### Judicial Review: Origin, Constitutional Basis, Categories and Tests

**Judicial review** is the constitutional authority of superior courts (the Supreme Court and the High Courts) to examine the constitutionality of **legislative enactments, constitutional amendments, and executive/administrative actions** of both the Union and the States, and to declare them **void (*ultra vires*)** to the extent they contravene the Constitution.

Historically, the doctrine originated in the **United States** in Chief Justice John Marshall's epochal verdict in ***Marbury v. Madison* (1803)**, which inferred judicial review from the supremacy of a written Constitution even though the US Constitution did not spell out the power explicitly. In India, by contrast, the Constitution supplies explicit textual machinery for judicial review—yet carries a classic UPSC Prelims quirk: **the exact phrase "judicial review" does NOT appear anywhere in the text of the Indian Constitution!** Instead, the power flows directly from **twelve interlocking constitutional provisions**:

- **`Article 13`:** Declares all pre-constitutional (`13(1)`) and post-constitutional (`13(2)`) laws inconsistent with or abridging **Part III Fundamental Rights** void to the extent of inconsistency (the primary textual anchor of judicial review).
- **`Article 32` & `Article 226`:** Empower the Supreme Court and High Courts respectively to issue writs enforcing Fundamental Rights (and legal rights under `Art. 226`).
- **`Articles 131–136` & `Article 143`:** Confer original federal, appellate, special leave, and advisory review jurisdictions on the Supreme Court.
- **`Article 227`:** Confers High Court judicial superintendence over all subordinate courts and tribunals within its territory.
- **`Articles 245, 246, 251, and 254`:** Demarcate territorial and Seventh Schedule federal legislative competence between Parliament and State Legislatures, invalidating *ultra vires* or repugnant laws.
- **`Article 372`:** Subjects pre-constitutional laws continuing in force to adaptation and constitutional conformity.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court elevated judicial review into an **inviolable feature of the Basic Structure**—first in ***Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India* (1980)** (striking down `Articles 368(4)` and `368(5)` inserted by the `42nd Amendment`, which had tried to bar judicial review of constitutional amendments) and definitively in the seven-judge Constitution Bench ruling in ***L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997)**, which declared that the power of judicial review vested in the **High Courts (`Articles 226/227`) and the Supreme Court (`Article 32`)** is an integral and essential feature of the Basic Structure that **cannot be ousted even by a constitutional amendment** (invalidating `Articles 323A(2)(d)` and `323B(3)(d)` to the extent they excluded High Court scrutiny over statutory tribunals).

#### Three Categories of Judicial Review and Modern Doctrinal Tests

Following the authoritative classification articulated by Justice Syed Shah Mohammed Quadri (standardised in M. Laxmikanth), Indian judicial review operates across **three distinct domains**, each governed by sharp, evolving constitutional tests:

| Domain of Judicial Review | Constitutional Anchors | Governing Judicial Scrutiny Tests | Landmark Cases |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Constitutional Amendments** | `Article 368` + implied constitutional limits | **Basic Structure Doctrine**; **Width Test** & **Identity Test** (*M. Nagaraj*, 2006) | *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), *Minerva Mills* (1980), *M. Nagaraj* (2006), *NJAC* (2015) |
| **2. Union, State & Subordinate Legislation** | `Arts. 13, 245–246, 251, 254, 372` | (i) **Legislative competence**; (ii) Breach of **Part III / express text**; (iii) **Manifest Arbitrariness** (`Art. 14`). **NOT** abstract Basic Structure alone (*Anjum Kadari*, Nov 2024) | *Kuldip Nayar* (2006), *Shayara Bano* (2017), *Navtej Johar* (2018), *Electoral Bonds* (Feb 2024), *Anjum Kadari* (Nov 2024) |
| **3. Administrative & Executive Action** | `Arts. 14, 32, 226, 227` | **Illegality**, ***Wednesbury* Irrationality**, **Procedural Impropriety** + **Four-Step Proportionality Test** | *Tata Cellular* (1994), *Modern Dental College* (2016), *K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017), *Anuradha Bhasin* (2020), *Madhyamam Broadcasting* (2023) |

Two modern standards have sharply elevated the rigour of Indian judicial review over the past decade:

1. **Doctrine of Manifest Arbitrariness (`Article 14`):** Crystallised in ***Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017)** (Triple Talaq) and applied in ***Navtej Singh Johar* (2018)** (Section 377 IPC) and the unanimous five-judge Constitution Bench ruling in ***Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India* (Electoral Bonds Case, 15 February 2024)**, this doctrine allows courts to strike down plenary legislation if it is enacted *"capriciously, irrationally and/or without adequate determining principle,"* or is excessive and disproportionate.
2. **Four-Step Proportionality Standard:** Adopted in ***Modern Dental College* (2016)**, ***K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017)**, ***Anuradha Bhasin* (2020 — Kashmir internet shutdowns)**, ***Madhyamam Broadcasting* (2023)**, and ***Electoral Bonds* (2024)**, any State measure restricting a Fundamental Right must satisfy four cumulative prongs: **(i) Legitimate Goal** (lawful state aim); **(ii) Suitability / Rational Nexus** (rational connection between measure and goal); **(iii) Necessity / Least Restrictive Means** (no less intrusive equally effective alternative exists); and **(iv) Balancing *Stricto Sensu*** (the restriction does not impose a disproportionate burden on the right-holder relative to the public benefit).

> [!news]
> **Two Defining Judicial Review Guardrails (2024) — *Electoral Bonds* (Feb 2024) and *Anjum Kadari* (Nov 2024):**
> - **Sharpening Rights Scrutiny — *Electoral Bonds* (15 February 2024, 5-Judge Unanimous Bench):** Struck down the Electoral Bonds Scheme and amendments to the Companies Act, RPA 1951, and Income-Tax Act by combining **four-step proportionality** (anonymous political funding was NOT the least restrictive way to curb black money and violated voters' `Art. 19(1)(a)` right to information) with **manifest arbitrariness** (removing the 7.5% corporate donation cap equated loss-making shell companies with profit-making firms under `Art. 14`).
> - **Disciplining Overbroad Review — *Anjum Kadari v. Union of India* (5 November 2024, `2024 INSC 831`, UP Madarsa Act):** Setting aside an Allahabad High Court verdict that had struck down the UP Board of Madarsa Education Act, 2004 for allegedly violating "secularism" under the Basic Structure, a three-judge Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud reaffirmed a vital doctrinal boundary (earlier articulated in *Raj Narain* 1975 and *Kuldip Nayar* 2006): **an ordinary statute CANNOT be struck down merely for violating abstract, undefined "Basic Structure" concepts**; a statutory challenge must be anchored strictly in **(a) lack of legislative competence** or **(b) violation of Part III or an express constitutional provision**.

#### The Ninth Schedule (Article 31B) and I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)

To immunise early zamindari abolition and land-reform statutes from judicial challenges under the then Fundamental Right to Property, Parliament enacted the **`1st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951`**, inserting **`Article 31B`** and the **Ninth Schedule** (originally holding **13 agrarian statutes**). Over subsequent decades, however, successive governments turned the Ninth Schedule into an omnibus constitutional dustbin, swelling its roster to **284 statutes**—sweeping in wholly non-agrarian enactments such as election amendments (`39th Amendment`) and Tamil Nadu's 69 per cent reservation law (`76th Amendment, 1994`) purely to evade judicial review.

In ***Waman Rao v. Union of India* (1981)**, the Court drew a prospective line at the date of *Kesavananda Bharati*: **24 April 1973**. Settling the issue definitively, a unanimous **nine-judge Constitution Bench** in ***I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu* (11 January 2007)** ended blanket Ninth Schedule immunity forever:

- **No Blanket Immunity Post-24 April 1973:** Every law placed into the Ninth Schedule **on or after 24 April 1973** is fully open to judicial review if it violates Fundamental Rights forming part of the Basic Structure—specifically the **Golden Triangle (`Articles 14, 19, and 21`)** and **`Article 15`**.
- **Direct Impact ("Rights Test") and "Essence of Rights Test":** Courts examine the direct effect and actual impact of a Ninth Schedule statute rather than its mere legislative form; if a law abrogates or destroys the essence of core Fundamental Rights and damages the Basic Structure, it must be struck down notwithstanding `Article 31B`.

#### Self-Imposed Limits on Judicial Review

Judicial review in India is neither absolute nor omnipotent. Courts observe well-settled self-imposed boundaries: (1) a strong **presumption of constitutionality** attaches to every enacted statute; (2) courts exercise deference toward complex **macro-economic, fiscal, and monetary policy decisions** unless manifestly arbitrary or unconstitutional (*BALCO Employees' Union*, 2002; *Vivek Narayan Sharma* / Demonetisation, 2023); (3) internal **legislative procedural irregularities** are shielded (`Articles 122, 212`); (4) **delimitation orders** and electoral constituencies enjoy explicit constitutional immunity (`Article 329(a)`); and (5) per ***Special Reference No. 1 of 2025***, courts decline merits review of `Articles 200–201` Bill-assent choices or pre-enacted Bills (outside `Article 143`).

### Judicial Activism, Judicial Restraint and Judicial Overreach

While **judicial review** is a constitutional *power*, **judicial activism** and **judicial restraint** are competing *judicial philosophies* governing how boldly or cautiously judges deploy that power—and **judicial overreach** marks the contested boundary where activism crosses into unconstitutional usurpation.

#### Origins and Drivers of Judicial Activism

The phrase **"judicial activism"** was coined in **1947** by American historian **Arthur Schlesinger Jr.** in an article in *Fortune* magazine profiling the US Supreme Court. In India, judicial activism emerged decisively in the **mid-to-late 1970s**—partly as institutional atonement for the Supreme Court's capitulation to executive detention during the Emergency (*ADM Jabalpur*, 1976)—and was pioneered by four visionary judges: **Justices V.R. Krishna Iyer, P.N. Bhagwati, O. Chinnappa Reddy, and D.A. Desai**.

Legal scholars (including Upendra Baxi and M. Laxmikanth) identify five structural drivers behind India's activist turn: (1) **legislative vacuums** where Parliament failed to enact urgent protective laws; (2) **executive apathy and administrative collapse** in delivering basic socio-economic rights; (3) **open-textured constitutional promises** inviting harmonious reading of Part III (`Article 21`) with Part IV DPSPs; (4) rising **rights-consciousness among civil society** and marginalised groups; and (5) institutional confidence that an unelected judiciary could act as a counter-majoritarian conscience-keeper.

#### Constructive Judicial Activism: Expanding Substantive Democracy

At its finest, Indian judicial activism transformed the Constitution from a formal lawyer's document into a living charter of human dignity:

- **Substantive Expansion of `Article 21`:** Read into the bare words *"life or personal liberty"* an expansive catalogue of implied positive rights—right to speedy trial (*Hussainara Khatoon*, 1979), livelihood (*Olga Tellis*, 1985), clean environment (*Subhash Kumar*, 1991), primary education (*Unni Krishnan*, 1993 — catalysing the `86th Amendment` / `Art. 21A`), health (*Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity*, 1996), transgender self-identification (***NALSA v. Union of India*, 2014**), privacy (***K.S. Puttaswamy*, 2017**), passive euthanasia with dignity (*Common Cause*, 2018), and consensual adult autonomy (*Navtej Singh Johar*, 2018).
- **Filling Genuine Legislative Vacuums:** Issued binding interim norms under `Articles 141–142` where Parliament had left glaring rights voids—most celebratedly ***Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997)** (workplace sexual-harassment guidelines governing India for 16 years until the **POSH Act, 2013**), ***D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal* (1997)** (11 mandatory arrest and custodial-detention safeguards later codified in CrPC / BNSS), ***M.C. Mehta* (1986 onwards)** (**Absolute Liability** doctrine after Oleum gas leak, Taj Trapezium protection, Delhi CNG transition), and ***Prakash Singh v. Union of India* (2006)** (seven foundational police-reform directives).

#### Judicial Restraint vs Judicial Overreach

**Judicial restraint** urges judges to respect the democratic mandate of elected legislatures, decide only genuine constitutional breaches, avoid substituting judicial policy preferences for administrative expertise, and preserve separation of powers. When proactive rights enforcement slides into routine **policy-making, fiscal budgeting, or executive micro-management**—where judges possess neither democratic accountability nor institutional capacity to bear socio-economic consequences—activism degenerates into **judicial overreach**.

In ***Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass* (6 December 2007)**, setting aside lower-court directions ordering a state body to create new permanent posts for daily-wage workers, Justice Markandey Katju issued Indian jurisprudence's classic admonition against overreach:

> "Judges must know their limits and must not try to run the Government. They must have modesty and humility, and not behave like Emperors. There is broad separation of powers under the Constitution and each organ of the State—the legislature, the executive and the judiciary—must have respect for the others and must not encroach into each other's domains." — Justice Markandey Katju, *Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass* (2007)

Four high-profile episodes are routinely cited in UPSC Mains as textbook illustrations of **judicial overreach**:

1. **Highway Liquor-Vend Ban (December 2016):** Invoking `Article 142`, the SC imposed a sweeping 500-metre ban on liquor shops along all National and State Highways—triggering severe state excise-revenue shocks, mass hospitality job losses, and absurd municipal "denotifications" of highways into city roads before the Court substantially diluted the order in 2017–2018.
2. **Mandatory Cinema National Anthem Order (November 2016):** In ***Shyam Narayan Chouksey v. Union of India***, the Court ordered all cinema halls nationwide to play the National Anthem before every movie screening with mandatory standing and closed exit doors—a non-statutory patriotism mandate widely criticised as executive legislation from the Bench, which the SC itself modified into an *optional* directive in **January 2018**.
3. **Administrative and Sports Micro-Management:** Running the **Board of Control for Cricket in India (BCCI)** through court-appointed Committees of Administrators (Lodha reforms), entertaining censorship review petitions over films (such as the Bombay HC's *Jolly LLB 2* four-cut directive, 2017), and issuing granular urban-governance mandates (e.g., setting Diwali firecracker hours or directly administering municipal demolition/traffic plans).
4. **Rigid Bill-Assent Timelines and "Deemed Assent" (April 2025):** The two-judge *State of Tamil Nadu* order imposing fixed 1–3 month constitutional deadlines and invoking `Article 142` for *"deemed assent"*—a classic overreach self-corrected seven months later by the five-judge Bench in ***Special Reference No. 1 of 2025* (20 November 2025)**.

#### The Opposite Peril: Judicial Abdication and "Sealed Cover Jurisprudence"

A truly balanced Mains analysis never treats judicial restraint as blind passivity. Just as **judicial overreach** imperils democratic governance, its mirror opposite—**judicial abdication** (excessive executive deference, prolonged non-listing of sensitive constitutional challenges, or uncritical acceptance of secret executive memos)—leaves citizens defenceless against State arbitrariness. Confronting the growing practice of government agencies handing confidential material directly to judges in closed envelopes without sharing it with affected litigants—derisively termed **"sealed cover jurisprudence"**—a Division Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud and Justice Hima Kohli in ***Madhyamam Broadcasting Ltd. v. Union of India* (MediaOne Case, 5 April 2023)** struck down the Centre's refusal to renew a news channel's broadcast licence. Declaring routine sealed covers antithetical to **natural justice (*audi alteram partem*) and open justice**, the Court mandated a structured, least-restrictive **Public Interest Immunity (PII)** procedure (using redactions or judicial summaries) whenever national security confidentiality is claimed.

> [!remember]
> **Avoid the One-Sided Trap in GS-2 Mains Answers:**
> Always frame India's constitutional equilibrium against **BOTH** twin hazards: **Judicial Overreach** (*Aravali Golf Club*, highway liquor ban, April 2025 deemed assent) **AND Judicial Abdication** (unquestioned sealed-cover executive deference curbed in ***Madhyamam Broadcasting*, April 2023**). True constitutionalism lies in **principled judicial review**—vigilant on Fundamental Rights, modest on executive policy.

### Public Interest Litigation (PIL / Social Action Litigation)

The single greatest procedural gift of Indian judicial activism to comparative constitutional law is **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)**—preferentially termed **Social Action Litigation (SAL)** by legal scholar **Upendra Baxi** to highlight that whereas American PIL (originating in the 1960s US legal-aid movement) focused largely on civic participation and consumer/environmental representation, Indian PIL was forged specifically to vindicate the basic survival rights of the **impoverished, bonded, incarcerated, and socially voiceless**.

Pioneered in the late 1970s and early 1980s by **Justices V.R. Krishna Iyer and P.N. Bhagwati**, Indian PIL dismantled the rigid Anglo-Saxon adversarial model through **four revolutionary procedural innovations**:

1. **Relaxation of *Locus Standi* (Representative Standing):** Traditional Anglo-American law insisted that *only* the directly injured person possessed *locus standi* (standing) to sue. In ***S.P. Gupta v. Union of India* (1981)**, Justice P.N. Bhagwati formally declared that whenever a legal wrong or constitutional injury is inflicted on a person or determinate class who—by reason of **poverty, helplessness, disability, or socially or economically disadvantaged position**—cannot approach the court, **any member of the public acting *bona fide*** may invoke `Article 32` (SC) or `Article 226` (HC) on their behalf.
2. **Epistolary Jurisdiction:** Stripping away expensive court fees and technical drafting barriers, the Supreme Court converted **simple letters, postcards, telegrams, and even newspaper clippings** addressed to judges into formal writ petitions—famously treating a prisoner's smuggled note in ***Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration* (1980)** and a three-page letter on Haryana quarry workers in ***Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India* (1984)** as `Article 32` writs!
3. **Non-Adversarial, Collaborative Fact-Finding:** Recognising that destitute petitioners cannot afford forensic evidence against the State, courts appointed independent **socio-legal commissions, expert committees, and *amicus curiae*** to inspect jails, quarries, forests, and mental asylums directly.
4. **Continuing Mandamus:** Rather than disposing of systemic governance failures with a single paper decree, courts keep PILs alive for years, issuing phased interim directions and requiring periodic executive compliance affidavits (e.g., *Vineet Narain*, 1997 on CBI/CVC independence; *T.N. Godavarman* forest conservation; *M.C. Mehta* Ganga and Delhi air pollution).

#### Supreme Court's Ten Permitted PIL Categories and Five Excluded Matters

As PILs proliferated, the Supreme Court framed **PIL Guidelines (1988, updated in 2003)** to channel its epistolary Cell systematically. Memorising the exact distinction between **entertained** and **excluded** categories prevents easy Prelims elimination errors (directly matching M. Laxmikanth, Ch. 29):

- **Ten Permitted Letter-Petition / PIL Categories:**
  1. **Bonded labour** matters;
  2. **Neglected children**;
  3. **Non-payment of minimum wages** to workers and exploitation of casual workers, plus complaints of violation of labour laws (except individual disputes);
  4. **Petitions from jails** complaining of harassment, premature release, seeking release after 14 years, death in jail, release on personal bond, or **speedy trial** as a Fundamental Right;
  5. **Petitions against police** for refusing to register a case, harassment by police, and death in police custody;
  6. **Petitions against atrocities on women**—in particular harassment of bride, bride-burning, rape, murder, kidnapping, etc.;
  7. **Petitions complaining of harassment or torture of villagers by co-villagers or police** from persons belonging to **SC, ST, and economically backward classes**;
  8. **Petitions pertaining to environmental pollution**, disturbance of ecological balance, drugs, food adulteration, maintenance of heritage and culture, antiques, forest and wildlife, and other matters of public importance;
  9. **Petitions from riot-victims**; and
  10. **Family pension** matters.
- **Five Categories Ordinarily EXCLUDED from PIL:** (1) **Landlord–tenant** disputes; (2) **Service matters** and those pertaining to **pension and gratuity** (other than family pension); (3) Complaints against Central/State Government departments and local bodies *except* those falling under the ten permitted categories above; (4) **Admission to medical and other educational institutions**; and (5) **Petitions for early hearing of cases** pending in High Courts and subordinate courts.

#### Misuse of PIL and Judicial Safeguards Against Frivolous Litigation

Ironically, PIL's extraordinary success bred its own pathology: the proliferation of motivated, politically sponsored, blackmailing, or attention-seeking petitions—wryly dubbed **"Publicity Interest Litigation," "Private Interest Litigation," "Political Interest Litigation," or "Paisa Interest Litigation."** Such abuse clogs judicial dockets, delays vital infrastructure projects, and crowds out genuine petitions of undertrials and destitute citizens.

To rescue PIL's noble spirit without closing courthouse doors to the weak, the Supreme Court in ***State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal* (2010)** mandated **eight binding safeguards** across all High Courts and the SC: (1) encourage genuine *bona fide* PILs while effectively discouraging extraneous ones; (2) compel every High Court to frame clear PIL screening rules; (3) **verify the credentials of the petitioner** rigorously before entertaining a PIL; (4) satisfy *prima facie* correctness of the contents; (5) verify that **substantial public interest** is involved; (6) give priority to petitions addressing larger public injury and urgency; (7) confirm there is **no personal gain, private motive, or oblique political agenda**; and (8) **impose exemplary financial costs** on frivolous, busybody, or vexatious litigants (complementing Justice R.S. Pathak's earlier 1984 caution in *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* and Justice A.S. Anand's warning that PIL must remain *"a shield for the weak, not a sword for publicity"*).

### Institutional Reforms and Mains Way Forward

Synthesising all five dimensions of this chapter—composition, removal and independence, jurisdiction, separation of powers, and judicial review/PIL—a high-scoring GS-2 Mains answer must conclude with an actionable, committee-grounded **institutional reform blueprint** balancing **judicial independence (Basic Structure)** with **transparent accountability and operational capacity**:

1. **Finalising a Transparent MoP and Collegium Secretariat:** Break the decade-long post-2016 stalemate by codifying published objective eligibility and diversity criteria, establishing a permanent independent **Collegium Secretariat** for background vetting, institutionalising the **April–May 2025** public asset and Collegium record disclosures as permanent binding norms, and fixing **strict statutory/MoP timelines** for the Union Executive to clear or return recommendations—ending both Collegium opacity and the Centre's *"pocket veto."*
2. **Calibrated Judicial Accountability Beyond All-or-Nothing Removal:** Address the stark accountability gap exposed by the **2025–2026 Justice Yashwant Varma** resignation controversy by enacting a constitutionally sound **Judicial Standards and Accountability framework** (built strictly under independent judicial/non-executive oversight so `Article 124` insulation is never compromised) featuring graded sanctions—advisories, public censure, withdrawal of judicial work, and **post-resignation continuation of proven-misconduct inquiries** affecting pensionary entitlements.
3. **Master of the Roster Transparency, Live-Streaming and Technology:** Institutionalise transparent, rules-based automated case listing for Constitution Benches (addressing concerns raised since January 2018 and *Shanti Bhushan*), expand nationwide **live-streaming of constitutional and public-interest proceedings** (mandated in ***Swapnil Tripathi v. Supreme Court of India*, 2018**, operationalised at the SC from September 2022), and scale **e-Courts Phase III**, AI-assisted vernacular judgment translation (**SUVAS**), and digital filing to democratise access.
4. **Deploying the 38-Judge Strength, SLP Discipline and Regional Benches:** Leverage the **August 2026 expansion to 38 judges** to maintain **permanent, standing Constitution Benches** so vital 5-, 7-, and 9-judge references never stall for years; strictly tighten **`Article 136` SLP admission thresholds** so High Courts remain final courts of routine appeal (curbing the ~95,000-case SC docket); and evaluate the **229th Law Commission Report (2009)** proposal for **four Regional Cassation Benches** (or hybrid virtual benches) to resolve geographic access inequity.
5. **Social Diversity Pipeline and Post-Retirement Cooling-Off Period:** Build proactive, merit-anchored pipelines to lift the representation of **women** (only 3 of 37 sitting SC judges as of November 2026), **SC/ST, OBC, and minority** jurists in the higher judiciary; simultaneously adopt a **mandatory two-year cooling-off period** (aligned with the **14th Law Commission's** caution) before retired apex judges accept post-retirement gubernatorial, political, or executive appointments—paired with full-salary pensions to preserve public trust.
6. **Reciprocal Restraint, Doctrinal Discipline and Open Justice:** Institutionalise mutual respect for the constitutional *Lakshman Rekha* exemplified by the **twin November 2025 verdicts**—legislatures refraining from unconstitutional overrides (*Madras Bar Association*, Nov 2025) and courts eschewing `Article 142` textual rewriting (*Special Reference No. 1 of 2025*, Nov 2025)—while consistently applying **four-step proportionality**, replacing sealed covers with **Public Interest Immunity** (*Madhyamam Broadcasting*, 2023), and enforcing *Balwant Singh Chaufal* PIL gatekeeping.

## 1. Organisation, Composition and Appointment of Judges (Part V, Articles 124–128)

- **Single Integrated Judiciary (`Part V, Arts. 124–147`):** Unified pyramid (from **GoI Act, 1935**; unlike US dual courts) enforcing Union & State laws: SC → 25 HCs → Subordinate Courts. Inaugurated **28 Jan 1950** (1st CJI: **H.J. Kania**), succeeding **Federal Court (1937)** & ending **Privy Council** appeals (**1949 Act**).
- **Sanctioned Strength (`Art. 124(1)`):** Raised by **Parliament via ORDINARY LAW** (NOT `Art. 368`!) under **1956 Act**: **8 (1+7)** → 11 (1956) → 14 (1960) → 18 (1977) → 26 (1986) → 31 (2009) → **34 (1+33)** (2019) → **38 (1+37)** via **SC (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2026** (Act 14 of 2026; assented **11 Aug 2026**, w.e.f. **16 May 2026**, replacing Ord. 1 of 2026 after CJI Surya Kant's 11 May letter citing 9-judge Sabarimala Bench). **Working strength (1 Nov 2026, DoJ): 37** (1 vacancy).
- **CJI Succession & Diversity (Nov 2026):** 51st **Sanjiv Khanna** (Nov 2024–May 2025) → 52nd **B.R. Gavai** (14 May–23 Nov 2025; 1st Buddhist, 2nd SC CJI) → **53rd CJI Surya Kant** (**24 Nov 2025 – 9 Feb 2027**). **13 women** total since **M. Fathima Beevi (Oct 1989)**; **3 sitting of 37**: **B.V. Nagarathna** (Collegium; slated 1st woman CJI Sept 2027, 36 days), **V. Mohana** (2nd direct Bar woman, June 2026), **Sunita Agarwal** (Oct 2026).
- **Appointment & Four Judges Cases (`Art. 124(2)` — "Collegium" NOT in Constitution!):** President appoints after **"consultation"** (CJI mandatory for puisne judges). Seniority breached twice: **A.N. Ray (1973)** & **M.H. Beg (1977)**.
  - ***1st Judges* (*S.P. Gupta*, 1981, 7 JJ):** Consultation ≠ concurrence; **Executive primacy**.
  - ***2nd Judges* (*SCAORA*, 1993, 9 JJ, 7–2):** Consultation = **concurrence**; **Judicial primacy**; **CJI + 2** Collegium; **CJI seniority rule**; binding unanimous reiteration.
  - ***3rd Judges* (*Special Ref. 1/1998*, 9 JJ):** **CJI + 4** (SC) / **CJI + 2** (HC) + parent-HC consultee; sole CJI view invalid; **2 dissents** block a name.
  - ***4th Judges* (*SCAORA*, Oct 2015, 5 JJ, 4–1; Chelameswar J. dissent):** Voided **`99th Amend`** (`Arts. 124A–124C`) & **NJAC Act** (6 members, 2-member veto) for breaching **Judicial Independence (Basic Structure)**; revived Collegium; revised **MoP** stalled since 2016.
- **2025 Transparency:** Full Court (**1 Apr 2025**) mandated **website asset disclosure**; **5 May 2025** published appointment workflow & Nov 2022–May 2025 Collegium approvals (category & judicial-family ties).
- **Qualifications, Oath, Tenure & Salaries (`Arts. 124–125`):** Citizen + **5 yrs HC Judge** OR **10 yrs HC Advocate** OR **"Distinguished Jurist"** (**unused; SC ONLY, NOT HCs**); **NO minimum age / minimum tenure**. **Third Schedule** oath (`16th Amend`). Retires at **65** (HC: 62); age disputes per **Parliament's law (`124(2A)`, 15th Amend)** (HC `217(3)`: President + CJI); resigns to **President** (*ex proprio vigore*, *Gopal Chandra Mishra* 1978). Salaries (2018 Amend): **CJI ₹2.80 lakh**, **SC Judge ₹2.50 lakh**; cut ONLY under **`Art. 360`**; CFI-charged.
- **Temporary Judges (`126–128`), Seat (`130`) & Bench (`145`):** **Acting CJI (`126`)** by **President**; **Ad-hoc (`127`)** = HC judge by **CJI** (President + HC CJ) for **quorum**; **Retired (`128`)** by **CJI** (President + judge; **NOT deemed SC judge**). **Seat (`130`):** Delhi or elsewhere by **CJI + President** (discretionary; **229th Law Comm. 2009** 4 Cassation Benches). **`Art. 145(3)`:** ≥**5 judges** for constitutional questions & `Art. 143` (largest: 13 JJ *Kesavananda*). CJI = **Master of the Roster** (*Shanti Bhushan*, 2018).

## 2. Removal and Independence of the Supreme Court

- **Grounds & Term (`Art. 124(4)`):** Word **"impeachment" NOT used** (ONLY President `Art. 61`!). **ONLY 2 grounds:** **"proved misbehaviour"** or **"incapacity."** Not President's pleasure.
- **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 (`Art. 124(5)`):** **≥100 LS OR ≥50 RS MPs** → **Speaker/Chairman admits or refuses** → **3-member Inquiry Committee** (SC Judge/CJI + HC CJ + distinguished jurist) → if guilty, **Special Majority** (**>50% total + ≥2/3rd present & voting**) in **BOTH Houses, SAME SESSION** (no Joint Sitting) → **Presidential removal order**. Does **NOT lapse on LS dissolution** (*Sub-Committee on Judicial Accountability*, 1991).
- **Track Record (ZERO Removed):** **V. Ramaswami (1993)** survived LS vote (205 Congress abstentions) despite 11/14 proved charges; **Soumitra Sen** (RS passed 189–17) & **P.D. Dinakaran** resigned (2011); **CJI Dipak Misra (2018)** 64-MP notice refused by RS Chairman.
- **Justice Yashwant Varma Crisis (2025–2026):** Burnt ₹500 notes (14–15 Mar 2025) → Allahabad HC repatriation & SC in-house indictment → **146-MP LS motion** admitted (**12 Aug 2025**) → 3-member panel (reconstituted Mar 2026: Aravind Kumar J., Bombay HC CJ Shree Chandrashekhar, B.V. Acharya) → resigned **9 Apr 2026** → report submitted **18 May** & **tabled 12 Aug 2026** holding all 3 charges **"proved"** → Nov 2026 letter claimed proceedings **"infructuous"** (*Gopal Chandra Mishra*), highlighting the mid-inquiry resignation loophole.
- **Ten Pillars of Independence:** (1) Collegium (`124(2)`); (2) Tenure security (`124(4)`); (3) Fixed service conditions (`125`, cut only `Art. 360`); (4) **CFI-charged** expenses (`112(3)(d), 146(3)`); (5) Conduct **non-discussable** (`Arts. 121, 211`); (6) **Total post-retirement practice ban (`124(7)`** — stricter than HC `220`; executive/RS posts unbarred — **14th Law Comm.** / 2-yr cooling-off debate); (7) Contempt (`129`); (8) Own staff (`146`); (9) Parliament can **enlarge, NEVER curtail** jurisdiction (`138`); (10) Executive separation (`Art. 50`, CrPC 1973 / BNSS 2023). ***Subhash Chandra Agarwal* (2019):** CJI office = **RTI public authority**.

## 3. Complete Jurisdiction and Powers of the Supreme Court (Articles 129–145)

- **Original & Exclusive (`Arts. 131 & 71`):** Federal **legal-right** disputes (Centre–State / State–State); **NO private citizens/PSUs** (*State of Bihar*, 1970). **6 Exclusions:** pre-1950 treaties (`131` proviso), **`Art. 262`** river waters, **Finance Commission**, `Art. 290` expenses/pensions, ordinary commercial disputes, State damage suits vs Centre. **2024 Suits:** ***Kerala* (`2024 INSC 253`, Apr 2024)** `Art. 293` borrowing sent to 5-judge CB; ***West Bengal* (`2024 INSC 502`, Jul 2024)** CBI consent suit maintainable. **`Art. 71`:** President/VP poll disputes (prior acts valid; vacancy no bar, `11th Amend`).
- **Writ (`Art. 32`):** Original, **NOT exclusive** (concurrent with HC `Art. 226`), **ONLY Fundamental Rights** (narrower than `226`), itself a Fundamental Right (non-discretionary; `32(3)` unused).
- **Appellate (`Arts. 132–134A, 139A`):** **Constitutional (`132`)** final order + `134A` certificate; **Civil (`133`)** substantial question of general importance (**`30th Amend 1972`** dropped ₹20,000 bar; no single-judge appeal); **Criminal (`134`)** **as of right** if HC reverses acquittal/withdraws trial for **death** (**1970 Act** added **life / 10+ yrs**; none on acquittal!) or via `134A` certificate. **`134A`** (`44th Amend`): immediate certificate. **`139A`** (`42nd`/`44th`): withdraw/transfer HC cases.
- **Special Leave Petition (`Art. 136`):** Plenary **discretionary** appeal against **final/interlocutory** orders of **any court/tribunal**, **EXCEPT Armed Forces courts/tribunals (`136(2)`)**. Drives >80% docket: **92,755–95,954** SC pending cases (Jul 2026 NJDG; >10,000 >10 yrs; ~5.65 cr total India).
- **Advisory (`Art. 143`, GoI 1935 s. 213; absent in US):** **`143(1)`** public importance → *may decline* (declined 1993 Ayodhya *Ismail Faruqui* 1994 & 1982 J&K Resettlement in 2001); **`143(2)`** pre-1950 treaties → *must answer*. **Non-binding** under BOTH; ≥5 judges (`145(3)`). **All 16 References (1951–2025):** *Delhi Laws* (51), *Kerala Edu.* (58), *Berubari* (60), *Sea Customs* (63), *Keshav Singh* (64), *Presidential Poll* (74), *Special Courts* (78), *J&K Resettlement* (82/01), *Cauvery* (91), *Ayodhya* (93/94), *3rd Judges* (98), *Gujarat Gas* (01/04), *Gujarat Polls* (02), *Punjab Termination* (04/16), *2G Allocation* (12), and ***Special Ref. No. 1 of 2025* (20 Nov 2025, 5 JJ, CJI Gavai)**: NO rigid `Arts. 200–201` timelines, NO `Art. 142` **"deemed assent"**, merits non-justiciable, only **limited mandamus** for indefinite inaction.
- **Court of Record (`129`), Precedent (`141`) & Equity (`142`):** `129` = evidentiary record + contempt covering HCs/lower courts (*Delhi Judicial Service Assn*, 1991); **1971 Act** = Civil (`2(b)`) vs Criminal (`2(c)` — scandalising/prejudicing/obstructing; **2006 Truth defence** in public interest; UK repealed scandalising 2013). **`141`:** binds all courts **EXCEPT SC itself** (*Bengal Immunity*, 1955; *Sita Soren*, 2024). **`142`:** complete justice (*Union Carbide*, *Ayodhya*, *Perarivalan*, ***Shilpa Sailesh* 2023** irretrievable breakdown divorce, ***Chandigarh Mayor* Feb 2024**), but **cannot override express statutes (*SCBA*, 1998)** or **Constitution (*Special Ref. 2025*)**. `144`: authorities act in aid.
- **Review (`137`) vs Curative (*Rupa Ashok Hurra*, 2002):** Review in **30 days**, same bench, chambers (**open court for death penalty**, *Mohd. Arif*, 2014). Curative post-review (`137 & 142`) for gross miscarriage/bias; **Senior Advocate certificate**; **3 senior-most + original bench**; rare grants: *Navneet Kaur* (2014), ***DMRC v. DAMEPL* (10 Apr 2024, ₹8,000+ cr vacated)**.
- **Advocates:** **Senior Advocates** (`s. 16(2)` — no filing/appearing without AoR; ***Jitender @ Kalla*, 13 May 2025** discarded *Indira Jaising* 100-point rubric → holistic Full Court review + 10-yr rule); **Advocates-on-Record (AoR)** (**EXCLUSIVE right to file in SC**; 4+1 yrs + AoR exam + office ≤16 km); **Other Advocates** (argue only).

## 4. Separation of Powers and Checks and Balances

- **Doctrine & Indian Buffers:** Aristotle, Locke, **Montesquieu** (*The Spirit of the Laws*, 1748). **NO single rigid separation clause** in India (Ambedkar rejected K.T. Shah's Draft Art. 40-A due to `Art. 75(3)` fusion). Five buffers: **`Art. 50`** (judiciary separated from executive — CrPC 1973 / BNSS 2023), **`53(1)/154(1)`**, **`121/211`** (no judge debate), **`122/212`** (procedural immunity; illegality reviewable, *Raja Ram Pal* 2007), **`361`** (personal immunity).
- **Overlaps & Landmarks:** Ordinances (`123, 213` — re-promulgation void, *D.C. Wadhwa* & *Krishna Kumar Singh* 2017), pardons (`72, 161` — *Epuru Sudhakar* 2006), judge removal, Tenth Schedule (*Kihoto Hollohan* 1992). ***In Re: Delhi Laws* (1951)** (essential legislative function non-delegable); ***Ram Jawaya Kapur* (1955)** (sufficient differentiation, no usurpation); ***Raj Narain* (1975)** (**Separation = Basic Structure**); ***Bhim Singh* (2010)** (MPLADS valid); ***NJAC* (2015)**; ***NHPC Ltd.* (Sept 2023)** (valid **abrogation** vs void **override**).
- **Twin Nov 2025 Rulings:** ***Madras Bar Assn* (19 Nov 2025)** voided verbatim re-enacted 2021 Tribunals Act clauses (prompting **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026**, assented **13 Aug 2026**, creating 5-member **National Tribunals Commission**); ***Special Ref. 1/2025* (20 Nov 2025)** checked judicial Bill-assent timelines & deemed assent.
- **Comparative Synthesis & `Art. 21`:** **UK** = Diceyan **Parliamentary Sovereignty** (**2005 Act / 2009 UK SC** did NOT end sovereignty) vs **USA** = **Judicial Supremacy** (5th/14th *"due process"*) vs **India** = **Constitutional Supremacy**. **`Art. 21`** textually reads Japan's **"procedure established by law"** (*Gopalan* 1950); ***Maneka Gandhi* (1978, 7 JJ)** linked `14–19–21` (*"fair, just & reasonable"*) importing **substantive due process**.

## 5. Judicial Review, Judicial Activism, Overreach and PIL

- **Judicial Review:** US origin (***Marbury v. Madison*, 1803**); exact phrase **absent in India's Constitution**; anchored in **`Arts. 13, 32, 131–136, 143, 226, 227, 245–246, 251, 254, 372`**. **Basic Structure:** ***Minerva Mills* (1980)** & ***L. Chandra Kumar* (1997, 7 JJ)**.
- **3 Categories & Tests:** (1) **Amendments:** Basic Structure (*M. Nagaraj* width/identity); (2) **Statutes:** competence + Part III/express text + **manifest arbitrariness** (*Shayara Bano* 2017, *Electoral Bonds* Feb 2024) — **NOT abstract Basic Structure alone** (***Anjum Kadari*, Nov 2024**, UP Madarsa Act); (3) **Executive:** illegality, *Wednesbury*, procedural impropriety (*Tata Cellular*) + **4-step proportionality** (*Puttaswamy* 2017, *Anuradha Bhasin* 2020, *Electoral Bonds* 2024).
- **Ninth Schedule (`Art. 31B`, `1st Amend 1951`, 284 laws):** ***I.R. Coelho* (2007, 9 JJ)** + *Waman Rao* (1981): post-**24 Apr 1973** insertions reviewable under **rights / essence of rights tests** (`Arts. 14, 15, 19, 21` & Basic Structure).
- **Activism vs Overreach vs Abdication:** Coined by **Arthur Schlesinger Jr. (1947)**; Indian 1970s pioneers: **Krishna Iyer, Bhagwati, Chinnappa Reddy, D.A. Desai**. Constructive (*Olga Tellis*, *NALSA*, *Vishaka* → POSH Act 2013, *D.K. Basu*, *M.C. Mehta*, *Prakash Singh*) vs **Overreach** (***Aravali Golf Club*, 2007** Katju J.; 2016 highway liquor ban, cinema Anthem order, BCCI, Apr 2025 deemed assent) vs **Abdication** (***Madhyamam Broadcasting*, Apr 2023** curbed **sealed covers**, adopting **PII** redactions).
- **PIL / Social Action Litigation (Upendra Baxi):** Relaxed ***locus standi*** (*S.P. Gupta*, 1981), **epistolary jurisdiction** (*Sunil Batra*, *Bandhua Mukti Morcha*), fact-finding commissions, **continuing mandamus**. First classic PIL: ***Hussainara Khatoon* (1979)** (**Kapila Hingorani**, "Mother of PIL"). **10 Permitted Categories** (bonded labour, neglected children, minimum wages, jails/speedy trial, police atrocities, women atrocities, SC/ST atrocities, environment/heritage, riot victims, family pension) vs **5 Excluded** (landlord-tenant, service/pension/gratuity, routine dept pleas, educational admissions, early hearing). ***Balwant Singh Chaufal* (2010):** 8 anti-abuse checks & **exemplary costs**.
- **Reforms:** Transparent MoP + secretariat & executive timelines; graded accountability plugging resignation gaps; automated roster + *Swapnil Tripathi* live-streaming; permanent Constitution Benches (38-judge 2026 Act) + SLP discipline + **229th Law Comm.** regional benches; diversity pipeline + **2-yr post-retirement cooling-off**.
