## 1. Three groups of rights, one concern

This note covers three groups of Fundamental Rights: the right against exploitation (Articles 23 and 24), the right to freedom of religion (Articles 25 to 28), and the cultural and educational rights (Articles 29 and 30). They look unrelated, but they answer one concern of the framers: how to protect the weak, the vulnerable and the different in a society marked by deep hierarchy and great diversity. The right against exploitation protects the poor and children from being used. The religious freedoms protect belief while letting the State reform society. And the cultural rights let minorities keep their identity in a democracy where the majority could otherwise outvote them.

## 2. Article 23: no trafficking or forced labour

Article 23 prohibits **traffic in human beings, begar** (forced labour without payment) and **other similar forms of forced labour**. Any breach is an offence punishable by law. The right is available to citizens and non-citizens alike, and it protects against private persons as well as the State.

The Supreme Court has read "forced labour" broadly. In ***People's Union for Democratic Rights* (1982)**, the Asiad workers' case, it held that labour extracted by paying less than the **minimum wage** is forced labour, because a worker driven by poverty to accept it has no real choice. Trafficking covers the selling and buying of men, women and children like goods, immoral traffic in women and children, and devadasi practices.

Parliament has given effect to Article 23 through, among others, the **Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956**, the **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976**, and the minimum wage laws.

> **In the news:** The four **Labour Codes** came into force on **21 November 2025**: the Code on Wages, 2019, the Industrial Relations Code, 2020, the Code on Social Security, 2020, and the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020. They consolidate 29 central labour laws. The Code on Wages, which replaces laws including the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, provides for a national floor wage, so it bears directly on the *PUDR* reading of Article 23.

Article 23 contains one exception. The State may impose **compulsory service for public purposes**, such as military service or social service, and need not pay for it. In doing so it must not discriminate on grounds only of religion, race, caste or class.

## 3. Article 24: no child labour in hazardous work

Article 24 prohibits the employment of **children below the age of 14** in any factory, mine or other hazardous activity, such as construction work or railways. It does not prohibit employment in harmless or innocent work.

Parliament has gone further than the Constitution requires. The **Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016**, renamed the law the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act. It **banned the employment of children below 14 in all occupations and processes**, except in family enterprises after school hours and as artists in the entertainment industry. It also banned the employment of **adolescents aged 14 to 18 in hazardous occupations**. In ***M.C. Mehta* (1996)** the Supreme Court directed the creation of a Child Labour Rehabilitation-cum-Welfare Fund, to which employers of child labour must contribute.

> **Example:** Articles 23 and 24 work together with other provisions to protect children: Article 21A (education for ages 6 to 14), Article 39(e) and (f) (directives against the abuse and exploitation of children), and the **Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005**, which set up the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR).

## 4. Freedom of religion: the Indian approach

Articles 25 to 28 make India a secular state of a particular kind. The State does not establish or favour any religion. It protects every person's freedom to believe and practise, and every religious group's freedom to manage its affairs. But unlike the American model of strict separation, the Indian State may intervene in religion, to regulate its secular activities and to reform social practices such as untouchability or caste exclusion from temples. Scholars describe this as "principled distance": the State keeps away from religion, or engages with it, depending on what equality and liberty require.

## 5. Article 25: conscience, profession, practice and propagation

Article 25 says that all persons are equally entitled to **freedom of conscience** and the right freely to **profess, practise and propagate religion**.

- **Conscience** is the inner freedom to shape one's relationship with God, or with no god at all.
- **Profession** is the open declaration of one's beliefs and faith.
- **Practice** is the performance of religious worship, rituals and ceremonies.
- **Propagation** is the transmission and spread of one's religious beliefs to others, by explaining its tenets. In ***Rev. Stainislaus* (1977)** the Supreme Court held that this **does not include a right to convert** another person to one's own religion, because that would impinge on the other person's freedom of conscience. On this reasoning the Court upheld state laws regulating conversion by force, fraud or inducement.

The freedom is subject to **public order, morality and health**, and to the other Fundamental Rights. Article 25 also lets the State make laws:

- regulating or restricting any economic, financial, political or other **secular activity** associated with religious practice;
- providing for **social welfare and reform**, or throwing open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus.

Two explanations accompany the Article. The **wearing and carrying of kirpans** is included in the profession of the Sikh religion. And for the purpose of throwing temples open, "Hindus" includes Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists.

### Which practices are protected?

Article 25 protects religious practices, but not every practice a believer claims. Since the ***Shirur Mutt* case (1954)** the courts have applied the **essential religious practices** test: only practices that are essential and integral to a religion are protected. The test makes judges decide questions of theology, and it has been criticised for that. It has been central to several recent cases:

- In ***Indian Young Lawyers Association* (September 2018)**, the Sabarimala case, a five-judge bench held by 4 to 1 that excluding women aged 10 to 50 from the Sabarimala temple violated Articles 14, 15, 17 and 25. Justice Indu Malhotra dissented, arguing that courts should not interfere with essential practices of a religious denomination. In November 2019 the review petitions were kept pending, and broader questions about religious freedom were referred to a larger bench.
- In the Karnataka **hijab** case (*Aishat Shifa*, October 2022), a two-judge bench gave a split verdict on a ban on wearing the hijab in government colleges. The case was placed before a larger bench.

## 6. Article 26: managing religious affairs

Article 26 gives every **religious denomination**, or any section of it, four rights:

1. to establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes;
2. to manage its own affairs in matters of religion;
3. to own and acquire movable and immovable property;
4. to administer such property in accordance with law.

Article 25 protects individuals; Article 26 protects religious groups collectively. Article 26 is subject only to public order, morality and health, not to the other Fundamental Rights. The Supreme Court has held that a **religious denomination** must have three features: a common faith, a common organisation, and a distinctive name (*S.P. Mittal*, 1983).

> **In the news:** The **Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025**, which changes how waqf properties are created, registered and governed, was challenged in the Supreme Court partly under Article 26. On **15 September 2025** the Court refused to stay the Act as a whole, but in an interim order it stayed certain provisions, among them the requirement that a person must have practised Islam for five years to create a waqf. That provision is suspended until rules are framed to verify it.

## 7. Articles 27 and 28: taxes and religious instruction

**Article 27** says that no person shall be compelled to pay any **taxes** whose proceeds are specifically used for promoting or maintaining a particular religion or religious denomination. The State should not spend public money collected by taxation to promote a particular religion. The Article bars a tax, but not a **fee**: the State may levy a fee on pilgrims, for example, to provide them with special services or to regulate the secular administration of religious institutions.

**Article 28** deals with religious instruction in educational institutions. Its effect depends on how the institution is run and funded:

| Type of institution | Religious instruction |
|---|---|
| Wholly maintained by the State | Completely prohibited |
| Administered by the State, but established under an endowment or trust that requires religious instruction | Permitted |
| Recognised by the State | Permitted, but only with the student's consent (a guardian's, for a minor) |
| Receiving aid from the State | Permitted, but only with the student's consent (a guardian's, for a minor) |

## 8. Article 29: protecting language, script and culture

Article 29(1) provides that **any section of the citizens** residing in any part of India with a distinct language, script or culture of its own has the right to **conserve** it. Article 29(2) provides that no citizen shall be denied admission into an educational institution maintained by the State, or receiving aid from it, on grounds **only of religion, race, caste or language**.

Article 29 protects both religious minorities and linguistic minorities. The Supreme Court has held that it is not confined to minorities in the technical sense: its words "any section of the citizens" also cover sections of the majority. The right to conserve a language includes the right to agitate for its protection.

## 9. Article 30: minorities' educational institutions

Article 30 gives **all minorities, religious or linguistic**, the right to **establish and administer educational institutions of their choice**. It adds two safeguards. If the State acquires the property of a minority educational institution, the compensation must not restrict or abrogate this right; the 44th Amendment added this when it removed the right to property from Part III. And the State, in granting aid, shall not discriminate against an institution because it is managed by a minority.

Article 30 is available **only to minorities**. The Constitution does not define "minority". In ***T.M.A. Pai Foundation* (2002)**, an eleven-judge bench held that minority status, whether religious or linguistic, must be decided **state by state**, not on the basis of the country as a whole.

The courts have tried to balance the minorities' autonomy against the State's interest in standards. The State may regulate minority institutions to maintain academic quality, but may not interfere with the core of their administration. In *P.A. Inamdar* (2005), the Court held that the State cannot impose reservation policies on unaided minority and non-minority professional colleges. The 93rd Amendment's Article 15(5), which allows reservation in private institutions, expressly exempts minority institutions.

> **In the news:** On **8 November 2024**, a seven-judge bench of the Supreme Court, by a majority of **4 to 3**, overruled ***S. Azeez Basha* (1967)**, which had held that Aligarh Muslim University was not a minority institution because it was established by a central law. The majority held that an institution does not lose minority status merely because a statute created it; what matters is who established it and for whose benefit. The Court left the question of AMU's actual status to a regular bench, applying the tests it laid down.

### Who are the minorities?

Under the **National Commission for Minorities Act, 1992**, the central government has notified six religious communities as minorities at the national level: **Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Parsis** (the first five, notified in 1993) and **Jains** (notified in 2014). The **National Commission for Minority Educational Institutions** (NCMEI), set up in 2004, decides questions about the minority status of educational institutions. Linguistic minorities are identified state by state, and the Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (Article 350B) watches over their safeguards.

> **Mains angle:** These rights generate many of the most contested questions in Indian politics. **Secularism:** the Indian model of principled distance against Western separation, the essential practices test, and judicial intervention in faith (Sabarimala, hijab). **Conversion:** propagation versus the right not to be induced (*Stainislaus*), and the anti-conversion laws. **Minority rights:** autonomy versus regulation (*T.M.A. Pai*, *Inamdar*, *AMU*), and state-wise minority status. **Exploitation:** the persistence of bonded and child labour, the new labour codes, and trafficking. A balanced answer shows how the Constitution tries to protect diversity and belief while still pursuing equality and reform, and why the courts, as interpreters, keep returning to the balance.

- **Article 23:** no trafficking, begar or forced labour. Applies against the State and private persons, and to all persons. Wages below the minimum wage are forced labour (*PUDR*, 1982, the Asiad case). Exception: compulsory service for public purposes, without discrimination on religion, race, caste or class.

- **Article 24:** no child under 14 in factories, mines or hazardous work. The 2016 amendment banned all work under 14 (except family enterprises after school, and entertainment) and hazardous work for ages 14 to 18. *M.C. Mehta* (1996) set up the rehabilitation fund. NCPCR under the 2005 Act.
- **Article 25:** conscience, profession, practice and propagation, for all persons. Subject to public order, morality, health and other Fundamental Rights. The State may regulate secular activities and open Hindu temples to all. Kirpans are covered. "Hindus" includes Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists. No right to convert (*Rev. Stainislaus*, 1977).
- **Essential religious practices** test since *Shirur Mutt* (1954). Sabarimala (*IYLA*, September 2018, 4:1; Justice Indu Malhotra dissented; larger bench referral, November 2019). Hijab case (*Aishat Shifa*, October 2022, split verdict).
- **Article 26:** rights of religious denominations to institutions, management, property and administration. Subject only to public order, morality and health. Denomination test (*S.P. Mittal*, 1983): common faith, organisation and name.
- **Waqf (Amendment) Act 2025:** the Supreme Court's interim order of 15 September 2025 stayed some provisions (such as the five years of practice) but not the whole Act.
- **Article 27:** no tax to promote a religion; a fee is allowed.
- **Article 28:** no religious instruction in wholly State-maintained institutions. Allowed if established under a trust requiring it. With consent in State-recognised or State-aided institutions.
- **Article 29:** any section of citizens may conserve its language, script or culture (majority sections too). No denial of admission to State or State-aided institutions on religion, race, caste or language only.
- **Article 30:** minorities (religious or linguistic) may establish and administer educational institutions. Compensation safeguard (44th Amendment). No discrimination in aid. Minority status is decided state-wise (*T.M.A. Pai*, 2002, eleven judges). No reservation imposed on unaided colleges (*P.A. Inamdar*, 2005). Minority institutions are exempt from Article 15(5).

- **National minorities:** Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists and Parsis (1993), and Jains (2014). NCM Act 1992; NCMEI 2004; Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (Article 350B).
