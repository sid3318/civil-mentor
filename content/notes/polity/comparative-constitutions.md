## 1. Why comparative constitutionalism matters in India

No modern constitution is written on a blank slate, and India's framers never pretended otherwise. Between 1946 and 1949, Constitutional Adviser **B.N. Rau** and the Drafting Committee studied roughly sixty constitutions alongside the administrative frame of the **Government of India Act, 1935**. When critics dismissed the Draft as a "bag of borrowings", Dr. B.R. Ambedkar answered on **4 November 1948** that constitutional fundamentals had long matured worldwide; true originality lay in selective adaptation to cure known institutional flaws for Indian conditions.

> "One likes to ask whether there can be anything new in a Constitution framed at this hour in the history of the world. ... The only new things, if there can be any, in a Constitution framed so late in the day are the variations made to remove the faults and to accommodate it to the needs of the country." — B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 4 November 1948

This **conscious adaptation** placed India at a unique crossroads: Westminster responsibility without parliamentary sovereignty, American rights and review without rigid separation, Canadian strong-Centre unity, Australian concurrency and interstate trade, Irish directives, French republican ideals, Japanese Article 21 phrasing, and South African amendment mechanics.

For UPSC, Prelims tests the exact **divergences** between Indian clauses and their foreign inspirations, while Mains GS-2 evaluates which foreign safeguards can credibly reform Indian institutions.

## 2. India and the United Kingdom: Westminster adapted to a republic

India shares Britain's parliamentary grammar—cabinet accountability, rule of law, single citizenship, bicameralism and prerogative writs—yet parts ways on ultimate legal authority.

Britain has an **uncodified** constitution built from historic statutes (**Magna Carta 1215**, **Bill of Rights 1689**, **Parliament Acts 1911 & 1949**, **Human Rights Act 1998**, **Constitutional Reform Act 2005**), common law and conventions. With no legal wall between ordinary and constitutional statutes, Westminster exercises A.V. Dicey's **Parliamentary Sovereignty**: Parliament can make or unmake any law by simple majority, and no court can void a primary Act. India inverted this rule through **constitutional supremacy**: **Article 13** invalidates rights-violating laws, and the **Basic Structure doctrine** (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973) limits even Article 368 amendments.

The **Constitutional Reform Act 2005** established an independent **Supreme Court of the United Kingdom** (operational **October 2009**), replacing the House of Lords' Law Lords. Unlike India's Supreme Court, it **cannot strike down primary Acts**: under **Section 4 of the Human Rights Act 1998**, it issues only a **declaration of incompatibility**, leaving amendment to Parliament. Yet British courts police executive prerogative vigorously—requiring statutory authority to trigger Brexit in ***Miller* (2017)** and voiding the five-week prorogation of Parliament in ***Miller II* (2019)**.

Two parliamentary offices highlight sharp divergence. Upon election, the UK Commons **Speaker resigns party membership** and runs conventionally unopposed by major parties ("once a Speaker, always a Speaker"); Indian Speakers remain party members while deciding **Tenth Schedule** defections (*Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992). That is why the **Second ARC** wanted defection cases decided on the Election Commission's advice, and why the Supreme Court in ***Keisham Meghachandra Singh* (2020)** suggested an independent tribunal in the Speaker's place. Likewise, India's **Rajya Sabha** is a permanent federal chamber of indirectly elected state representatives plus 12 Presidential nominees (Article 80) with special federal powers (Articles 249, 312), whereas Britain's **House of Lords** is unelected and can delay ordinary public bills by at most one year. Following the 1999 Act (which left 92 "excepted" hereditary peers), Britain enacted the **House of Lords (Hereditary Peers) Act 2026** (Royal Assent **18 March 2026**, effective **29 April 2026**), abolishing the sitting and voting rights of all remaining hereditary peers so the chamber now contains only **life peers** and **26 Lords Spiritual**.

| Dimension | India | United Kingdom |
|---|---|---|
| **Constitutional form** | Codified, supreme text (1950) | Uncodified statutes, common law & conventions |
| **Supremacy** | **Constitutional supremacy** + Basic Structure | **Parliamentary sovereignty** (Diceyan) |
| **Head of State** | Elected **President** (Republic) | Hereditary **Monarch** |
| **Judicial review** | Voids primary Acts & violating amendments | **Cannot void** primary Acts; HRA s. 4 declaration |
| **Speaker** | Keeps party ticket; Tenth Schedule tribunal | **Resigns party**; conventionally unopposed |
| **Upper House** | **Rajya Sabha:** federal, indirectly elected | **House of Lords:** life peers & Lords Spiritual (hereditary peers ended April 2026) |
| **Executive norms** | PM from either House; 6-month non-MP window; no Shadow Cabinet; no countersignature | PM from Commons; MP/Peer ministers only; formal **Shadow Cabinet**; countersignature |

> **In the news:** Receiving Royal Assent on **18 March 2026** and taking effect on **29 April 2026**, the **House of Lords (Hereditary Peers) Act 2026** ended centuries of hereditary voting seats through an ordinary Act—demonstrating the extraordinary flexibility of Britain's uncodified constitution.

> **Prelims pointer:** Never equate British **rule of law** with Indian **judicial supremacy**: UK courts police executive prerogative (*Miller II*, 2019) but cannot invalidate primary Acts of Parliament.

## 3. India and the United States: written federalism on contrasting lines

The **US Constitution (1787, effective 1789)** inspired India's Fundamental Rights, judicial review, independent judiciary, Vice-President's office and Presidential impeachment, yet reflects opposite priorities: American **separation and state sovereignty** versus Indian **accountability and national cohesion**.

Under strict **separation of powers**, the US **President** (both Head of State and Government, fixed **four-year term**, two-term limit via the 22nd Amendment) appoints non-legislator Secretaries, cannot dissolve Congress, and cannot be felled by no-confidence. Checks operate through presidential vetoes (overridable by two-thirds in both Houses), Senate confirmation of treaties and appointments, and judicial review—contrasting with India's parliamentary **fusion** (Articles 74–75).

On federalism, **Alfred Stepan** contrasted America's **"coming-together"** union with India's **"holding-together"** union. Thirteen sovereign colonies pooled limited powers in 1787, forming—as ***Texas v. White* (1869)** declared—an **"indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States"**: Congress cannot alter state borders without state consent. Every US state holds **two Senate seats**, retains **residuary powers** (**Tenth Amendment**), and runs its own constitution, **dual citizenship** and **dual judiciary**. Post-Partition India reversed these defaults: an **indestructible Union of destructible States** (Article 3), single citizenship, an integrated judiciary, population-weighted Rajya Sabha seats, and **Union residuary powers** (Article 248).

Rights and liberty show a parallel contrast. America's **Bill of Rights (1791)** states freedoms unqualifiedly, leaving courts to imply **"police power"** limits, whereas India's Part III codifies explicit **"reasonable restrictions"** (Articles 19(2)–(6)). The US **Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments** guarantee **"due process of law"** (testing both procedure and substantive fairness). Wary of *Lochner*-era invalidation of welfare laws, B.N. Rau—advised by Justice Felix Frankfurter—borrowed **"procedure established by law"** from Japan's Article 31 for India's **Article 21**. After reading it literally in ***A.K. Gopalan* (1950)**, the Supreme Court converged toward substantive due process in ***Maneka Gandhi* (1978)**, demanding procedures be **"right, just and fair"**.

Finally, **Article V** makes US amendment extraordinarily rigid—two-thirds of both Houses (or a convention called by two-thirds of states) plus ratification by **three-fourths of the states** (38 of 50), yielding just **27 amendments** since 1789 versus **106** under India's three-tier **Article 368**. US Supreme Court justices are **nominated by the President, confirmed by the Senate** (simple majority) and hold **life tenure**, unlike India's **Collegium** appointees retiring at **65**. In US impeachment (covering Presidents and federal judges alike), the **House** indicts by simple majority and the **Senate** convicts by **two-thirds present** (Chief Justice presiding for the President). India separates **Article 61 Presidential impeachment** (initiated in *either* House, requiring **two-thirds of total membership** in both Houses solely for "violation of the Constitution") from **Article 124(4)** judicial removal.

| Feature | India | United States |
|---|---|---|
| **Executive** | Parliamentary fusion (dual executive) | Presidential separation (single, fixed 4-yr term) |
| **Federalism** | Holding-together; destructible States (Art. 3) | Coming-together; indestructible States (*Texas v. White*) |
| **Upper House & Residuary** | Population-weighted RS; **Union** residuary (Art. 248) | Equal Senate (**2/state**); **State** residuary (10th Amdt) |
| **Courts & Citizenship** | Integrated judiciary; single citizenship | Dual federal/state courts; dual citizenship |
| **Liberty clause** | Art. 21 "procedure established by law" → *Maneka* | 5th/14th Amdts "**due process of law**" |
| **Amendment** | Art. 368 (special maj. / + half states); **106** | Art. V (2/3 Congress + **3/4 states**); **27** |
| **SC Judges** | Collegium; retires at **65** | Presidential nomination + Senate vote; **life tenure** |

> **Mains angle:** The last two Mains papers asked three India–US comparisons, each answerable from this section. **Pardon (2025):** the US President pardons federal offences on his own judgement, "except in cases of impeachment" (Article II), and the courts have allowed it at any time after the offence, even before a charge. That is a **pre-emptive pardon**: Ford's of Nixon in 1974, and Biden's of January 2025. India's President acts on ministerial advice under Article 72 (*Maru Ram*, 1980), the decision is open to limited judicial review (*Epuru Sudhakar*, 2006), and since the Article speaks of persons "convicted", it is used after conviction, not to forestall a trial. **Appointing judges (2025):** a political process in the open (nomination, Senate hearings and vote, life tenure) against the Collegium's judicial primacy, which protects independence but is criticised as opaque. **Prime Minister and President (2026):** a Prime Minister with a Lok Sabha majority leads the executive and, through the party whip, the legislature too, yet can be removed any day the majority is lost; the US President has a fixed term, a veto and command of the forces, but cannot dissolve Congress or count on it to pass his budget, laws, treaties or nominees.

> **Prelims pointer:** In the US, only the **House** initiates impeachment (simple majority) and only the **Senate** tries it (two-thirds present). Under India's **Article 61**, **either House** initiates, both Houses must pass by **two-thirds of total membership**, and **nominated MPs vote** while **state MLAs do not**.

## 4. India and France: semi-presidentialism, *laïcité* and dual courts

While India's Preamble echoes France's revolutionary triad of **liberty, equality and fraternity**, France's **Fifth Republic (1958)**—crafted by Charles de Gaulle to cure Fourth Republic instability—diverges sharply from India's parliamentary model.

France pioneered **semi-presidentialism**, pairing a **directly elected President** (five-year term since 2000, two-term cap) who chairs Cabinet, leads foreign and defence policy, and can **dissolve the National Assembly** (Article 12), with a **Prime Minister** accountable to the Assembly. Opposing presidential and Assembly majorities create ***cohabitation*** (1986–88, 1993–95, 1997–2002), shifting domestic authority to the Prime Minister. Under **Article 49.3** (rationalised parliamentarism), the Prime Minister may stake government survival on a bill, adopting it **without a vote** unless a **motion of censure**, tabled within 24 hours, is carried by an absolute majority of deputies. Since the **2008 constitutional reform**, Article 49.3 is unlimited for Finance and Social Security Financing Bills but capped at **one** other bill per session. India has **no equivalent**: Ordinances (Article 123) lapse without parliamentary approval within six weeks of reassembly.

> **In the news:** Following France's hung National Assembly elections in mid-2024, Prime Minister **Michel Barnier** triggered **Article 49.3** on 2 December 2024 for the 2025 Social Security Budget; on **4 December 2024**, 331 deputies passed a censure motion—the **first government brought down after using Article 49.3**, and only the second censured in the Fifth Republic (the first was Pompidou's in 1962). Subsequent minority Prime Ministers **François Bayrou** (February 2025) and **Sébastien Lecornu** (January 2026) again invoked Article 49.3 to pass annual budgets.

Secularism and courts also contrast sharply. French ***laïcité*** (**1905 Separation Law**; **Article 1 of the 1958 Constitution**) enforces strict public-sphere exclusion of religion, banning conspicuous religious symbols in state schools (**2004**) and public full-face veils (**2010**). Indian secularism—termed **"principled distance"** by **Rajeev Bhargava** and basic structure under ***S.R. Bommai* (1994)**—accommodates public religious expression, permits non-discriminatory aid to minority institutions (Article 30), and allows reformist state intervention (Articles 17, 25(2)). Judicially, France separates **ordinary courts** (*Cour de cassation*) from **administrative courts** (***Conseil d'État***, applying *droit administratif*) and vests constitutional review in the ***Conseil Constitutionnel***—historically limited to *a priori* pre-enactment scrutiny until the **2008 reform** (effective **March 2010**) added *a posteriori* referrals (**QPC**, Article 61-1). India integrates all three functions inside a single unified judiciary.

## 5. India, Canada and Australia: Commonwealth federations compared

**Canada's Constitution Act, 1867** (formerly BNA Act) served as India's primary federal blueprint, supplying the strong-Centre **"Union"** model, **central residuary powers** (Canada's **Section 91** "Peace, Order, and good Government" → India's Article 248), **centrally appointed provincial heads** (Lieutenant Governors → Governors), and **Supreme Court advisory references** (→ Article 143). Yet practice diverged: Canadian federal **disallowance and reservation** of provincial bills (Sections 55–57, 90) have fallen into disuse (the last disallowance was in 1943), whereas India's Articles 200–201 remain active flashpoints; Canada's **Senate** is **appointed** (to age 75) unlike India's indirectly elected Rajya Sabha; and Canada's **Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982)** includes **Section 33—the "Notwithstanding Clause"**—allowing legislatures to expressly override certain rights (Section 2, Sections 7–15) for **renewable five-year periods** (excluding democratic, mobility and language rights), an override India rejects.

**Australia's Constitution Act 1900** (effective 1901) created a coming-together federation with **state residuary powers** and equal Senate seats (**12 per state**). India adapted three key tools from it: the **Concurrent List and federal paramountcy** (Sections 51 and 109 → List III and Article 254); **freedom of interstate trade** (Section 92 → Part XIII, Articles 301–307, with explicit Indian exceptions); and the **joint sitting** (Section 57 → Article 108)—with a classic Prelims trap: Australia requires a **double dissolution of both Houses and a fresh general election** before a joint sitting can meet (achieved once, in 1974), whereas India convenes an Article 108 joint sitting directly on a six-month deadlock. Unlike India, Australia has **no national constitutional Bill of Rights** (relying on common law, implied political-communication freedom, and state statutory charters), and **Section 128** requires a **double-majority referendum** (national majority **and** majority in at least **4 of 6 states**)—passing only **8 of 45** proposals since 1901, underscored by the defeat of the **Indigenous Voice referendum** on **14 October 2023** (39.9 per cent Yes; zero states).

| Dimension | India | Canada | Australia |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Federal bias & Residuary** | Strong Centre; **Union** (Art. 248) | Strong Centre text; **Federal** (s. 91) | Strong States; **States** (s. 107) |
| **Upper House** | Indirectly elected **Rajya Sabha** | **Appointed Senate** | Elected **Senate** (12/state) |
| **Rights framework** | Justiciable **Part III** | **1982 Charter** + **s. 33 override** (5-yr) | **No national constitutional BoR** |
| **Deadlock resolution** | Direct joint sitting (**Art. 108**) | No joint sitting | **Double dissolution + election**, then joint sitting (**s. 57**) |
| **Amendment** | **Art. 368** (Parl. + half states) | **1982 7/50 formula** | **s. 128 double-majority referendum** (**8/45** passed) |

## 6. High-yield global benchmarks: Germany, Ireland, Japan, South Africa and Switzerland

Five further constitutions furnish vital Prelims pegs and Mains reform analogies. Post-war **Germany's Basic Law (*Grundgesetz*, 1949)** is India's closest doctrinal twin: **Article 79(3)**—the **Eternity Clause (*Ewigkeitsklausel*)**—prohibits amendments undermining federalism, human dignity (Article 1), or the democratic, federal, social rule-of-law state (Article 20), directly paralleling India's Basic Structure doctrine (shaped by the German jurist **Dieter Conrad's** 1965 lecture, which was cited in *Kesavananda Bharati*). **Article 67** mandates a **constructive vote of no-confidence**—the *Bundestag* removes a Chancellor only by simultaneously electing a successor—endorsed for India by the **NCRWC (2002)**. Its ***Bundesrat*** seats delegated *Land* government ministers casting weighted **bloc votes** (3–6 per *Land*). (Historically, India's Emergency rights suspension echoed Weimar Germany, though the 44th Amendment insulated Articles 20–21.)

Four other benchmarks sharpen key contrasts. **Ireland's Article 45 (1937)** supplied India's non-justiciable **Part IV DPSPs**, alongside Presidential election mechanics and upper-House nominations. **Japan's Article 31 (1947)** supplied Article 21's exact phrase—*"procedure established by law"*—into which both supreme courts later read natural justice. **South Africa** offers a dual lesson: its **1909 Act** shaped India's **Article 368** special-majority amendment and indirect Rajya Sabha election, while its **1996 Constitution** pioneers **directly justiciable socio-economic rights** (housing, Section 26; health care, food, water and social security, Section 27), enforced via deferential **"reasonableness review"** (*Grootboom*, 2000)—contrasting with India's DPSP–Article 21 synthesis. The **Soviet Constitution** gave India the idea of **Fundamental Duties** and the Preamble's ideal of social, economic and political **justice**. Finally, **Switzerland (1999)** anchors **direct democracy**—**mandatory constitutional referenda**, **optional referenda** (**50,000 signatures** in 100 days) against federal laws, and **popular initiatives** (**100,000 signatures** in 18 months) proposing constitutional amendments—paired with a **plural executive**: a seven-member collegial **Federal Council** with an annually rotating Presidency (*primus inter pares*).

| Country | Signature feature | Comparative relevance to India |
|---|---|---|
| **Germany (1949)** | **Art. 79(3)** Eternity Clause; **Art. 67** constructive no-confidence; *Bundesrat* | Textual twin of Basic Structure; NCRWC (2002) stability reform |
| **Ireland (1937)** | **Art. 45** Directive Principles of Social Policy | Direct blueprint for **Part IV DPSPs** |
| **Japan (1947)** | **Art. 31** "procedure established by law" | Origin of **Article 21** phrasing pre-*Maneka Gandhi* |
| **South Africa (1909/1996)** | Art. 368 model (1909); **justiciable socio-economic rights** (1996, *Grootboom*) | Benchmarks welfare enforcement vs India's DPSP–Art. 21 path |
| **Switzerland (1999)** | **Referenda** (50k optional / 100k initiative); 7-member **Federal Council** | Direct democracy & collegial executive (rejected by Assembly) |

> **Mains angle:** Avoid listing borrowings mechanically. Frame GS-2 answers functionally: (1) **Ambedkar's conscious adaptation** (4 Nov 1948); (2) **Institutional contrasts** (*laïcité* vs principled distance, coming-together vs holding-together federalism, parliamentary vs constitutional supremacy); and (3) **Targeted reforms**—**UK Speaker neutrality** for impartial Tenth Schedule rulings, **German constructive no-confidence** for hung Houses, and **South African reasonableness review** for welfare accountability—aligned with India's Basic Structure and pluralism.

- **Conscious adaptation:** On **4 Nov 1948**, Ambedkar defended studying ~60 constitutions—originality lies in **variations removing known faults** for India.
- **UK vs India supremacy:** Uncodified **Parliamentary Sovereignty** (courts cannot void primary Acts; **HRA 1998 s. 4** yields only a **declaration of incompatibility**) vs India's **Art. 13** and **Basic Structure**.
- **UK Supreme Court:** Created by **Constitutional Reform Act 2005** (operational **Oct 2009**); polices executive prerogative (*Miller*, 2017; *Miller II*, 2019) without voiding primary Acts.
- **Speaker neutrality:** **UK Speaker resigns party** and runs conventionally unopposed; Indian Speakers keep party ties while judging **Tenth Schedule** cases (*Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992).
- **House of Lords Act 2026:** Royal Assent **18 Mar 2026** (effective **29 Apr 2026**), ending remaining hereditary peers' seats; Lords now holds only **life peers and 26 Lords Spiritual**.
- **US vs India federalism:** US **"coming-together"** (*Texas v. White*, 1869: *indestructible Union of indestructible States*; **2 Senators/state**, dual citizenship/courts, **10th Amdt** state residuary) vs India's **"holding-together"** (*destructible States* under **Art. 3**, single citizenship/courts, **Art. 248** Union residuary).
- **Rights & Due Process:** US **Bill of Rights (1791)** omits textual limits ("police power") and guarantees **"due process"** (5th/14th Amdts); India codifies **"reasonable restrictions"** and took **"procedure established by law"** from **Japan (Art. 31)**, converging via ***Maneka Gandhi* (1978)**.
- **Amendment rigidity:** US **Article V** (2/3 Congress + **3/4 states**) = **27 amendments**; India's **Art. 368** = **106**.
- **SC judges & impeachment (US vs India):** US justices serve for **life** (Senate-confirmed); Indian judges come via **Collegium** (retire at **65**). US **House** indicts (simple majority), **Senate** convicts (2/3 present); India's **Art. 61** lets **either House** initiate, requires **2/3 total membership** in both Houses (**nominated MPs vote**, MLAs do **not**).
- **France Fifth Republic (1958):** **Semi-presidentialism** (5-year elected President + Assembly-accountable PM); rival majorities create ***cohabitation***.
- **France Article 49.3 (2024–26):** Adopts bills without vote unless censured (post-2008: budgets + **1 bill/session**). PM **Michel Barnier** fell **4 Dec 2024** to the first 49.3-provoked censure; PMs Bayrou (2025) and Lecornu (Jan 2026) again used 49.3 on budgets.
- ***Laïcité* vs Principled Distance:** French *laïcité* (**1905 Law**, **Art. 1**) bans conspicuous religious symbols in state schools (2004) and face veils (2010); India's **principled distance** (Rajeev Bhargava) accommodates public religion while allowing social reform (Arts. 17, 25(2), 30).
- **French Dual Judiciary:** Ordinary (*Cour de cassation*) vs administrative (***Conseil d'État***) courts, plus ***Conseil Constitutionnel*** (*a priori* + *a posteriori* **QPC** since 2010).
- **Canada (1867 & 1982):** Strong Centre, federal residuary (**s. 91**), appointed Governors, SC advisory references. Divergence: **s. 33 Notwithstanding Clause** allows **5-year renewable** legislative overrides of Charter rights.
- **Australia (1900):** **Concurrent List** (ss. 51, 109), **Part XIII trade** (s. 92), **joint sitting** (s. 57—requires **double dissolution + election** first, unlike India's direct **Art. 108**); no national Bill of Rights; **s. 128 double-majority referenda** (**8/45** passed; **14 Oct 2023 Voice** defeated).
- **Germany (1949):** **Art. 79(3) Eternity Clause** (Basic Structure twin), **Art. 67 constructive no-confidence** (NCRWC 2002), and *Bundesrat* (*Land* ministers casting bloc votes).
- **Ireland (1937), Japan (1947) & USSR:** Ireland's **Art. 45** inspired **DPSPs**; Japan's **Art. 31** supplied Art. 21's **"procedure established by law"**; the Soviet Constitution gave **Fundamental Duties** and the Preamble's ideal of justice.
- **Pardon, India vs US:** US President acts alone, federal offences only, not impeachment, and may pardon before trial (**pre-emptive pardon**: Nixon 1974, Biden's of January 2025); India's President acts on ministerial advice (*Maru Ram*), with limited judicial review (*Epuru Sudhakar*), after conviction.
- **South Africa (1909/1996) & Switzerland (1999):** South Africa shaped Art. 368 (1909) and pioneers **justiciable socio-economic rights** (1996, *Grootboom*); Switzerland features **optional referenda** (50k), **popular initiatives** (100k) and a 7-member **Federal Council**.
