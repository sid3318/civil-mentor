## 1. Roots of British Constitutional Control (1600–1773)

The Constitution of India did not emerge in a historical vacuum on 26 January 1950. Nearly a century and three-quarters of British parliamentary enactments—spanning the `Regulating Act of 1773` to the `Indian Independence Act of 1947`—shaped the administrative framework, legislative institutions, judicial hierarchy, federal distribution of powers, and bureaucratic conventions of modern India. Studying this evolution bridges **General Studies Paper I (Modern Indian History)** and **General Studies Paper II (Indian Polity and Constitution)**, while supplying some of the highest-frequency factual questions in the UPSC Prelims.

Constitutional development under British rule falls into two distinct eras. The first, **Company Rule (1773–1858)**, saw the British Parliament gradually assert political sovereignty over the English East India Company, strip away its commercial monopoly, centralise administration under a single Governor-General, and introduce nascent legislative separation and merit-based civil services. The second, **Crown Rule (1858–1947)**, began after the Revolt of 1857 and witnessed the slow, grudging expansion of Indian representation, legislative devolution to provinces, communalisation of the electorate, partial and then full provincial responsibility, and finally the transfer of sovereignty amid Partition. Before Parliament intervened in 1773, however, a merchant corporation had to transform itself into a territorial sovereign.

### Charter of 1600 & the East India Company's Trading Monopoly

British presence in India began purely as a commercial venture. On `31 December 1600`, **Queen Elizabeth I** granted a Royal Charter incorporating the *Governor and Company of Merchants of London Trading into the East Indies*—popularly known as the **East India Company (EIC)**. The Charter conferred upon the Company an exclusive monopoly of trade with all lands lying east of the Cape of Good Hope and west of the Straits of Magellan for an initial period of fifteen years, a privilege later extended indefinitely by King James I in `1609` (subject to three years' notice of revocation).

Internally, the Company was structured as a joint-stock corporation governed from London by a **Court of Proprietors** (its shareholders), which annually elected a 24-member executive body known as the **Court of Directors** (headed by a Governor and Deputy Governor) to manage day-to-day commercial operations. Though created for profit rather than empire, the Charter of 1600 planted the earliest legal seeds of colonial governance by empowering the Company to frame reasonable "bye-laws, constitutions, orders, and ordinances" for the internal discipline of its servants, provided they were not repugnant to the laws of England.

During the seventeenth century, the Company secured royal *farmans* from Mughal emperors (starting with Jahangir's permission to establish a permanent factory at Surat in `1613`, followed by Sir Thomas Roe's embassy) and fortified three coastal enclaves that matured into the three autonomous **Presidencies**: **Madras** (Fort St. George, founded `1639`), **Bombay** (ceded by Portugal to Charles II as dowry in `1661` and leased to the Company in `1668` for an annual rent of £10), and **Calcutta** (Fort William, founded `1690–1698` across Sutanuti, Kalikata, and Gobindapur). Each Presidency was administered independently by a President (later Governor) and Council answerable directly to the Court of Directors in London, with no coordinating authority in India.

A notable early judicial milestone arrived with the **Charter of 1726** (issued by George I), which established **Mayor's Courts** in Madras, Bombay, and Calcutta as Crown courts applying English common law, and formally authorised the respective Governors-in-Council to make bye-laws and rules for their settlements. Yet until the mid-eighteenth century, the Company remained essentially a coastal trading enterprise operating under the nominal suzerainty of the Mughal Empire.

### Battle of Plassey (1757), Battle of Buxar (1764) & Grant of Diwani (1765)

The collapse of central Mughal authority after Aurangzeb's death (`1707`) and Anglo-French imperial rivalry in the Carnatic transformed the East India Company from a commercial body into a militarised political actor. Two decisive military victories in Bengal completed this metamorphosis within a single decade.

First, at the **Battle of Plassey (`23 June 1757`)**, Company forces under **Robert Clive** defeated Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah through battlefield intrigue and conspiracy with Mir Jafar, the Nawab's commander-in-chief. Plassey did not immediately confer formal sovereignty, but it made the Company the de facto kingmaker of India's richest province, enabling it to extract vast indemnities, private fortunes, and the zamindari of the 24 Parganas while installing pliable Nawabs.

Second, at the **Battle of Buxar (`22 October 1764`)**, Company troops led by **Major Hector Munro** decisively defeated the combined armies of **Mir Qasim** (the deposed Nawab of Bengal), **Shuja-ud-Daulah** (Nawab of Awadh), and the titular Mughal Emperor **Shah Alam II**. Unlike Plassey, Buxar was a straight military triumph over the major powers of northern India, leaving the British unchallenged masters of the lower Gangetic basin.

The constitutional consequence of Buxar was sealed through the **Treaty of Allahabad (`August 1765`)**, negotiated by Robert Clive:

- **Grant of Diwani (`12 August 1765`):** Emperor Shah Alam II granted the East India Company the **Diwani**—the right to collect land revenue and administer civil justice—over the provinces of **Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa** in perpetuity. In return, the Company agreed to pay the Emperor an annual tribute of **₹26 lakh** and ceded to him the districts of Kora and Allahabad (taken from Awadh).
- **Control over Nizamat:** Through a parallel agreement with the minor Nawab of Bengal (Najm-ud-Daulah), the Company secured control over **Nizamat** functions—military defence, police, and criminal justice—by paying the Nawab an annual allowance of **₹53 lakh** (later reduced) for household and administrative upkeep, while retaining the right to nominate the Deputy Subahdar (Deputy Nazim).
- **Career as a Territorial Power:** With the grant of Diwani in `1765`, the Company—hitherto carrying purely trading functions—formally commenced its career as a **territorial and revenue-collecting power** in India.

### Dual Government in Bengal (1765–1772) & Financial Crisis Forcing Parliamentary Intervention

Unwilling to assume the visible burden of direct governance—which might alarm rival European powers, provoke Indian resistance, or invite immediate intervention from the British Parliament—Robert Clive devised the **Dual System of Government** (*Dyarchy of Bengal*) that operated from `1765` to `1772`. Under this arrangement, the Company exercised both Diwani (directly by imperial grant) and Nizamat (indirectly through its right to nominate the Deputy Subahdar), yet chose to run actual collection and justice through Indian deputies—**Mohammad Reza Khan** in Bengal and **Raja Shitab Rai** in Bihar.

The Dual System institutionalized a catastrophic divorce between authority and accountability: the Company enjoyed **power without responsibility**, while the Nawab bore **responsibility without power** or financial resources. Company servants systematically abused trade exemptions (*dastaks*) for private inland commerce, ruined indigenous weavers and merchants, and farmed out land revenue to the highest bidders. Administration collapsed, law and order disintegrated, and the crisis peaked in the horrific **Great Bengal Famine of 1770**, in which an estimated **one-third of Bengal's population** (roughly ten million people) perished while Company officials rigorously enforced land-revenue collection and even raised assessments the following year.

When **Warren Hastings** arrived as Governor of Bengal in `1772`, he abolished the Dual Government, dismissed the Indian deputy diwans, transferred the treasury from Murshidabad to Calcutta, and placed revenue collection directly under British Collectors. By then, however, the Company's finances in London were in acute distress.

A glaring paradox outraged British public and parliamentary opinion. On one hand, returning Company servants—derisively called **"nabobs"**—flashed immense private fortunes in Britain, purchasing landed estates and parliamentary boroughs. On the other hand, the Company itself hovered on the brink of insolvency due to administrative corruption, costly wars (especially the First Anglo-Mysore War against Hyder Ali), unsold tea stockpiles in London warehouses, and an annual tribute of **£400,000** imposed by the British Parliament since `1767` in exchange for letting the Company keep its territorial revenues.

In `1772`, the Court of Directors was forced to apply to the Bank of England and Prime Minister **Lord North's** government for an emergency rescue loan (ultimately granted at **£1.4 million** at 4% interest). Parliament seized the moment. A **Secret Committee** and a **Select Committee** of the House of Commons investigated Indian affairs and exposed systemic maladministration, bribery, and constitutional ambiguity: could a private trading corporation exercise sovereign political authority over millions of subjects independent of the Crown and Parliament? The answer was an emphatic no. Parliament attached stringent legislative strings to the bailout, enacting the **Regulating Act of 1773**—the opening chapter of British India's constitutional history.

```keyfacts
# From Traders to Territorial Rulers (1600–1773)
31 Dec 1600 | Royal Charter of Elizabeth I | Incorporated the EIC with an exclusive eastern trade monopoly and power to make internal bye-laws
1757 & 1764 | Plassey and Buxar | Military triumphs converting a coastal trading corporation into the dominant power of northern India
12 Aug 1765 | Grant of Diwani | Treaty of Allahabad conferred revenue and civil justice rights over Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa
1765–1772 | Dual Government & Famine | Clive's split of power and responsibility ended in the 1770 famine and EIC's £1.4M bailout plea
```

> [!mains]
> **Framing Colonial Constitutionalism:** Avoid presenting British constitutional statutes as a benevolent, linear gift of democracy. In both GS-1 and GS-2 answers, emphasise their **reactive and pragmatic character**: the `1773` Act stemmed from a financial bailout; `1813` and `1833` reflected British industrial free-trade lobbying; `1858` and `1861` followed the shock of 1857; and every step from `1892` to `1947` was extracted by escalating Indian nationalist mobilisation, always hedged with imperial safeguards.

## 2. Company Rule — Early Parliamentary Statutes (1773–1813)

The forty years between `1773` and `1813` established the core constitutional relationship between Westminster, the East India Company, and the emerging British Indian state. Across five interconnected statutes—the Regulating Act (`1773`), the Amending Act (`1781`), Pitt's India Act (`1784`), the Act of `1786`, and the Charter Acts of `1793` and `1813`—Parliament asserted political supervision over Company territories, drew formal boundaries between the executive and the judiciary, strengthened the Governor-General against his own council and subordinate presidencies, and finally cracked open the Company's commercial monopoly.

```timeline
# Company Rule Constitutional Statutes (1773–1853)
1773 | Regulating Act | First British parliamentary regulation of EIC; created Governor-General of Bengal (Warren Hastings), 4-member Council & Calcutta Supreme Court (1774).
1781 | Amending Act (Act of Settlement) | Exempted Governor-General, Council & revenue matters from Supreme Court; mandated personal laws for Hindus and Muslims.
1784 | Pitt's India Act | Introduced Double Government (Board of Control vs Court of Directors); termed EIC territories 'British possessions in India'.
1786 | Act of 1786 | Granted Lord Cornwallis power to override his Council in special cases and serve concurrently as Commander-in-Chief.
1793 | Charter Act of 1793 | Extended override power to future Governors-General & Governors; renewed monopoly 20 years; Board of Control paid from Indian revenues.
1813 | Charter Act of 1813 | Abolished EIC Indian trade monopoly except tea & China; asserted Crown sovereignty; allowed missionaries; ₹1 lakh annual education grant.
1833 | Charter Act of 1833 | Peak centralisation: Governor-General of India (William Bentinck); ended EIC commercial role; 4th Law Member (Macaulay) & Section 87 merit clause.
1853 | Charter Act of 1853 | Separated legislative & executive wings (6 legislative councillors, 4 provincial); open ICS competition (Macaulay Committee 1854); indefinite Company trust.
```

### Regulating Act of 1773

The **Regulating Act of 1773** holds immense constitutional importance for three foundational reasons: first, it was the **first step taken by the British Government to control and regulate the affairs of the East India Company in India**; second, it **recognised, for the first time, the political and administrative functions of the Company**; and third, it **laid the foundations of central administration in India**.

#### Key Provisions of the 1773 Act

- **Governor-General of Bengal & Executive Council:** The Act designated the Governor of Bengal as the **Governor-General of Bengal** and created an **Executive Council of four members** to assist him. **`Lord Warren Hastings`** became the first Governor-General of Bengal, with **Philip Francis, John Clavering, George Monson, and Richard Barwell** named directly in the statute as the first four councillors for a five-year term. Decisions in the Council were to be taken by majority vote, with the Governor-General possessing only a **casting vote** in the event of a tie (and notably **no veto power**).
- **Subordination of Bombay and Madras Presidencies:** It made the Governors of the **Bombay** and **Madras** Presidencies subordinate to the Governor-General of Bengal in matters of war, peace, and diplomatic treaties with Indian powers—except in cases of imminent necessity or where they had received direct orders from London. Previously, all three presidencies were completely independent of one another.
- **Establishment of Supreme Court at Calcutta (`1774`):** The Act provided for the establishment of a **Supreme Court at Calcutta (Fort William)**, actually constituted by Royal Charter in **`1774`**, comprising **one Chief Justice** (**`Sir Elijah Impey`**) and **three other (puisne) judges** (Robert Chambers, Stephen Caesar Lemaistre, and John Hyde). The Court was vested with original and appellate civil, criminal, admiralty, and ecclesiastical jurisdiction over all British subjects and Company employees residing in Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa. Furthermore, legislative rules and ordinances framed by the Governor-General-in-Council were valid only after being **registered and published in the Supreme Court**.
- **Curbs on Private Trade and Corruption:** To eradicate the notorious "nabob" culture, the Act strictly **prohibited Company servants from engaging in any private trade** or **accepting presents, gifts, or bribes from the "natives"** (Indians), while simultaneously raising official salaries (the Governor-General received £25,000 per annum).
- **Parliamentary Oversight via Court of Directors:** It strengthened British Government control over the Company by requiring the **Court of Directors** (the Company's governing body in London) to **report on its revenue, civil, and military affairs in India** to the British Treasury and a Secretary of State. Inside London, the Act also reformed corporate governance by raising the shareholding threshold for voting in the Court of Proprietors from £500 to £1,000 and changing the tenure of Directors from one year to **four years**, with one-fourth retiring annually to ensure continuity.

#### Structural Defects & Working Frictions

Because the 1773 Act was Parliament's first experimental essay in colonial law-making, its ambiguities quickly triggered severe institutional deadlock in Calcutta:

1. **Executive Paralysis in the Council:** Lacking a veto, Warren Hastings was routinely outvoted 3–2 by the hostile triad of Francis, Clavering, and Monson, crippling administration until the deaths of Monson (`1776`) and Clavering (`1777`) restored Hastings's working majority through Barwell and his casting vote.
2. **Incomplete Control over Presidencies:** The "urgent necessity" loophole allowed Bombay and Madras to plunge the Company into unauthorised wars—most notably the First Anglo-Maratha War and hostilities in the Carnatic—forcing Bengal to bail them out financially and militarily.
3. **Executive–Judiciary Collision:** The statute failed to define the precise jurisdiction of the Supreme Court vis-à-vis the Governor-General-in-Council, revenue collectors, and the Company's own provincial courts (*Sadar Diwani Adalat* and *Sadar Nizamat Adalat*), nor did it clarify which substantive law (English common law or Indian personal law) governed Indian inhabitants. Three sensational cases exposed the crisis:
   - **Raja Nandakumar Case (`1775`):** An influential Bengali Brahmin who had accused Warren Hastings of bribery was tried by the Supreme Court for forgery under an English statute of 1728 (under which forgery was a capital felony, unlike Indian law) and executed—triggering widespread outrage over perceived judicial collusion and extraterritorial English law.
   - **Patna Case (`1777–1779`):** The Supreme Court awarded heavy damages against Indian law officers (*Qazi* and *Muftis*) of the Patna Provincial Council for executing a judicial decree in good faith, paralysing local Company courts.
   - **Cossijurah Case (`1779–1780`):** When the Supreme Court issued a writ against the Raja of Cossijurah (a zamindar who was neither a British subject nor a Company servant), the Governor-General-in-Council openly defied the Court and dispatched Company sepoys to arrest the Court's sheriff and bailiffs—bringing Bengal to the brink of armed civil conflict between executive and judiciary.

### Amending Act of 1781 (Act of Settlement)

To rectify the acute defects and institutional collision produced by the Regulating Act of 1773—following inquiries by the **Touchet Committee** in Parliament—the British Parliament passed the **Amending Act of 1781**, popularly known as the **Act of Settlement** (also called the Declaratory Act of 1781). Rather than abolishing the Supreme Court, the statute decisively subordinated judicial interference to administrative stability and drew clear jurisdictional lines:

- **Exemption of Governor-General, Council & Officials:** It **exempted the Governor-General and the Council** from the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court for all acts done by them in their **official capacity**. Similarly, it **exempted the servants of the Company** from the Supreme Court's jurisdiction for their official actions.
- **Exclusion of Revenue Matters:** It **excluded all revenue matters** and any matters arising in the collection of land revenue from the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court—removing the single largest flashpoint between Company collectors and Calcutta judges.
- **Territorial Scope & Recognition of Personal Laws:** It clarified that the Supreme Court was to exercise jurisdiction over **all inhabitants of Calcutta**. Crucially, it directed the Court to **administer the personal law of the defendants**—that is, **Hindus were to be tried according to Hindu law** and **Muslims according to Mohammedan law**—in disputes concerning inheritance, succession, and contracts. (Where parties belonged to different communities, the law of the defendant prevailed.)
- **Appellate Separation from Provincial Courts:** It laid down that **appeals from the Provincial Courts** (*Mofussil* courts) could be taken to the **Governor-General-in-Council** (sitting as *Sadar Diwani Adalat*) and **NOT to the Supreme Court**, formally recognising the Company's provincial judiciary alongside the Crown's Supreme Court.
- **Independent Rule-Making Power:** It empowered the **Governor-General-in-Council to frame Regulations for the Provincial Courts and Councils** without needing prior registration or approval by the Supreme Court.

> [!prelims]
> **1773 vs 1781 Classic UPSC Traps:**
> 1. The Supreme Court at Calcutta was *provided for* by the `Regulating Act 1773`, but actually *established* in **`1774`** (Sir Elijah Impey + 3 judges).
> 2. The `Act of Settlement 1781` did **NOT** abolish the Supreme Court; it restricted its jurisdiction to Calcutta, exempted official acts and revenue collection, recognised **Hindu and Muslim personal laws**, and routed provincial court appeals to the **Governor-General-in-Council**.
> 3. The power to frame **Regulations** for provincial courts without Supreme Court registration came in `1781`.

### Pitt's India Act of 1784

Even after the 1781 repairs, the Regulating Act left real administrative control largely inside the Company's hands, and the American Revolutionary War heightened Westminster's determination to tighten grip over its remaining eastern empire. After the Coalition Ministry's radical **Fox's India Bill (`1783`)**—which sought to strip Company directors of patronage—was defeated in the House of Lords at King George III's instigation, the young Prime Minister **William Pitt the Younger** introduced a deft compromise enacted as **Pitt's India Act of 1784**.

#### Key Features & the System of Double Government

- **Separation of Commercial and Political Functions:** The Act formally distinguished between the **commercial** and **political** functions of the East India Company.
- **System of Double Government:** It allowed the **Court of Directors** to continue managing the Company's **commercial affairs** (and crucially preserved their patronage over civil and military appointments), but created a brand-new ministerial body in London—the **Board of Control** (officially the *Board of Commissioners for the Affairs of India*)—to manage **political affairs**. This dual supervision in London came to be known as the **System of Double Government**.
- **Composition and Authority of the Board of Control:** The Board consisted of **six Commissioners** appointed by the Crown: the **Chancellor of the Exchequer**, a **Secretary of State**, and **four Privy Councillors** (with the senior commissioner soon emerging as President of the Board, functioning essentially as Britain's minister for India). It was empowered to **supervise and direct all operations of the civil and military government or revenues of the British possessions in India**. In urgent matters of war, peace, or diplomacy, the Board could dispatch secret orders directly to India through a **three-member Secret Committee** of the Court of Directors, bypassing the full Court of Directors.
- **Restructuring the Governor-General's Council:** To prevent the Council deadlocks that had plagued Warren Hastings, the Act **reduced the Governor-General's Executive Council from four members to three**, one of whom was to be the **Commander-in-Chief** of the Crown's forces in India. With only three councillors plus his own casting vote, the Governor-General henceforth needed the backing of just one member to command a majority. Similar 3-member councils were set up in Bombay and Madras.
- **Tighter Subordination & Restraint on Wars:** Bombay and Madras were made explicitly subordinate to Bengal across all questions of war, revenue, and diplomacy. Reflecting Westminster's financial caution, the Act also declared that "to pursue schemes of conquest and extension of dominion in India are measures repugnant to the wish, the honour, and policy of this nation," prohibiting the Governor-General from declaring war or entering treaties without express sanction from the Directors or Secret Committee (a clause routinely bypassed by ambitious Governors-General such as Wellesley).
- **Accountability of Company Servants:** Company officials were required to disclose their property in Britain and India within two months of joining/returning, and a special court was established in England to try Company servants accused of extortion or corruption in India.

#### Twin Constitutional Significance of 1784

Constitutional historians highlight two decisive milestones embedded in Pitt's India Act:

1. **First Use of "British Possessions in India":** For the **first time**, the Company's territories in India were officially called the **"British possessions in India"**—a quiet yet unmistakable assertion of British territorial sovereignty.
2. **Supreme Control of the British Government:** For the **first time**, the British Government was vested with **supreme control** over the Company's affairs and its administration in India. Though the Directors retained formal patronage and the initiative in commercial matters, real political command shifted decisively to Whitehall.

```flow
# System of Double Government under Pitt's India Act (1784)
British Parliament & the Crown | Supreme sovereign authority over 'British possessions in India'
Board of Control (6 Crown Commissioners) | Directed all political, civil, military & revenue operations via Secret Committee || Court of Directors (24 Company Directors) | Managed commercial trade affairs & retained patronage of appointments
Governor-General of Bengal in Council (3 Members) | Executed imperial policy with one member being Commander-in-Chief
Subordinate Presidencies of Bombay & Madras | Strictly subordinated to Bengal in war, diplomacy, and revenue
```

### Act of 1786 & Charter Act of 1793

Two short statutes over the next decade completed the consolidation of executive authority inside British India and systematised the periodic renewal of the Company's licence.

#### Act of 1786 (Lord Cornwallis's Demands)

In `1786`, **Lord Cornwallis**—a respected aristocrat and military commander—was offered the post of Governor-General of Bengal. Determined not to suffer the council paralysis that had hamstrung Warren Hastings, Cornwallis laid down **two specific preconditions** for accepting office, both enacted by Parliament in the **Act of 1786**:

- **Power to Override the Council:** He was granted the authority to **override the decision of his Executive Council in special cases** (in matters of high importance affecting the safety, tranquillity, or interests of British possessions), provided he recorded his reasons in writing and assumed personal responsibility.
- **Concurrent Office of Commander-in-Chief:** He was permitted to hold the office of **Commander-in-Chief** simultaneously with that of Governor-General, unifying supreme civil and military command in a single person.

#### Charter Act of 1793

Enacted as the first of the regular twenty-year Charter renewals (`1793`, `1813`, `1833`, `1853`), the **Charter Act of 1793** largely consolidated the framework of 1784 and 1786 while introducing key administrative and fiscal shifts:

- **Generalisation of Overriding Power:** It **extended the overriding power** granted personally to Lord Cornwallis over his council to **all future Governors-General** and also to the **Governors of the Presidencies** of Bombay and Madras.
- **Enhanced Central Control over Presidencies:** It gave the Governor-General **more powers and control over the subordinate Governments of Bombay and Madras**; whenever the Governor-General visited either Presidency, he automatically superseded the local Governor as executive head.
- **Renewal of Trade Monopoly:** It extended the Company's **trade monopoly in India for another period of twenty years** (until 1813), while requiring the Company to pay £500,000 annually to the British Government after covering necessary expenses (a stipulation rarely met).
- **Commander-in-Chief's Council Membership:** It provided that the **Commander-in-Chief was NOT to be an ex-officio member** of the Governor-General's Council unless specifically so appointed by the Court of Directors.
- **Home Government Salaries Charged to Indian Revenues:** Crucially, it laid down that the **members of the Board of Control and their staff** were henceforth to be **paid out of Indian revenues**—an extractive fiscal arrangement (part of the "Home Charges" later criticised by Dadabhai Naoroji's Drain Theory) that persisted for 126 years until the Government of India Act of `1919` shifted the Secretary of State's salary back to the British Exchequer.
- **Additional Regulatory Clauses:** Royal approval was mandated for appointing the Governor-General, Governors, and Commander-in-Chief; senior officials were barred from leaving India without permission (departure without leave counted as resignation); and the Company was empowered to issue private "country trade" licences to individuals and employees (which accelerated private opium shipments from Bengal to China). *(Separately in Bengal that same year, Lord Cornwallis's Bengal Regulations—the **Cornwallis Code of 1793**—introduced the Permanent Settlement and separated revenue administration from judicial functions by abolishing Maal Adalats.)*

### Charter Act of 1813

By `1813`, the intellectual and economic climate of Britain had been transformed. The **Industrial Revolution** was in full swing, generating powerful manufacturing lobbies in Manchester, Liverpool, and Glasgow clamorous for unrestricted access to Indian markets. Simultaneously, **Napoleon Bonaparte's Continental System** (Berlin and Milan Decrees, `1806–1807`) had shut continental European ports to British exports, intensifying commercial pressure to dismantle the East India Company's ancient eastern monopoly. Alongside laissez-faire capitalists (inspired by Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations*), influential **Evangelicals** of the Clapham Sect (led by **William Wilberforce** and former EIC director **Charles Grant**) campaigned intensely for legal admission of Christian missionaries to India. The **Charter Act of 1813** accommodated all three forces while affirming imperial sovereignty.

#### Key Provisions of the 1813 Act

- **Partial Abolition of EIC Trade Monopoly:** It **abolished the trade monopoly of the East India Company in India**, throwing Indian trade open to all British merchants. However, it **continued the Company's monopoly over trade in tea and trade with China** for another twenty years (the lucrative China tea trade remained vital to the Company's balance sheet and British customs duties).
- **Explicit Assertion of Crown Sovereignty:** It expressly **asserted the sovereignty of the British Crown** over the Company's territories in India—declaring that the Company held its Indian territories and revenues for twenty more years "without prejudice to the undoubted sovereignty of the Crown of the United Kingdom."
- **Admission of Christian Missionaries:** It **allowed Christian missionaries** to come to India under licence for the purpose of "enlightening" the people and promoting religious and moral improvement. It also provided for an Anglican ecclesiastical establishment in India headed by a **Bishop of Calcutta** (and three Archdeacons), paid from Indian revenues.
- **First State Financial Provision for Education (`₹1 Lakh`):** It provided for the **spread of Western education** (more precisely, the statute directed that a sum of not less than **one lakh rupees — ₹1 lakh — each year** be set apart and applied to "the revival and improvement of literature and the encouragement of the learned natives of India, and for the introduction and promotion of a knowledge of the sciences among the inhabitants of the British territories in India"). Although actual disbursement stalled for a decade, this clause laid the statutory seed for modern public education in India and ignited the celebrated **Orientalist–Anglicist controversy**, finally resolved in favour of English-medium Western education by **Lord Macaulay's Minute of `1835`**.
- **Power of Local Taxation:** It **authorised the Local Governments in India to impose taxes on persons** subject to the jurisdiction of the Supreme Courts and empowered them to **punish persons for non-payment of taxes**.
- **Accounting Separation & Oversight:** It directed the Company to maintain **separate accounts for its commercial transactions and territorial revenues**, capped shareholder dividends at 10.5%, required regulations made by the Councils of Madras, Bombay, and Calcutta to be laid before the British Parliament, and expanded the jurisdiction of provincial courts over European British subjects residing in the interior.

> [!mains]
> **GS-1 Economic & Social Legacy of the 1813 Act:** The Charter Act of `1813` is a pivotal link between constitutional history and economic history. By ending EIC's Indian trade monopoly while Britain retained prohibitive tariffs against Indian calicoes and muslins, the Act accelerated **deindustrialisation**—transforming India from a premier exporter of artisanal textiles into a captive market for Lancashire machine-made goods and an exporter of raw cotton, indigo, and opium. Meanwhile, the ₹1 lakh grant initiated state involvement in education, though designed primarily to cultivate an anglicised intermediary class.

## 3. Peak of Centralisation & Rise of Legislative Functions (1833–1853)

If the period 1773–1813 laid the foundations of parliamentary regulation, the twenty years spanned by the **Charter Acts of 1833 and 1853** transformed British India into a unified imperial administrative state. In Britain, the **Whig Reform era**—marked by the Great Reform Act of `1832`, the abolition of slavery across the British Empire (`1833`), and the intellectual ascendancy of **Utilitarianism** (Jeremy Bentham, James Mill) and classical liberalism (Thomas Babington Macaulay)—demanded rationalised governance, uniform codified laws, free trade, and meritocratic administration. The `Charter Act of 1833` carried administrative and legislative centralisation to its absolute zenith, while the `Charter Act of 1853` took the first structural step toward separating law-making from executive command and inaugurating an open competitive civil service.

### Charter Act of 1833 (Saint Helena Act)

Also known as the **Saint Helena Act of 1833** (because it transferred administrative control of the Atlantic island of Saint Helena from the East India Company to the British Crown), the **Charter Act of 1833** was the **final step towards centralisation in British India**.

#### Administrative and Legislative Centralisation

- **Governor-General of India:** The Act elevated the Governor-General of Bengal to the **Governor-General of India** and vested in him **all civil and military powers**. For the first time, the statute created a **Government of India** possessing unified authority over the entire territorial area possessed by the British in India. **`Lord William Bentinck`** became the **first Governor-General of India** (having served as Governor-General of Bengal since 1828).
- **Abolition of Provincial Law-Making Powers:** It **deprived the Governors of Bombay and Madras of their legislative powers**. The Governor-General of India in Council was vested with **exclusive legislative powers for the entire British India**—covering all persons (British, foreign, or Indian) and all courts, including the Crown's Supreme Courts. Bombay and Madras retained only the right to submit draft legislative proposals to the Central Government for consideration.
- **From "Regulations" to "Acts":** Laws made under previous statutes were designated as **Regulations**, whereas laws enacted under the Charter Act of 1833 were henceforth called **Acts**—symbolising their uniform, all-India statutory authority.

#### Termination of the Company's Commercial Career

- **Purely Administrative Body:** The Act completely **ended the activities of the East India Company as a commercial body**, abolishing even its remaining monopolies in **tea** and **trade with China**. The Company was directed to wind up its commercial business with all convenient speed and became a **purely administrative body**.
- **Territories Held "in Trust":** It explicitly declared that the Company's territories in India were held by it **"in trust for His Majesty, His heirs and successors"** for another twenty years. At the same time, all restrictions on **European immigration and acquisition of property/land** in India were removed, opening the doors to European planters in tea, coffee, and indigo.

#### Addition of the Law Member, First Law Commission & Codification

- **Fourth Member (Law Member):** To assist the centralised legislative apparatus, the Act enlarged the Governor-General's Executive Council by adding a **fourth member—a Law Member**—who was NOT a Company servant and could attend and vote **only at meetings held for legislative purposes** (not executive deliberations). **`Lord Thomas Babington Macaulay`** became the first Law Member (`1834–1838`).
- **Indian Law Commission & Legal Codification:** Because British India was then governed by a chaotic patchwork of Presidency regulations, English common law, Hindu *shastras*, Muslim *sharia*, and local customs, the Act directed the Governor-General in Council to appoint an **Indian Law Commission** to inquire into courts, police, and existing laws, and to consolidate and codify them into uniform, accessible statutes. The **First Law Commission (`1834`)**, chaired by Macaulay, produced the landmark draft of the **Indian Penal Code (drafted `1837`, enacted `1860`)**, and laid the groundwork for the **Code of Civil Procedure (`1859`)** and **Code of Criminal Procedure (`1861`)**.
- **Mandate Against Slavery:** Reflecting the British Empire's `1833` Slavery Abolition Act, the Charter Act instructed the Governor-General in Council to take urgent measures to ameliorate the condition of slaves in India and work toward eventual **abolition of slavery**—culminating in the **Indian Slavery Act (Act V of `1843`)** enacted under Lord Ellenborough.
- **Sub-division of Bengal Presidency (Deferred):** The Act also proposed splitting the overgrown Bengal Presidency into two Presidencies—Fort William and Agra—a provision suspended in `1835` and substituted instead by the creation of a Lieutenant-Governorship for the **North-Western Provinces** (`1836`).

#### Section 87 (Non-Discrimination Principle) & the Failed Attempt at Open ICS Competition

- **`Section 87` — The Charter of Equal Eligibility:** In a clause of lasting constitutional resonance, `Section 87` proclaimed that **"no native of the said territories, nor any natural-born subject of His Majesty resident therein, shall, by reason only of his religion, place of birth, descent, colour, or any of them, be disabled from holding any place, office, or employment under the said Company."** Though largely nullified in practice by racial exclusion from covenanted posts, `Section 87` became the first statutory articulation of meritocratic equality in Indian public service—a direct ancestor of `Article 16(2)` of the 1950 Constitution.
- **Attempted Open Competition Negated by Directors:** The `Charter Act of 1833` **attempted to introduce a system of open competition** for the selection of civil servants (proposing that four candidates be nominated for every vacancy to compete by examination for admission to Haileybury College). **However, this provision was negated** after fierce opposition from the **Court of Directors**, who refused to surrender their lucrative monopoly of patronage. Genuine open competition had to wait another twenty years.

> [!news]
> **From Macaulay's Law Commission (`1833`) to the `2024` Criminal Law Overhaul:** The **Indian Penal Code (`1860`)**, the crowning product of the First Law Commission established under the `Charter Act of 1833`, governed Indian substantive criminal law for over 160 years. On **`1 July 2024`**, Parliament's three new criminal statutes replaced the entire colonial criminal code architecture: the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023** replaced the IPC (`1860`); the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023** replaced the CrPC (`1973`, descended from `1861`); and the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA), 2023** replaced the Indian Evidence Act (`1872`). Cite this transition in GS-2 answers on colonial legal continuity and decolonisation.

### Charter Act of 1853

The **Charter Act of 1853** was the **last in the series of Charter Acts** passed by the British Parliament between 1793 and 1853. Enacted amid rising constitutional petitions from early Indian political associations—the **British Indian Association** (Calcutta), **Bombay Association**, and **Madras Native Association** (all formed around `1851–1852`)—and administrative reports from Governor-General **Lord Dalhousie**, it proved to be a pivotal constitutional watershed in four distinct respects.

#### Separation of Legislative and Executive Functions

- **Birth of a Separate Central Legislature:** For the **first time**, the Act **separated the legislative and executive functions** of the Governor-General's Council.
- **Six Legislative Councillors & the "Mini-Parliament":** It added **six new members**, called **legislative councillors**, to the Council, establishing a separate **Governor-General's Legislative Council** that soon came to be known as the **Indian (Central) Legislative Council**. Simultaneously, the **Law Member** (created in 1833) was elevated to a **full ordinary member** of the Executive Council with full voting rights in executive matters as well. Together, the expanded legislative body numbered **12 members**: the Governor-General, the Commander-in-Chief, the 4 ordinary Executive Councillors, the Chief Justice of the Calcutta Supreme Court, one puisne judge of the Calcutta Supreme Court, and 4 provincial representatives.
- **Parliamentary Procedures:** This legislative wing functioned like a **"mini-Parliament"**, consciously adopting the same procedures as the British House of Commons—public sittings, oral debates, referral of bills to Select Committees, questioning of the executive, and scrutiny of official policy. Thus, **legislation, for the first time, was treated as a special function of government**, requiring special machinery and special process. However, no bill became law without the **assent of the Governor-General**, who retained an absolute veto.
- **First Introduction of Local Representation:** The Act introduced, **for the first time**, **local representation** in the Indian (Central) Legislative Council. Out of the six new legislative members, **four members** were appointed by the local (provincial) governments of **Madras, Bombay, Bengal, and Agra** (each requiring at least ten years of Company service—meaning they were **provincial British civil servants**, not yet Indians).

#### Open Competition for Civil Services & the Macaulay Committee (1854)

- **Abolition of Directors' Patronage:** Realising what 1833 had failed to achieve, the `Charter Act of 1853` formally **introduced an open competition system** of selection and recruitment of civil servants, stripping the Court of Directors of their centuries-old patronage over covenanted appointments.
- **Eligibility of Indians & Macaulay Committee (`1854`):** The **covenanted civil service** was legally thrown open to Indians as well. Accordingly, the **Macaulay Committee** (the *Committee on the Indian Civil Service*) was appointed in **`1854`** under Thomas Babington Macaulay to design the competitive framework. Its report recommended selecting candidates aged 18–23 through rigorous written examinations in liberal arts and sciences conducted in **London**, emphasising general intellectual calibre over narrow vocational cramming (closing Haileybury College in 1858).
- **Practical Barriers:** Despite legal eligibility, holding the examination exclusively in London, testing European classics (Greek and Latin), and progressively lowering the maximum age limit (from 23 to 22 in `1860`, 21 in `1866`, and 19 in `1878` under Lord Lytton) severely restricted Indian entry. **`Satyendranath Tagore`** (elder brother of Rabindranath Tagore) became the **first Indian to qualify for the Indian Civil Service** in **`1863`** (joining service in 1864), followed by Surendranath Banerjea, Romesh Chunder Dutt, and Behari Lal Gupta in 1869.

#### Indefinite Extension of Company Rule & Administrative Reforms

- **No Fixed Term — Signal of Impending Termination:** Unlike all earlier Charter Acts (`1793`, `1813`, `1833`), which renewed Company rule for fixed 20-year periods, the `Charter Act of 1853` extended the Company's rule and allowed it to retain possession of Indian territories on trust for the Crown **WITHOUT specifying any particular period**. This was a deliberate, unmistakable signal that the Company's rule could be **terminated at any time** Parliament chose. Five years later, the Revolt of 1857 provided the occasion.
- **Separate Lieutenant-Governor for Bengal:** The Act empowered the Court of Directors to appoint a **separate Governor or Lieutenant-Governor for the Bengal Presidency** (implemented in `1854` with Sir Frederick Halliday as first Lieutenant-Governor), relieving the Governor-General of India from the day-to-day local administration of Bengal so he could focus on all-India governance.
- **Shrinking the Court of Directors:** The strength of the Court of Directors was reduced from 24 to **18 members**, of whom **6 were to be nominated by the British Crown**—further eroding independent corporate governance even before 1858.

> [!remember]
> **Three High-Frequency Prelims Traps Across the Company Statutes:**
> 1. **Three Titles, Three Acts, Three First Holders:** *Governor-General of Bengal* (`1773`, **Warren Hastings**) → *Governor-General of India* (`1833`, **Lord William Bentinck**) → *Viceroy of India* (`1858`, **Lord Canning**).
> 2. **Open ICS Competition (`1833` vs `1853`):** The `1833` Act only *attempted* open competition (blocked by the Court of Directors); the `1853` Act actually *introduced* it (operationalised by the **Macaulay Committee, 1854**).
> 3. **Local Representation (`1853`) vs Indian Representation (`1861`):** The 4 provincial members added in `1853` (Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Agra) were **British officials** representing local governments—**NO Indian** sat in the legislative council until the `Indian Councils Act of 1861`!

## 4. Crown Rule & Beginning of Representative Institutions (1858–1909)

The ninety-year epoch of **Crown Rule (`1858–1947`)** was governed by two interlocking constitutional currents that every UPSC Mains answer must track: **representation** (how Indians entered legislative councils, moving from hand-picked princes to indirect nominees and then directly elected legislators) and **responsibility** (whether the executive answered to those legislators rather than to Whitehall). Representation began modestly in `1861` and expanded through `1892` and `1909`, yet **executive responsibility** remained strictly withheld throughout this entire first half-century of Crown rule—even as British policymakers simultaneously seeded the structural poison of communal electorates.

### Revolt of 1857 & Queen's Proclamation (1 November 1858)

The great **Revolt of 1857**—variously characterised as the Sepoy Mutiny and India's First War of Independence—shattered the complacency of British imperial rule. Exposing the military fragility, economic grievances, and social alienation accumulated under a century of Company governance (compounded by Lord Dalhousie's aggressive **Doctrine of Lapse**), the uprising convinced Westminster across party lines that an immense continental empire could no longer be administered through a commercial intermediary.

Following parliamentary enactment of direct Crown takeover, **Queen Victoria's Proclamation** was ceremonially read out by Lord Canning at a grand Durbar held at **Allahabad on `1 November 1858`**. Hailed by early Indian nationalists as the **"Magna Carta of the Indian people,"** the Proclamation redefined the ideological posture of the British Raj:

- **End of Annexations & Alliance with Princes:** It renounced any desire for further territorial expansion, formally repudiated the Doctrine of Lapse, and pledged to respect the **"rights, dignity, and honour of native princes as our own"**—including their right to adopt heirs. Princely states were thus converted from annexation targets into loyal conservative "breakwaters in the storm" backing British supremacy.
- **Religious Neutrality & Social Caution:** It disclaimed any right or desire to impose British religious convictions upon Indian subjects, promising strict non-interference in Indian religious beliefs and customary worship (resulting in British withdrawal from aggressive socio-religious reform for decades).
- **Equal Protection & Non-Discrimination in Public Service:** It guaranteed equal and impartial protection of the law to all subjects and reaffirmed the `1833` promise that Indians, "of whatever race or creed, be freely and impartially admitted to offices in our service, the duties of which they may be qualified by their education, ability, and integrity duly to discharge."
- **Amnesty:** It granted a general pardon to all rebels who had not directly participated in the murder of British subjects.

### Government of India Act 1858 (Act for the Good Government of India)

Enacted in August 1858—formally titled *An Act for the Better Government of India* and popularly remembered as the **Act for the Good Government of India**—this statute formally terminated Company Rule and transferred imperial sovereignty to the British Crown.

#### Key Provisions of the 1858 Act

- **Transfer of Sovereignty to the Crown:** It **abolished the East India Company** as a governing agency (the corporate shell was formally dissolved in 1874) and transferred the powers of government, territories, and revenues directly to the **British Crown**. India was henceforth to be governed by, and in the name of, **Her Majesty**.
- **Creation of the Office of Viceroy:** It changed the designation of the Governor-General of India to that of **Viceroy of India**, making him the **direct representative of the British Crown** in India. **`Lord Canning`** thus became the **first Viceroy of India** (and the last Governor-General under the Company). In strict constitutional parlance, he remained *Governor-General* when administering British Indian provinces and *Viceroy* when representing the Crown before princely states.
- **Abolition of Double Government:** It **ended the System of Double Government** (created by Pitt's India Act of `1784`) by **abolishing both the Board of Control and the Court of Directors**.
- **Secretary of State for India:** It created a powerful new office, the **Secretary of State for India** (**`Lord Stanley`** was the first incumbent), vested with **complete authority and control over Indian administration**. Unlike the old Directors, the Secretary of State was a **member of the British Cabinet** and was ultimately **responsible to the British Parliament**. All official correspondence to and from India passed through him, and with the completion of the Indo-European telegraph cable (`1870`), London's micro-management of Calcutta intensified sharply.
- **15-Member Council of India:** It established a **15-member Council of India** in London to assist the Secretary of State. The Council was an **advisory body** (though the Secretary of State needed a majority vote of the Council on specific financial appropriations from Indian revenues and civil service rules). The **Secretary of State was the Chairman** of the Council; at least **nine** of its fifteen members were required to have served or resided in India for a minimum of ten years and not have left India more than ten years before appointment. Salaries of both the Secretary of State and his Council remained charged to **Indian revenues**.
- **Body Corporate:** It constituted the **Secretary of State-in-Council** as a **body corporate**, capable of **suing and being sued in India and in England** (this legal continuity later carried into `Article 300` of the Indian Constitution, allowing the Union and States to sue and be sued in the name of the Union of India / State).

#### Constitutional Assessment

Despite its dramatic rhetoric, the `Government of India Act 1858` was **largely confined to the improvement of the administrative machinery** by which the Indian Government was supervised and controlled in England. It did **NOT** alter in any substantial way the authoritarian system of government operating inside India: the executive remained completely unaccountable to Indian public opinion, and **no representative institution or Indian participation** was introduced in 1858.

### Indian Councils Act 1861

The shock of 1857 taught British statesmen a sobering lesson: ruling India without any channel to gauge indigenous sentiment bred dangerous isolation. Influential voices—from **Sir Bartle Frere** inside the Viceroy's Council to **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan** (in his tract *Asbab-e-Baghawat-e-Hind*, 1858)—argued that excluding Indians from the Legislative Council had prevented the government from foreseeing the discontent of 1857. Simultaneously, the Governors of Bombay and Madras chafed under the rigid centralisation of 1833, while the 1853 "mini-Parliament" had proved inconveniently assertive toward the executive. Enacted to address all three concerns, the **Indian Councils Act of 1861** is a vital landmark in India's constitutional and political history.

#### Key Provisions of the 1861 Act

- **Beginning of Representative Institutions (Association of Indians):** The Act made a **beginning of representative institutions** by **associating Indians with the law-making process**. It provided that the Viceroy should nominate some **Indians as non-official members** of his expanded legislative council (expanding the executive council for legislative purposes by **6 to 12 additional members**, at least half of whom had to be non-officials, serving a 2-year term). In **`1862`**, **`Lord Canning`** nominated **three Indians** to his legislative council: the **Raja of Benares**, the **Maharaja of Patiala**, and **Sir Dinkar Rao** (Dewan of Gwalior).
- **Beginning of Legislative Decentralisation (Devolution):** It **initiated the process of decentralisation** by **restoring legislative powers to the Bombay and Madras Presidencies**. It thereby **reversed the centralising tendency** that had begun with the Regulating Act of `1773` and reached its climax under the Charter Act of `1833`. This policy of legislative devolution gradually expanded provincial authority and culminated in the grant of almost complete internal autonomy to the provinces in `1937`.
- **Establishment of New Provincial Legislative Councils:** It also provided for the establishment of new legislative councils for **Bengal** (set up in `1862`), **North-Western Provinces** (set up in `1886`), and **Punjab** (set up in `1897`).
- **Statutory Recognition of the Portfolio System:** It empowered the Viceroy to make rules and orders for more convenient transaction of business in the council. In doing so, it gave **statutory recognition to the "Portfolio System"**, introduced earlier by **`Lord Canning` in `1859`**. Under this system, each member of the Viceroy's Executive Council was placed in charge of one or more specific departments of government and authorised to issue final orders on behalf of the council on matters relating to his department(s)—laying the direct administrative foundation of India's modern **Cabinet portfolio system**.
- **Viceroy's Emergency Ordinance Power:** It empowered the Viceroy to **issue Ordinances, without the concurrence of the legislative council, during an emergency**. The **life of such an ordinance was six months**—a direct constitutional ancestor of the President's ordinance-making power under `Article 123` (and Governor's under `Article 213`) of the Indian Constitution.
- **Executive Expansion & Deliberative Restrictions:** The Act added a **fifth ordinary member** (a jurist or financial specialist) to the Viceroy's Executive Council. Yet it deliberately clipped the wings of the "mini-Parliament" created in 1853: legislative councils were converted into strictly circumscribed law-making committees forbidden to inquire into executive administration, ask questions, or discuss the budget. Moreover, prior sanction of the Viceroy was required to introduce bills relating to public revenue or debt, religion, military affairs, or foreign relations, and both the Viceroy and the British Crown retained veto power over all bills.

### Indian Councils Act 1892

The twenty-five years after 1861 witnessed the rapid growth of modern Indian political consciousness, culminating in the founding of the **Indian National Congress (`1885`)**. From its very first session in Bombay, the Congress made the reform and expansion of legislative councils—specifically adding elected Indian members and granting budget control ("No taxation without representation")—the centrepiece of its Moderate constitutional agitation. Responding cautiously to Congress pressure and the recommendations of **Lord Dufferin's Committee (`1888`)**, Parliament enacted the **Indian Councils Act of 1892**.

#### Key Provisions of the 1892 Act

- **Expansion of Non-Official Members:** It **increased the number of additional (non-official) members** in both the Central and Provincial Legislative Councils, while carefully **maintaining an official majority** in them. In the Central Legislative Council, the number of additional members was raised from 6–12 to **10–16** (of whom not less than half—specifically up to 10—were non-officials). Provincial councils were similarly expanded (e.g., 8–20 in Madras, Bombay, and Bengal; up to 15 in North-Western Provinces and Oudh).
- **Expanded Deliberative Functions (Budget & Questions):** It increased the functions of legislative councils by giving them two significant parliamentary privileges for the first time:
  1. The power of **discussing the annual budget** (though members could neither vote upon it nor move any amendments or resolutions); and
  2. The power of **addressing questions to the executive** on matters of public interest (subject to six days' prior notice, with **no right to ask supplementary questions** and with the presiding officer empowered to disallow questions without assigning reasons).
- **Indirect Nomination / Election Principle:** It provided for the **nomination of some non-official members** of:
  - The **Central Legislative Council** by the Viceroy on the **recommendation** of the **Provincial Legislative Councils** and the **Bengal Chamber of Commerce** (5 seats: 4 recommended by non-official members of Bengal, Bombay, Madras, and NWP provincial councils + 1 by the Bengal Chamber of Commerce); and
  - The **Provincial Legislative Councils** by the Governors on the **recommendation** of certain bodies such as **district boards, municipalities, universities, trade associations, zamindars, and chambers of commerce**.
- **Avoidance of the Word "Election":** Tellingly, the Act **made a limited and indirect provision for the use of the principle of election** in filling up some non-official seats, yet the **word "election" was deliberately NOT used** anywhere in the statute—the process was euphemistically described as **nomination made on the recommendation of certain bodies**. Still, in practice, the government almost invariably accepted these recommendations, enabling towering nationalist leaders like **Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta, Surendranath Banerjea, and Rashbehari Ghosh** to use the councils as powerful platforms for articulating Indian economic and political critiques.

### Indian Councils Act 1909 (Morley–Minto Reforms)

By the opening decade of the twentieth century, the narrow concessions of 1892 proved utterly inadequate. **Lord Curzon's Partition of Bengal (`1905`)** ignited the mass **Swadeshi and Boycott Movement**, radicalised nationalist politics (culminating in the **Surat Split of `1907`** between Moderates and Extremists), and spurred revolutionary underground activity. Simultaneously, in **October `1906`**, a delegation of aristocratic Muslim leaders—the **Simla Deputation** led by the **Aga Khan**—met Viceroy **Lord Minto**, demanding separate electorates and weighted representation ("in excess of their numerical strength") in view of their historical and political importance; two months later, the **All-India Muslim League** was founded in Dacca (`December 1906`).

To counter rising nationalist militancy, Secretary of State **`Lord John Morley`** and Viceroy **`Lord Minto`** deployed a calculated **"carrot-and-stick" (rally-the-Moderates and divide-and-rule)** policy: harsh repression against Extremists paired with constitutional concessions enacted as the **Indian Councils Act of 1909** (popularly known as the **Morley–Minto Reforms**).

#### Key Provisions of the 1909 Act

- **Considerable Enlargement of Legislative Councils:** It substantially increased the size of both the Central and Provincial Legislative Councils. The number of additional members in the **Central Legislative Council** was raised from **16 to 60** (bringing total strength, including the Viceroy and Executive Councillors, to **69**). The membership of Provincial Legislative Councils was not uniform—capped at **50** additional members in the major provinces (Bengal, Madras, Bombay, United Provinces, and Eastern Bengal & Assam) and **30** in the smaller provinces (Punjab and Burma).
- **Official Majority at Centre vs Non-Official Majority in Provinces:** It **retained an official majority in the Central Legislative Council** (37 officials vs 32 non-officials, of whom 27 were elected), but **allowed the Provincial Legislative Councils to have a non-official majority**. Crucially, however, this "non-official majority" was **NOT an elected majority**—it combined *elected* non-officials with *nominated* non-officials (princes, landlords, loyalists), enabling provincial governors to maintain an effective pro-government voting bloc.
- **Enlarged Deliberative Functions:** It broadened legislative powers at both levels: members were now allowed to **ask supplementary questions**, **move resolutions on the budget** (and divide the council on individual budget items, though still unable to vote on the budget as a whole or touch "non-votable" heads like military spending and debt), and move resolutions on matters of general public interest (which remained recommendatory, not binding on the executive).
- **First Entry of Indians into Executive Councils:** It provided, **for the first time**, for the **association of Indians with the Executive Councils** of the Viceroy and Governors. **`Satyendra Prasad Sinha`** (later Lord Sinha) became the **first Indian to join the Viceroy's Executive Council**, appointed as the **Law Member** in `1909`. (Two years earlier, in `1907`, Morley had already appointed two Indians—**K.G. Gupta** (Sir Krishna Govinda Gupta) and **Syed Hussain Bilgrami**—to the Secretary of State's Council of India in London.)
- **Communal Representation & Separate Electorates for Muslims:** The Act's most fateful provision introduced a **system of communal representation for Muslims** by accepting the concept of **"separate electorate"**. Under this arrangement, Muslim members were to be **elected ONLY by Muslim voters** (with separate reserved constituencies and lower income/property voting qualifications for Muslims than for non-Muslims, alongside weightage in excess of population share). By embedding religious identity directly into electoral architecture, the Act **"legalised communalism"**, earning **`Lord Minto`** the infamous epithet **Father of Communal Electorate**.
- **Special Class & Interest Representation:** It also provided for **separate representation** of **presidency corporations, chambers of commerce, universities, and zamindars** (landholders).
- **Complex Indirect Elections:** For most general seats, elections remained **indirect**—local taxpayers elected municipal/district boards, which elected electoral colleges, which in turn elected provincial councillors, who finally elected central legislators—creating a narrow, heavily filtered franchise (except for Muslim and landlord constituencies where limited direct voting applied).

> [!debate]
> **Were the Morley–Minto Reforms a Step Toward Parliamentary Democracy?**
> - **The Official British Intent (Anti-Parliamentary):** Lord Morley explicitly disavowed any parliamentary objective in the House of Lords (`December 1908`), declaring that if the reforms led directly or indirectly to the establishment of a parliamentary system in India, he "would have nothing at all to do with it." Real power stayed firmly with an irresponsible executive, earning the reforms the description *"benevolent despotism with consultative trimmings."*
> - **The Long-Term Nationalist Impact (Procedural Advance vs Structural Poison):** Procedurally, `1909` introduced supplementary questions, budget resolutions, and Indian membership in the Viceroy's Executive Council. Politically, however, by institutionalising **separate electorates**, it diverted communal competition into electoral channels where candidates had no incentive to appeal across religious lines—a divisive logic expanded in `1919` and `1935` that fed directly into the politics of Partition.

## 5. Responsible Government, Dyarchy & Provincial Autonomy (1919–1935)

The First World War (`1914–1918`) altered the moral and political balance of the British Empire. Over a million Indian soldiers fought overseas and India contributed immense financial and material resources to Britain's war effort, sharpening demands for self-government. Inside India, the **Home Rule League Movement** (Annie Besant and Bal Gangadhar Tilak, `1916`) and the **Lucknow Pact (`1916`)**—which reunited Congress Moderates and Extremists and forged an unprecedented constitutional alliance between Congress and the Muslim League—convinced London that the `1909` policy of denying responsible government was untenable. Between `1917` and `1935`, British constitutional policy lurched from experimental provincial **Dyarchy (`1919`)** through a turbulent decade of commissions, conferences, and pacts to the monumental blueprint of **Provincial Autonomy and All-India Federation (`1935`)**.

### Montagu Declaration (20 August 1917) & Government of India Act 1919 (Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms)

#### The August Declaration (`20 August 1917`)

On **`20 August 1917`**, Secretary of State for India **`Edwin Montagu`** made a historic policy announcement in the House of Commons—known as the **August Declaration** (or Montagu Declaration)—marking a formal paradigm shift from Lord Morley's 1908 repudiation of parliamentary rule:

> "The policy of His Majesty's Government, with which the Government of India are in complete accord, is that of the increasing association of Indians in every branch of the administration and the gradual development of self-governing institutions with a view to the progressive realisation of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire." — Edwin Montagu, House of Commons, 20 August 1917

Henceforth, demanding self-government (*Swaraj*) could no longer be branded seditious. Yet the Declaration carried a stark imperial proviso: **the British Government and the Government of India—NOT the Indian people—would be the sole judges of the time and measure of each advance**. Following Montagu's tour of India and joint deliberations with Viceroy **`Lord Chelmsford`**, the **Montagu–Chelmsford Report (`July 1918`)** was published, culminating in the **Government of India Act of 1919**, which received royal assent in December 1919 and **came into force in `1921`**.

#### Complete Clause-by-Clause Provisions of the 1919 Act

- **Demarcation of Central and Provincial Subjects (Within a Unitary State):** The Act relaxed central control over the provinces by **demarcating and separating Central subjects** (defence, foreign affairs, railways, posts & telegraphs, customs, currency, civil/criminal law) and **Provincial subjects** (local self-government, public health, education, land revenue, police, agriculture, irrigation) through the **Devolution Rules**. Central and provincial legislatures were authorised to make laws on their respective lists. Crucially, however, **the structure of government continued to be centralised and unitary**—this was NOT federalism, because provincial powers were merely devolved by the Central Government, which retained overriding legislative competence and superintendence.
- **Provincial Dyarchy (Reserved vs Transferred Subjects):** The Act's signature innovation further divided provincial subjects into two unequal halves, introducing a dual scheme of governance in the **eight (later nine, adding Burma) Governors' Provinces** known as **Dyarchy** (derived from the Greek *di-arche*, meaning double rule, conceptualised by Lionel Curtis):
  - **Transferred Subjects** (nation-building departments such as education, public health, sanitation, local self-government, agriculture, industries, excise, public works, and cooperative societies): administered by the **Governor with the aid of Ministers responsible to the Provincial Legislative Council**.
  - **Reserved Subjects** (vital levers of coercive and fiscal power such as law and order, police, administration of justice, land revenue, **finance**, irrigation, prisons, and press control): administered by the **Governor and his Executive Council WITHOUT being responsible to the Legislative Council**.
  - In cases of deadlock or breakdown of constitutional machinery, the Governor could override ministers, certify rejected bills in reserved subjects, and resume direct administration of transferred departments.
- **Bicameralism and Direct Elections at the Centre:** For the **first time**, the Act introduced **bicameralism** and **direct elections** in the country. The Indian Legislative Council was replaced by a bicameral central legislature:
  1. **Upper House — Council of State:** **60 members** (**34 elected**, 26 nominated; tenure **5 years**; predominantly male electorate restricted to very wealthy property-holders and title-holders).
  2. **Lower House — Legislative Assembly:** **145 members** (**104 elected**—52 general, 30 Muslim, 2 Sikh, 20 special constituencies—and 41 nominated; tenure **3 years**).
  - Although the **majority of members of both Houses were chosen by direct election**, the Central Executive (Viceroy and his Executive Council) remained **completely non-responsible to the Central Legislature**. The Viceroy could summon, prorogue, dissolve either House, promulgate ordinances, restore rejected grants, and **certify** any bill rejected by the Assembly as "essential for the safety, tranquillity, or interests of British India."
- **Indianisation of Viceroy's Executive Council:** It required that **three of the six members** of the Viceroy's Executive Council (**other than the Commander-in-Chief**) were to be **Indians** (typically assigned portfolios such as Law, Education, Health, or Commerce—not Home, Finance, or Defence).
- **Widened Communal Representation:** It extended the divisive principle of **communal representation** by providing **separate electorates** for **Sikhs** (in Punjab), **Indian Christians**, **Anglo-Indians**, and **Europeans** (in addition to retaining separate electorates for Muslims).
- **Limited Franchise:** It granted franchise to a restricted electorate based on **property, tax, or education** qualifications—enfranchising roughly 5 million voters for provincial councils (~2.8% of British India's population), about 1 million for the Central Legislative Assembly, and barely 17,000 for the Council of State. While the Act initially did not grant women the vote, it empowered provincial legislatures to remove the sex disqualification; **Madras (`1921`)** and **Bombay (`1921`)** became the first provinces to enfranchise women on the same property qualifications as men.
- **High Commissioner for India in London & Shift in Salary:** It created a new office of the **High Commissioner for India in London** (appointed by the Governor-General-in-Council for a 5-year term; Sir William Meyer was the first) and transferred to him several agency, trade, and procurement functions hitherto performed by the Secretary of State. Simultaneously, it rectified the longstanding 1793 grievance by directing that the **Secretary of State for India and his establishment** were henceforth to be **paid out of the British Exchequer** (not Indian revenues).
- **Establishment of a Public Service Commission:** It provided for the establishment of a Public Service Commission to insulate civil service recruitment from political patronage. Accordingly, a **Central Public Service Commission** was set up in **`1926`** (chaired by Sir Ross Barker) on the recommendation of the **Lee Commission (`1923–1924`)** on Superior Civil Services in India (which also recommended 50:50 Indianisation of ICS within 15 years).
- **Separation of Provincial and Central Budgets:** For the **first time**, it **separated provincial budgets from the Central budget** and authorised provincial legislatures to enact their own budgets—ending the old system of rigid central "doles" (though provinces initially paid fixed annual contributions to the Centre under the Meston Award).
- **Chamber of Princes (*Narendra Mandal*):** Set up in `1921` by Royal Proclamation (linked to the Montford reforms) as a 120-member consultative and advisory forum for princely rulers, presided over by the Viceroy.
- **Ten-Year Statutory Review Commission:** It provided for the appointment of a **statutory commission** to inquire into and report on the working of the Act **after ten years** of its coming into force.

#### Why Dyarchy Failed & the Political Context of 1919

Provincial Dyarchy proved a constitutional failure in practice. First, the division of subjects was administratively **irrational**: a minister held *Agriculture* while *Irrigation* and *Land Revenue* stayed reserved; another held *Industries* while *Electricity* and *Factories* were reserved. Second, **Finance was a reserved subject**, leaving Indian ministers begging the Executive Councillor for funds to implement social schemes. Third, permanent **ICS officers** owed allegiance to the Secretary of State in London rather than to Indian ministers, undermining ministerial authority. Fourth, Governors routinely bypassed joint consultation, preventing any convention of **collective ministerial responsibility**.

Politically, the reforms were overshadowed before birth by the repressive **Rowlatt Act (`March 1919`)**, the **Jallianwala Bagh massacre (`13 April 1919`)**, and the Khilafat betrayal. The Indian National Congress rejected the reforms as "inadequate, unsatisfactory, and disappointing," boycotted the 1920 elections, and launched Mahatma Gandhi's **Non-Cooperation Movement (`1920–1922`)**. Liberal Moderates led by Surendranath Banerjea broke away to work the councils, while from `1923` onward the **Swaraj Party** (C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru) contested elections to expose the hollowness of Dyarchy and central non-responsibility from inside the legislatures.

### Simon Commission (1927–1930), Nehru Report (1928), Round Table Conferences (1930–1932), Communal Award & Poona Pact (1932)

The decade between `1927` and `1934` witnessed the most intense constitutional bargaining of the British era, transforming the abstract promise of `1917` into the detailed statutory draft of `1935`.

#### Simon Commission (Indian Statutory Commission, `1927–1930`)

Although the 1919 Act scheduled its statutory review for `1929`, Britain's Conservative government under Stanley Baldwin appointed the **seven-member Indian Statutory Commission** **two years early**, in **`November 1927`**, under the chairmanship of **Sir John Simon** (fearing that an expected Labour victory in Britain's 1929 general election would appoint a commission too sympathetic to Indian nationalists). Because **all seven members were British**—excluding Indians completely from determining their own constitutional future—the Commission triggered an unprecedented, unified boycott across India (Congress, the Jinnah faction of the Muslim League, Liberals, Hindu Mahasabha, and youth organisations) under the slogan **"Simon, Go Back!"**. During an anti-Simon demonstration in Lahore (`30 October 1928`), **Lala Lajpat Rai** suffered brutal lathi blows and died shortly thereafter (`17 November 1928`).

Despite the boycott, the Commission submitted its two-volume **Report in `May 1930`**, making five pivotal recommendations:

- **Abolition of Dyarchy** in the provinces and **extension of responsible government** to all provincial departments (subject to Governor's special reserve powers for law and order and minority protection).
- **No immediate responsibility at the Centre** (retaining executive supremacy under the Governor-General).
- Establishment, as a long-term goal, of a **Federation of British India and the Princely States**, with the Central Legislature indirectly elected by provincial legislatures.
- **Continuation of communal (separate) electorates**, rejecting universal franchise while proposing expansion of the electorate to 10–15% of the population.
- **Separation of Burma** from British India, **separation of Sind** from Bombay, and creation of a legislative council in NWFP.

#### Nehru Report (`August 1928`) — India's First Indigenous Constitutional Draft

Goaded by Secretary of State **Lord Birkenhead's** taunting challenge to Indians to draft a constitution commanding broad consensus across Indian communities, an **All-Parties Conference** appointed a committee in May 1928 chaired by **`Motilal Nehru`** (members included Tej Bahadur Sapru, Subhas Chandra Bose, Sir Ali Imam, M.S. Aney, Sardar Mangal Singh, Shuaib Qureshi, G.R. Pradhan, and N.M. Joshi, with Jawaharlal Nehru serving as secretary). Submitted in **`August 1928`**, the **Nehru Report** was the **first major Indian-authored constitutional blueprint** and anticipated core pillars of the 1950 Constitution:

- **Dominion Status:** Demanded immediate **Dominion Status** on par with self-governing British Dominions (Canada, Australia) as the next step—though younger nationalists (Jawaharlal Nehru, Subhas Chandra Bose, Satyamurti) dissented and founded the *Independence for India League*, pressing for *Purna Swaraj*.
- **Rejection of Separate Electorates:** Boldly **rejected separate communal electorates**, proposing instead **joint electorates with reserved seats** for Muslims at the Centre and in provinces where they were in a minority (in strict proportion to population, with the right to contest additional seats), and no reservation in Muslim-majority Punjab and Bengal.
- **19 Fundamental Rights & Universal Adult Suffrage:** Enshrined **nineteen Fundamental Rights**—including equal rights for women, freedom of conscience and expression, right to form unions, and **universal adult suffrage**—alongside a **secular state** with complete dissociation of state from religion.
- **Responsible Parliamentary Federalism:** Proposed responsible parliamentary executives at both Centre and provinces, a bicameral Central Parliament (Senate and House of Representatives), **linguistic reorganisation of provinces**, a Supreme Court, and **residuary powers vested in the Centre**.

At the All-Parties Convention in Calcutta (`December 1928`), **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** proposed three amendments (one-third Muslim representation at the Centre, population-proportionate reservation in Punjab and Bengal until adult suffrage, and **residuary powers with the provinces**). When these were rejected, Jinnah consolidated all Muslim factions around his famous **Fourteen Points (`March 1929`)**—demanding federalism with provincial residuary powers, separate electorates, one-third central representation, and safeguards for Muslim culture. Meanwhile, following Viceroy Lord Irwin's vague Diwali Declaration (`31 October 1929`) and failure to guarantee Dominion Status, Congress adopted the historic **`Purna Swaraj` (Complete Independence) Resolution** at its **Lahore Session (`December 1929`)** under Jawaharlal Nehru, proclaiming `26 January 1930` as Independence Day and launching the **Civil Disobedience Movement (`1930`)**.

#### Three Round Table Conferences (`1930–1932`)

To deliberate on the Simon Commission's proposals and frame a new constitutional Act, the British Government convened **three Round Table Conferences** in London bringing together representatives of the British Government, British Indian parties, and Indian Princely States:

1. **First Round Table Conference (`November 1930 – January 1931`):** Held while Congress was waging Civil Disobedience and its leaders were jailed (hence **boycotted by INC**). Attended by the Muslim League, Princely States (who surprisingly endorsed an All-India Federation in principle), Liberals, Hindu Mahasabha, and **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** (who articulated the demand for separate electorates for the Depressed Classes). Produced broad consensus on an All-India Federation with provincial autonomy and safeguards at the Centre.
2. **Second Round Table Conference (`September – December 1931`):** Following the **Gandhi–Irwin Pact (`5 March 1931`)**, **Mahatma Gandhi** attended as the **sole official representative of the Indian National Congress** (Sarojini Naidu, Madan Mohan Malaviya, and G.D. Birla attended in personal/other capacities). The conference deadlocked sharply over minority representation—as Muslims, Depressed Classes, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans forged a joint "Minorities Pact" demanding separate electorates—and over Britain's refusal to grant full immediate responsible government. Gandhi returned empty-handed and resumed Civil Disobedience.
3. **Third Round Table Conference (`November – December 1932`):** Boycotted by both the **Indian National Congress** and the **British Labour Party**; attended by only 46 delegates. Notably, **`Dr B.R. Ambedkar`** and **`Tej Bahadur Sapru`** were among the select leaders who attended **all three Round Table Conferences** (a favourite UPSC Prelims anchor).

Based on these deliberations, the British Government published a **White Paper on Constitutional Reforms (`March 1933`)**, which was scrutinised by a **Joint Select Committee** of both Houses of the British Parliament chaired by **Lord Linlithgow**. Its report (`November 1934`) formed the legislative draft of the **Government of India Act of 1935**.

#### Communal Award (`16 August 1932`) & Poona Pact (`24 September 1932`)

Exploiting the Second RTC deadlock on minorities, British Prime Minister **`Ramsay MacDonald`** announced the **Communal Award** on **`16 August 1932`**. The scheme not only continued separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans (and added seats for women, labour, and Marathas in Bombay), but **extended separate electorates to the Depressed Classes (Scheduled Castes)**—declaring them a separate minority distinct from Hindus and awarding them **71 seats** in provincial legislatures alongside a "double vote" (one in special Depressed Class constituencies and one in general constituencies) for twenty years.

Viewing the permanent political severance of the Depressed Classes from Hindu society as a fatal wound to both national unity and the moral reform of caste, **Mahatma Gandhi**—then imprisoned in **Yerawada Central Jail (Poona)**—commenced a **fast unto death** on `20 September 1932`. Intense, anguished negotiations between nationalist leaders and **`Dr B.R. Ambedkar`** produced the historic **Poona Pact (`24 September 1932`)** (signed by Ambedkar for the Depressed Classes and Madan Mohan Malaviya alongside other caste-Hindu leaders on Gandhi's behalf):

- **Retention of Joint Electorate:** Separate electorates for the Depressed Classes were **abandoned**; they remained part of the **general Hindu joint electorate**.
- **Massive Expansion of Reserved Seats:** In exchange, seats **reserved for the Depressed Classes** in provincial legislatures were more than **doubled**—from **71** under the Communal Award to **147** (specifically **148** across the eight British Indian provincial tallies in the original Pact text: Madras 30, Bombay with Sind 15, Punjab 8, Bihar & Orissa 18, Central Provinces 20, Assam 7, Bengal 30, United Provinces 20; later adjusted to 147/151 in the 1935 Act)—and **18% of general seats** were reserved for them in the Central Legislature.
- **Two-Tier Primary Election Mechanism:** For each reserved seat, Depressed Class voters alone would first elect a **panel of four candidates** in a primary election; subsequently, the **entire joint electorate** (caste Hindus and Depressed Classes together) would elect the final representative from among those four nominees.
- **Educational & Public Service Pledges:** Guaranteed fair representation in public services and earmarked educational grants for the Depressed Classes. The British Government accepted the Pact as a formal modification of the Communal Award and embedded it in the **Government of India Act 1935**—establishing the conceptual template for **reserved seats in joint electorates** later enshrined in `Articles 330` and `332` of the Constitution.

### Government of India Act 1935

Enacted in August 1935, the **Government of India Act of 1935** marked the second major milestone towards a completely responsible government in India. A monumental, intricate legal document—the longest Act ever passed by the British Parliament up to that time—it comprised **321 Sections and 10 Schedules** and became the single largest structural reservoir for the Constitution of independent India.

#### Complete Clause-by-Clause Features of the 1935 Act

1. **All-India Federation Blueprint & Three Legislative Lists:** It provided for the establishment of an **All-India Federation** consisting of **Provinces** and **Princely States** as constituent units. Accession was **compulsory** for British Indian provinces, but **voluntary** for Princely States via an **Instrument of Accession**; furthermore, the Federation could come into existence only when states accounting for at least **50% of the total princely population** and entitled to **52 of the 104 princely seats** in the Council of States acceded. The Act divided legislative powers between the Centre and units into **three exhaustive Lists**:
   - **Federal List** (for the Centre): **59 items** (defence, external affairs, currency, railways, posts, customs, census).
   - **Provincial List** (for the Provinces): **54 items** (police, public order, local government, agriculture, land revenue, education, public health).
   - **Concurrent List** (for both Centre and Provinces): **36 items** (criminal law, civil procedure, marriage & divorce, labour welfare, newspapers, factories).
   - **Residuary Powers:** Unlike modern India (where Parliament holds residuary powers) or the USA (where states hold them), **residuary legislative powers were vested in the Viceroy (Governor-General)**, who could assign any unenumerated subject to either the Federal or Provincial legislature—a deliberate compromise between Congress (demanding central residuary powers) and the Muslim League (demanding provincial residuary powers).
   - **Fate:** The **All-India Federation NEVER came into being** because the **Princely States refused to join**, fearing erosion of their autocratic internal sovereignty and treaty privileges.
2. **Abolition of Provincial Dyarchy & Introduction of Provincial Autonomy:** The Act **abolished Dyarchy in the provinces** and introduced **"Provincial Autonomy"** in its place. Provinces were recognised as **autonomous units of administration** with independent legal personality deriving authority directly from the Crown rather than as delegated agents of the Central Government. Even more significantly, the Act introduced **responsible government in the provinces**—requiring the Governor to act with the advice of ministers responsible to the provincial legislature across all provincial subjects (though severely hedged by the Governor's discretionary powers, "individual judgment," special responsibilities for law and order/minorities, and emergency takeover power under `Section 93`). This part came into effect on **`1 April 1937`** and was discontinued in **`October–November 1939`** when Congress ministries resigned.
3. **Adoption of Dyarchy at the Centre:** Ironically, while ending Dyarchy in the provinces, the Act **provided for the adoption of Dyarchy at the Centre**. Federal subjects were divided into **Reserved subjects** (defence, external affairs, ecclesiastical affairs, and tribal areas—administered by the Governor-General with up to three appointed counsellors, completely unaccountable to the legislature) and **Transferred subjects** (administered with the aid of a Council of Ministers responsible to the Federal Legislature, yet hedged by the Governor-General's vast safeguards over financial stability and credit). The proposed **Federal Legislature** was bicameral: a permanent **Council of State** (260 members: 156 British India + 104 Princely States, one-third retiring every 3 years) and a **Federal Assembly** (375 members: 250 British India + 125 Princely States, 5-year term; unusually, the upper house was directly elected while the lower house was indirectly elected by provincial assemblies!). **However, Central Dyarchy and the Federal Legislature NEVER came into operation** because the Federation failed to materialise; the Central Government continued operating under the `1919` framework until `1947`.
4. **Bicameralism in Six Provinces:** It introduced **bicameralism** in **six out of eleven Governors' Provinces**—specifically **Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Bihar, Assam, and the United Provinces**—each equipped with a **Legislative Council** (upper house) and a **Legislative Assembly** (lower house). The remaining five provinces (**Punjab, North-West Frontier Province, Sind, Orissa, and Central Provinces & Berar**) remained unicameral. Numerous financial and legislative restrictions were still placed on all provincial chambers.
5. **Further Extension of Communal & Special Group Representation:** The Act deepened electoral fragmentation by extending communal and special-group representation to **Depressed Classes (Scheduled Castes)** (implemented via the Poona Pact's reserved seats within general electorates alongside the Act's communal schedule), **women**, and **labour (workers)**—in addition to retaining separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans.
6. **Abolition of the Council of India:** It **abolished the Council of India** (established in London by the `Government of India Act 1858`), replacing it with a small **team of advisers** (three to six advisers) to assist the Secretary of State for India.
7. **Expansion of Franchise:** It **extended the franchise** substantially: roughly **10% of the total population** (about 30 million voters across British India, including ~4.5 million women) gained the right to vote—still restricted by property, land-revenue, tax, and literacy qualifications rather than universal adult suffrage.
8. **Reserve Bank of India (`1935`):** It provided for the establishment of a **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** to control the currency and credit of the country (formally inaugurated on **`1 April 1935`** under the RBI Act, 1934).
9. **Three-Tier Public Service Commissions:** It provided for the establishment of not only a **Federal Public Service Commission (FPSC)**, but also a **Provincial Public Service Commission (PPSC)** for each province and a **Joint Public Service Commission (JPSC)** for two or more provinces.
10. **Establishment of the Federal Court (`1937`):** It provided for the establishment of a **Federal Court**, formally inaugurated in Delhi in **`October 1937`** with **`Sir Maurice Gwyer`** as its first Chief Justice (assisted by two Indian judges, Sir Shah Muhammad Sulaiman and M.R. Jayakar). The Federal Court exercised **original jurisdiction** in constitutional disputes between the Centre and provinces or inter-provincial disputes, **appellate jurisdiction** over provincial High Courts on substantial questions of constitutional law, and **advisory jurisdiction** upon reference by the Governor-General. Yet ultimate appellate supremacy remained with the **Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London**.
11. **Territorial Reorganisations & Statutory Authorities:** The Act **separated Burma from India** (effective `April 1937`), **separated Aden** from India (converting it into a Crown Colony), and carved out **two new provinces**—**Orissa** (separated from Bihar) and **Sind** (separated from Bombay)—raising British India to **11 Governors' Provinces** (plus 6 Chief Commissioners' Provinces). It also created an independent **Federal Railway Authority** (insulated from ministerial interference) and strengthened the office of the **Auditor-General of India** (direct institutional precursor to the Comptroller and Auditor General under `Article 148`).

#### The 1937 Provincial Elections, 28 Months of Congress Rule & Nationalist Critique

Despite intense nationalist hostility to the Act—**Jawaharlal Nehru** famously condemned it as **"a new charter of slavery"** and **"a machine with strong brakes, but no engine"** (because over 80% of the central budget remained non-votable, the Governor-General and Governors wielded sweeping discretionary overrides, and India still lacked sovereignty), while M.A. Jinnah denounced it as "thoroughly rotten, fundamentally bad, and totally unacceptable"—the Congress decided (at its Lucknow and Faizpur sessions, `1936`) to contest the provincial elections of early **`1937`** to carry its anti-imperialist message to millions of voters and demonstrate mass legitimacy.

The **1937 election results** stunned the British Raj. Across the 11 Governors' Provinces, the Indian National Congress won **716 out of 1,161 seats** it contested—securing outright majorities in **five provinces** (Madras, United Provinces, Central Provinces, Bihar, and Orissa) and emerging as the single largest party in **Bombay, Assam, and the North-West Frontier Province**. After extracting informal assurances from Viceroy Lord Linlithgow that Governors would not lightly invoke special powers in day-to-day governance, Congress formed ministries initially in six provinces (`July 1937`), expanding soon to **eight of the eleven provinces** (Madras, Bombay, Central Provinces, United Provinces, Bihar, Orissa, NWFP, and Assam—the three exceptions being **Bengal** under Fazlul Huq's Krishak Praja Party–League coalition, **Punjab** under Sikandar Hayat Khan's Unionist Party, and **Sind**).

During their **28 months in office (`July 1937 – October/November 1939`)**, Congress ministries delivered India's first real laboratory of self-government: they repealed emergency press curbs, lifted bans on nationalist organisations, released thousands of political prisoners, enacted agrarian tenancy and rural debt-relief statutes, expanded primary education and Harijan welfare, and proved that nationalist leaders could run complex provincial administrations with discipline and integrity.

Yet the period also deepened the fatal communal rift. Having fared poorly in 1937—winning barely **4.8% of the total Muslim vote** and failing to form a government in even a single Muslim-majority province—the **All-India Muslim League** under Jinnah pivoted aggressively toward mass communal mobilisation, publishing partisan indictments (the **Pirpur Report, `1938`**, and Shareef Report) accusing Congress ministries of "Hindu Raj." When World War II broke out in September `1939` and Viceroy **Lord Linlithgow** unilaterally declared India a belligerent without consulting Indian ministries or legislatures, all Congress ministries **resigned in protest (`October–November 1939`)**—triggering Governor's rule under `Section 93` across Congress provinces, while the Muslim League infamously celebrated **`22 December 1939`** as a **"Day of Deliverance."**

## 6. Constitutional Deadlock, Partition & Independence (1940–1947)

The Second World War (`1939–1945`) shattered British imperial capacity while locking the Raj, the Indian National Congress, and the Muslim League into a triangular constitutional stalemate. With the League adopting the **Lahore Resolution (`23 March 1940`)** demanding independent Muslim-majority "zones" in the north-west and east, every British constitutional initiative between `1940` and `1946` struggled—and ultimately failed—to reconcile two competing imperatives: preserving India's territorial unity while securing communal consensus.

### August Offer (1940), Cripps Mission (1942), Rajagopalachari Formula & Wavell Plan (1945)

#### August Offer (`8 August 1940` — Lord Linlithgow)

Stunned by the fall of France and Hitler's blitzkrieg in Europe, Viceroy **Lord Linlithgow** issued the **August Offer** on `8 August 1940` to secure Indian cooperation in the war:

- **Dominion Status & Constituent Assembly:** Explicitly acknowledged **Dominion Status** as the objective for India and, for the **first time**, conceded Indians' right to frame their own constitution through a **Constituent Assembly** after the war—where **mainly Indians** (not exclusively Indians) would decide the constitution based on social, economic, and political conceptions.
- **Expansion of Executive Council:** Proposed immediate expansion of the Viceroy's Executive Council to include more Indians (carried out in `July 1941`, giving Indians 8 of 12 seats, though Defence, Finance, and Home remained in British hands) and creation of a War Advisory Council.
- **The Minority Veto:** Explicitly declared that **no future constitution would be adopted without the consent of minorities**—effectively handing the Muslim League a permanent constitutional veto over Indian political progress.

Congress rejected the offer outright (Jawaharlal Nehru famously dismissed Dominion Status as "dead as a doornail," and Mahatma Gandhi launched **Individual Satyagraha** in `October 1940`, with Vinoba Bhave as first satyagrahi). The Muslim League welcomed the minority veto yet rejected the offer because it did not explicitly promise Partition.

#### Cripps Mission (`March 1942` — Sir Stafford Cripps)

After Japan's lightning conquest of Malaya, Singapore (`February 1942`), and Rangoon (`March 1942`) brought the war to India's eastern doorstep—and amid sharp pressure from US President Franklin D. Roosevelt and Chinese leader Chiang Kai-shek—Winston Churchill's War Cabinet dispatched **Sir Stafford Cripps** (a socialist Labour cabinet minister sympathetic to Indian leaders) with a Draft Declaration (`30 March 1942`):

- **Indian Union with Dominion Status:** Proposed setting up an **Indian Union with Dominion Status** immediately after the cessation of hostilities, free to decide its relations with the British Commonwealth and to participate in the United Nations and other international bodies.
- **Solely Indian Constituent Assembly:** After the war, a **Constituent Assembly** composed **solely of Indians** would be convened to frame the new constitution—its members partly elected by provincial assemblies through proportional representation and partly nominated by the princes.
- **Provincial Opt-Out Clause (First Blueprint of Partition):** The British Government would accept the new Constitution subject to **two conditions**: (1) **any province not prepared to accept the new Constitution would have the right to retain its existing constitutional position or frame a separate constitution and obtain the same full Dominion status as the Indian Union**; and (2) a treaty would be negotiated between the Constituent Assembly and Britain covering transfer of power and protection of racial and religious minorities.
- **Wartime Defence & Executive Control:** During the war, the **defence of India** would remain strictly under British control, and the Governor-General's powers would stay completely intact.

Both major parties rejected the Cripps proposals. Congress objected to Dominion Status instead of full independence, the nomination (rather than election) of princely representatives, the absence of immediate cabinet responsibility (or an Indian Defence Member), and—most fatally—the **provincial right to secede**, which violated national unity; Gandhi dismissed the delayed wartime promise as a **"post-dated cheque"** (popularly remembered with the added phrase "on a crashing bank"). The Muslim League rejected it because it envisaged a single Indian Union rather than an explicit sovereign Pakistan. Negotiations collapsed in April 1942, precipitating the historic **Quit India Movement (`August 1942`)**.

#### C. Rajagopalachari Formula (`1944`) & Desai–Liaquat Pact (`1945`)

With senior Congress leaders jailed after Quit India, veteran Madras leader **C. Rajagopalachari** drafted a pragmatic compromise (**CR Formula, `1944`**), backed by Gandhi, to break the Congress–League impasse: the Muslim League would endorse Indian independence and cooperate with Congress in forming a provisional interim government; after the war, a commission would demarcate contiguous Muslim-majority districts in north-west and north-east India where the **entire population** would decide via **plebiscite** whether to form a separate sovereign state; if separation occurred, mutual agreements would safeguard defence, commerce, and communications. During the **Gandhi–Jinnah Talks (`September 1944`)**, Jinnah rejected the formula—insisting that **only Muslims** should vote in the plebiscite, rejecting any common centre, and demanding acceptance of the two-nation theory *before* British departure.

A subsequent informal initiative—the **Desai–Liaquat Pact (`January 1945`)**, negotiated between Bhulabhai Desai (leader of Congress in the Central Assembly) and Liaquat Ali Khan (deputy leader of the Muslim League)—proposed an interim central government with equal Congress and League representation (40% each) plus 20% seats for minorities, operating within the 1935 framework; neither party leadership formally endorsed it, though the idea of **parity** resurfaced months later at Shimla.

#### Wavell Plan & the Shimla Conference (`June–July 1945`)

With the European war ending in May 1945 and UK general elections approaching, Viceroy **Lord Wavell** unveiled the **Wavell Plan (`14 June 1945`)** and invited 21 prominent Indian leaders to the **Shimla Conference (`25 June – 14 July 1945`)**:

- **Indianised Executive Council:** Except for the **Governor-General** and the **Commander-in-Chief**, **all members of the Viceroy's Executive Council were to be Indians**.
- **Caste-Hindu and Muslim Parity:** **Caste Hindus and Muslims were to have equal representation** in the reconstituted Council (a glaring concession to the League despite Muslims comprising ~25% of the population).
- **Interim Character & Veto:** The reconstituted Council would function as an interim government within the framework of the `1935` Act (still NOT responsible to the Central Assembly), and the Governor-General retained his veto (to be exercised on ministerial advice, not unreasonably), while Foreign Affairs would be handed to an Indian member.

The **Shimla Conference broke down** over Jinnah's uncompromising demand that **ALL Muslim members of the Executive Council must be nominated exclusively by the Muslim League**—denying Congress (then presided over by **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad**) the right to nominate any Muslim and excluding Punjab's powerful non-League Unionist Party—paired with a demand for a communal veto requiring a two-thirds majority whenever Muslim members opposed a decision. Congress refused to be branded an exclusively "caste Hindu" party. Instead of proceeding without the League, Wavell abruptly terminated the conference—inadvertently validating Jinnah's claim to be the "sole spokesman" of Indian Muslims and setting the stage for polarised provincial elections in the winter of `1945–1946` (where Congress swept general seats and the League swept almost all Muslim seats).

### Cabinet Mission Plan (`16 May 1946`)

By early `1946`, Britain was economically exhausted, Labour Prime Minister **Clement Attlee** presided over a pro-decolonisation Cabinet, and India was swept by post-war revolutionary ferment—the **INA Trials (`November 1945`)** at the Red Fort and the **Royal Indian Navy (RIN) Mutiny (`February 1946`)**. On `24 March 1946`, a high-powered three-member **Cabinet Mission** arrived in New Delhi consisting of **`Lord Pethick-Lawrence`** (Secretary of State for India, Chairman), **`Sir Stafford Cripps`** (President of the Board of Trade), and **`A.V. Alexander`** (First Lord of the Admiralty). Following failed tripartite talks at Shimla, the Mission announced its landmark **Cabinet Mission Plan on `16 May 1946`**—the final serious constitutional blueprint for an undivided India.

#### Core Provisions of the Cabinet Mission Plan

- **Explicit Rejection of a Sovereign Pakistan:** After rigorously examining the League's demand, the Mission **rejected the creation of a full-fledged, sovereign Pakistan** on compelling demographic, administrative, and strategic grounds: (1) a Pakistan comprising all of Punjab, Bengal, and Assam would still contain nearly **38% non-Muslims** in the north-west and **48% non-Muslims** in the north-east, solving no communal problem; (2) conversely, partitioning Punjab and Bengal would fracture linguistic, cultural, and Sikh communities; (3) economic ties, postal/rail networks, and the **Indian Armed Forces** would be dangerously disrupted; and (4) the two wings of Pakistan would be separated by over a thousand miles of Indian territory.
- **Three-Tier Federal Union Structure:** To allay Muslim fears without destroying Indian unity, the Plan designed a loose, decentralised three-tier federation:
  1. **Tier 1 — Union of India:** Embracing both British India and the Princely States, restricted strictly to **three subjects**: **Foreign Affairs, Defence, and Communications**, together with the power to raise the finances required for these subjects. Any decision in the Union Legislature raising a **major communal issue** required a majority of representatives present and voting of **each of the two major communities** (Hindus and Muslims) as well as a majority of the whole House.
  2. **Tier 2 — Grouping of Existing Provincial Assemblies into Three Sections:** Provinces were compulsorily assigned to three regional Sections to frame provincial constitutions and decide whether to form a **Group Constitution** with shared regional subjects:
     - **Section A (Hindu-majority):** Madras, Bombay, Central Provinces, United Provinces, Bihar, and Orissa (6 provinces).
     - **Section B (Muslim-majority, North-West):** Punjab, North-West Frontier Province (NWFP), and Sind (3 provinces).
     - **Section C (Muslim-majority, East):** Bengal and Assam (2 provinces).
  3. **Tier 3 — Provinces & Princely States:** **All subjects other than Union subjects, and all residuary powers**, were vested in the **Provinces** (and Princely States retained all powers not ceded to the Union). After the first general elections under the new constitution, any province could elect to **opt out of its Group**, and any province could call for a **reconsideration of the terms of the Union/Group Constitution** after **10 years** (and at 10-year intervals thereafter).
- **Constituent Assembly (`389 Members`):** Provided for an indirectly elected **Constituent Assembly** of **389 members**—**292** from Governors' Provinces, **4** from Chief Commissioners' Provinces, and **93** from Princely States—allocated roughly at **one seat per million population**, with British Indian seats distributed among **General, Muslim, and Sikh** communities and elected by provincial MLAs via **Proportional Representation by Single Transferable Vote (PR-STV)**. *(See Chapter 2: Making of the Constitution for detailed elections and committee working.)*
- **Interim Government & Lapse of Paramountcy:** Proposed an immediate all-Indian **Interim Government** at the Centre enjoying major-party support (sworn in on **`2 September 1946`** under Jawaharlal Nehru; the Muslim League joined in October 1946 largely to obstruct functioning from within), and declared that **British paramountcy over princely states would lapse** upon transfer of power—neither retained by Britain nor transferred to the new Indian government.

#### Breakdown of the Cabinet Mission Plan

The Plan's fatal flaw lay in **deliberate ambiguity over whether provincial grouping was compulsory or optional**. The Muslim League initially accepted the Plan (`6 June 1946`) solely because it read **compulsory grouping** (Sections B and C) as the legal embryo of Pakistan. Congress accepted the long-term Plan (`24 June 1946`) with its own interpretation—that provinces (particularly Congress-ruled **NWFP** and Hindu-majority **Assam**) could choose *not* to join a Section from the very beginning.

When Jawaharlal Nehru remarked at a Bombay press conference (`10 July 1946`) that Congress entered the Constituent Assembly completely unfettered—and predicted that grouping would likely collapse because NWFP and Assam would opt out—Jinnah seized the pretext. The Muslim League **withdrew its acceptance (`29 July 1946`)**, called for **"Direct Action Day" (`16 August 1946`)**—which plunged Calcutta into the horror of the Great Calcutta Killings and ignited retaliatory massacres across Noakhali, Bihar, Garhmukteshwar, and Punjab—and **boycotted the inaugural sitting of the Constituent Assembly on `9 December 1946`**.

### Attlee's Statement (`20 February 1947`) & Mountbatten Plan (`3 June 1947`)

#### Attlee's Historic Declaration (`20 February 1947`)

Confronted by paralysed Interim Government politics, escalating communal violence, and rapidly eroding British administrative control, Prime Minister **Clement Attlee** made a dramatic deadline announcement in the House of Commons on **`20 February 1947`**:

- **Fixed Deadline for British Exit:** Declared that British rule in India would end by **`30 June 1948`** ("a date not later than June 1948"), irrespective of whether Indian political parties reached constitutional agreement.
- **Hint of Partition:** Stated that if a fully representative Constituent Assembly had not framed a constitution by that date, Britain would decide to whom to hand over central power—whether as a whole to some form of central government, or **in some areas to the existing provincial governments**, or in such other way as seemed most reasonable.
- **Change of Viceroy:** Announced that **`Lord Louis Mountbatten`** would replace Lord Wavell as the **last Viceroy of India** to execute the transfer of power.

#### Mountbatten Plan (`3 June 1947` — June 3rd Plan)

Arriving in March 1947, Mountbatten quickly concluded that the Cabinet Mission Plan was dead and Partition unavoidable. After Nehru vehemently rejected Mountbatten's initial draft—**"Plan Balkan"** (also known as the Ismay Plan), which contemplated transferring power to individual provinces or confederations, risking the splintering of India into dozens of states—Mountbatten worked closely with Reforms Commissioner **V.P. Menon** to draft a streamlined alternative built on **Dominion Status** for two successor states. Announced on **`3 June 1947`** and swiftly accepted by the **Indian National Congress**, the **Muslim League**, and **Sikh representatives**, the **Mountbatten Plan** stipulated:

- **Partition of Bengal and Punjab:** The Provincial Legislative Assemblies of **Bengal** and **Punjab** were to meet in **two separate parts**—one representing **Muslim-majority districts** and the other **non-Muslim-majority districts**—and vote separately on whether the province should be partitioned. If a simple majority of **either part** voted for partition, the province would be partitioned (both provincial assemblies duly voted for partition, giving India West Bengal and East Punjab, and Pakistan East Bengal and West Punjab).
- **Choice of Sind, Baluchistan, NWFP & Sylhet:** The **Sind** Legislative Assembly would take its own decision (it voted for Pakistan); **Baluchistan** would decide via the Shahi Jirga and non-official members of Quetta municipality (chose Pakistan); and **referendums** were ordered in the **North-West Frontier Province (NWFP)** and the Muslim-majority **Sylhet district of Assam** to decide their fate (both voted to join Pakistan, though Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan's *Khudai Khidmatgars* boycotted the NWFP referendum demanding a third option of *Pakhtunistan*).
- **Constituent Assemblies:** The existing Constituent Assembly would continue framing the Constitution for areas joining India, while a **separate Constituent Assembly** would be formed for areas choosing Pakistan.
- **Princely States:** While legally paramountcy would lapse, Mountbatten firmly discouraged princely independence (unlike Plan Balkan), pressing rulers to accede to either India or Pakistan based on geographical contiguity and popular will.
- **Boundary Commission:** A **Boundary Commission** would be constituted to demarcate international boundaries in the partitioned provinces of Bengal and Punjab.
- **Accelerated Independence Date:** Transfer of power was dramatically brought forward by more than ten months—from June 1948 to **`15 August 1947`** (the second anniversary of Japan's World War II surrender to Mountbatten's South East Asia Command), leaving barely **72 days** to partition an empire of 400 million people.

### Indian Independence Act 1947

To give immediate statutory effect to the Mountbatten Plan, the British Parliament introduced the Indian Independence Bill on `4 July 1947`, passed it through both Houses in less than two weeks without amendment, and secured **Royal Assent on `18 July 1947`**. The **Indian Independence Act of 1947** formally closed 174 years of British parliamentary governance over India.

#### Complete Clause-by-Clause Provisions of the 1947 Act

1. **End of British Rule & Declaration of Sovereignty:** It ended British rule in India and declared India an **independent and sovereign state from `15 August 1947`**.
2. **Partition into Two Independent Dominions:** It provided for the **partition of India** and the creation of **two independent Dominions—India and Pakistan**—effective `15 August 1947`, with the full **right to secede from the British Commonwealth**.
3. **Abolition of Viceroy & Dominion Governors-General:** It **abolished the office of Viceroy** and provided, for each Dominion, a **Governor-General**, who was to be **appointed by the British King on the advice of the respective Dominion Cabinet**. Crucially, His Majesty's Government in Britain was to have **no responsibility whatsoever** with respect to the Government of India or Pakistan.
4. **Full Sovereignty & Dual Role of Constituent Assemblies:** It empowered the **Constituent Assemblies** of both Dominions to **frame and adopt any Constitution** for their respective nations and to **repeal any Act of the British Parliament, including the Indian Independence Act itself**. It further empowered each Constituent Assembly to **legislate for its respective territory** until the new Constitution was drafted and enforced. **No Act of the British Parliament passed after `15 August 1947`** was to extend to either of the new Dominions unless explicitly extended by a law of that Dominion's legislature.
5. **Abolition of Secretary of State for India:** It **abolished the office of the Secretary of State for India** and transferred his remaining transitional functions to the **Secretary of State for Commonwealth Affairs**.
6. **Lapse of British Paramountcy:** It proclaimed the **lapse of British paramountcy over the Indian Princely States** and **treaty relations with tribal areas** from `15 August 1947`. Consequently, it granted **freedom to the Indian Princely States** either to join the Dominion of India, join the Dominion of Pakistan, or remain independent (a legal vacuum swiftly resolved inside India by Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and V.P. Menon's integration of 552 princely states).
7. **Interim Governance under the GoI Act 1935:** It provided for the governance of each of the Dominions and the provinces by the **Government of India Act of 1935** (as adapted and modified by each Dominion's Governor-General) until the new Constitutions were framed.
8. **Removal of Royal Veto & Constitutional Heads:** It **deprived the British Monarch of his right to veto bills** or ask for the reservation of certain bills for his approval. However, this right was reserved for the **Governor-General**, who was given full power to assent to any bill in the name of His Majesty. Simultaneously, it designated the **Governor-General of India** and the **Provincial Governors** as **constitutional (nominal) heads** of the states, bound to act on the advice of their respective Councils of Ministers in all matters.
9. **Dropping of Imperial Royal Title:** It **dropped the title of "Emperor of India"** from the royal titles of the King of England.
10. **Discontinuation of Secretary of State's Civil Services:** It **discontinued appointments to civil services and reservation of posts by the Secretary of State for India**. However, members of the civil services appointed **before `15 August 1947`** continued to enjoy all service conditions and benefits to which they were entitled up to that date (later protected constitutionally under `Article 314`, subsequently repealed by the `28th Amendment Act, 1972`).

#### The Radcliffe Boundary Commissions & the Midnight Transfer of Power

To demarcate the new international borders of partitioned Punjab and Bengal (including Sylhet), two **Boundary Commissions** were established under the chairmanship of **`Sir Cyril Radcliffe`**—a British barrister who had **never visited India before**—assisted in each commission by four Indian High Court judges (two nominated by Congress, two by the Muslim League). Because the party nominees deadlocked predictably on every contested tehsil, Radcliffe bore sole responsibility for drawing lines across dense irrigation canals, railway networks, and mixed-population districts within barely five weeks. Although Radcliffe completed his awards on `12 August 1947`, Mountbatten delayed their publication until **`17 August 1947`**—two full days *after* independence—so that partition violence would not mar the independence ceremonies, tragically compounding the chaos of displacement across Punjab and Bengal.

At the stroke of midnight on **`14–15 August 1947`**, British rule ended and sovereign power passed to the two new independent Dominions of India and Pakistan. **`Lord Mountbatten`** became the **first Governor-General of the new Dominion of India** (while M.A. Jinnah became Governor-General of Pakistan) and swore in **`Jawaharlal Nehru`** as the **first Prime Minister of independent India**. When Mountbatten departed in **`June 1948`**, **`C. Rajagopalachari`** succeeded him to become the **first and only Indian Governor-General of India** (and the last Governor-General until the inauguration of the Republic on `26 January 1950`).

Meanwhile, the **Constituent Assembly of India**—constituted in `1946` under the Cabinet Mission Plan—became a **fully sovereign body** and doubled as the **first Parliament of free India** (chaired by **Dr Rajendra Prasad** whenever it met to frame the Constitution, and by **G.V. Mavalankar** whenever it sat as the Dominion Legislature).

## 7. Legacy of Colonial Statutes on the Indian Constitution

When the Constituent Assembly adopted the Constitution of India on `26 November 1949` (enforced on `26 January 1950`), critics immediately dubbed it a **"carbon copy of the Government of India Act, 1935"** or a "glorified edition" of colonial law. Indeed, roughly **250 out of the 395 original Articles** were adapted—often closely—from the `1935` statute and earlier enactments stretching back to `1773`. Yet a rigorous UPSC Mains analysis must separate **structural administrative continuity** from **philosophical and democratic rupture**: while the framers wisely retained familiar institutional machinery to prevent post-Partition state collapse, they infused it with a revolutionary democratic philosophy that overturned every foundational premise of British rule.

### Direct Structural Borrowings from Colonial Statutes

Across nearly two centuries, colonial enactments built an intricate institutional grammar that Indian politicians, judges, and administrators had already operated (especially during the 1937–39 ministries and the 1947–50 Dominion interlude). Retaining tested structural bones allowed the fledgling Republic to focus its energy on national integration, Partition rehabilitation, and socio-economic transformation.

| Institutional Feature in 1950 Constitution | Constitutional Provision | Colonial Statutory Origin & Continuity |
|---|---|---|
| **Three-List Federal Scheme** | `Article 246` & `Seventh Schedule` (Union, State, Concurrent Lists) | **GoI Act 1935** (Federal 59, Provincial 54, Concurrent 36 Lists) — *Key change: residuary powers shifted from Viceroy to Parliament (`Art. 248`)* |
| **Office of Governor** | `Articles 153–163`, `200`, `201` (Central nominee; reservation of state bills) | **GoI Act 1935** (Crown-appointed Governor with discretionary & reservation powers) |
| **Emergency Provisions** | `Part XVIII` (`Articles 352`, `356`, `360`) | **GoI Act 1935** (`Section 102` war/internal disturbance emergency & `Section 93` failure of provincial constitutional machinery) |
| **Ordinance-Making Power** | `Article 123` (President) & `Article 213` (Governor) | **Indian Councils Act 1861** (Viceroy's 6-month emergency ordinance) & **GoI Act 1935** (`Sections 42–43`, `88`) |
| **Integrated Judiciary & Federal Jurisdiction** | `Articles 124–147` (Supreme Court: original, appellate & advisory) | **Regulating Act 1773** (Calcutta SC `1774`), High Courts Act `1861`, and **Federal Court (`1937`) under GoI Act 1935** |
| **Public Service Commissions & Merit Bureaucracy** | `Articles 315–323` (UPSC, SPSC, JPSC) & `Article 312` (All-India Services) | **Charter Act 1853** (open ICS), **GoI Act 1919** (CPSC `1926`), and **GoI Act 1935** (FPSC, PPSC, JPSC) |
| **Cabinet Portfolio System & Bicameralism** | `Articles 74–78`, `79–81`, `168–171` | **Charter Act 1853** (separate legislature), **1861 Act** (Canning's Portfolio System `1859`), **GoI Act 1919** (bicameral Centre), **1935** (provincial bicameralism) |
| **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)** | `Articles 148–151` | **GoI Act 1919 & 1935** (statutory Auditor-General of India independent of executive) |
| **State Suability (Body Corporate)** | `Article 300` (Union/States can sue and be sued) | **GoI Act 1858** (Secretary of State-in-Council constituted as body corporate) & **1935 Act** (`Section 176`) |
| **Non-Discrimination in Public Employment** | `Article 16(2)` | Seeded in **`Section 87` of Charter Act 1833** and Queen's Proclamation (`1858`) |

### Transformative Breaks in 1950: Why the Constitution Was Revolutionary

Equating administrative continuity with colonial mimicry misses the tectonic break accomplished in `1950`. On every fundamental question of sovereignty, citizenship, rights, and representation, the Constitution repudiated the colonial state:

- **Republican Popular Sovereignty:** Every statute from `1773` to `1947` was an Act of the British Parliament deriving legitimacy from the British Crown. In `1950`, **"We, the People of India"** asserted autochthonous sovereignty—repealing both the `GoI Act 1935` and the `Indian Independence Act 1947` via `Article 395` (even severing formal British legal pedigree!), abolishing Dominion Status, and establishing a **Sovereign Democratic Republic** headed by an elected President.
- **Universal Adult Suffrage (`Article 326`):** Under the `1935` Act, barely **10% of the population** met property, tax, or literacy thresholds to vote. In one of history's boldest acts of democratic faith, the 1950 Constitution leaped straight to **universal adult franchise** (all citizens aged 21+, lowered to 18+ by the `61st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1988`)—rejecting decades of colonial insistence that an illiterate, impoverished society was "unfit" for democracy.
- **Justiciable Fundamental Rights & Socio-Economic Transformation (`Parts III & IV`):** The `Government of India Act 1935` conspicuously **omitted any Bill of Rights** (Britain's Joint Parliamentary Committee explicitly dismissed Fundamental Rights as impractical "abstract declarations"). Drawing instead on the indigenous nationalist canon—the **Nehru Report (`1928`)** and the **Karachi Congress Resolution (`1931`)**—the 1950 Constitution guaranteed enforceable **Fundamental Rights (`Articles 12–35`)** backed by writ remedies (`Articles 32` and `226`), abolished untouchability (`Article 17`), and paired them with **Directive Principles of State Policy (`Articles 36–51`)** aimed at social and economic revolution.
- **Abolition of Separate Electorates (`Article 325`):** The Constituent Assembly decisively dismantled the four-decade colonial edifice of **communal separate electorates** (`1909` → `1919` → `1935`), mandating **one general electoral roll** for every territorial constituency with no exclusion on grounds of religion, race, caste, or sex. To uplift historically marginalised communities without fracturing civic nationhood, the framers adopted the **Poona Pact model** of time-bound **reserved seats in joint electorates** solely for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (`Articles 330` and `332`).
- **Genuine Responsible Parliamentary Federalism & Independent Guardians:** Unlike `1935`—which kept Defence, Foreign Affairs, Finance, and residuary powers in the Viceroy's irresponsible hands—the 1950 Constitution established full **collective ministerial responsibility** to the Lok Sabha (`Article 75(3)`), vested **residuary powers in an elected Parliament** (`Article 248`), created an **independent Supreme Court** terminating Privy Council appeals (via the *Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act, 1949*), and instituted an autonomous **Election Commission of India (`Article 324`)** to guarantee free and fair elections.

> [!mains]
> **Dr B.R. Ambedkar's Iconic Defence (`4 November 1948`, Constituent Assembly):** Whenever a GS-2 question probes whether the Indian Constitution is merely an "expanded version of the Government of India Act, 1935," anchor your evaluation with Ambedkar's classic reply while introducing the Draft Constitution:
> *"As to the accusation that the Draft Constitution has reproduced a good part of the provisions of the Government of India Act, 1935, I make no apologies. There is nothing to be ashamed of in borrowing. It involves no plagiarism. Nobody holds any patent rights in the fundamental ideas of a Constitution."*
> Ambedkar clarified that what was borrowed pertained overwhelmingly to **administrative details** (included in the Constitution to protect fledgling Indian democracy from administrative distortion), whereas the **soul of the Constitution**—sovereignty, justice, liberty, equality, and fraternity—belonged wholly to India's freedom struggle.

## 1. Roots of British Constitutional Control (1600–1773)

- **Charter (`31 Dec 1600`):** Queen Elizabeth I incorporated the EIC with exclusive eastern trade monopoly (15 yrs; indefinite under James I, `1609`); governed by Court of Proprietors & 24-member **Court of Directors**; `Charter of 1726` set up Mayor's Courts at Madras, Bombay, Calcutta.
- **Territorial Shift:** **Plassey (`23 June 1757`)** & **Buxar (`22 Oct 1764`)** (Hector Munro vs Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daulah, Shah Alam II) → **Treaty of Allahabad (`12 Aug 1765`)** granted EIC the **Diwani** (revenue + civil justice) of **Bengal, Bihar, Orissa** (₹26 lakh tribute).
- **Dual Government (`1765–1772`):** Clive split power (EIC) and responsibility (Nawab + deputies Mohammad Reza Khan, Raja Shitab Rai) → **Bengal Famine (`1770`)** (~1/3 dead); ended by Warren Hastings (`1772`); EIC's £1.4M bailout plea triggered parliamentary control (`1773`).

## 2. Early Company Statutes (1773–1813)

- **Regulating Act `1773`:** First parliamentary regulation of EIC; recognised political/administrative functions; base of central administration. **Governor-General of Bengal** (**Warren Hastings**) + **4-member Council** (casting vote only, no veto); Bombay & Madras subordinated; **Calcutta Supreme Court (`1774`)** (CJ **Sir Elijah Impey** + 3 judges); banned private trade & bribes; Directors reported revenue, civil, military affairs to UK Govt.
- **Amending Act `1781` (Act of Settlement):** Exempted GG, Council & servants from Calcutta SC for **official acts**; excluded **revenue matters**; mandated **Hindu & Muslim personal laws**; routed provincial appeals to **GG-in-Council**; GG-in-Council framed **Regulations** without SC registration.
- **Pitt's India Act `1784`:** Split commercial (**Court of Directors**) vs political (**6-member Board of Control**) = **Double Government**; Council cut **4 → 3** (1 C-in-C); first called EIC territories **"British possessions in India"**; gave British Govt **supreme control**.
- **Act of `1786`:** Gave **Lord Cornwallis** Council **override power** in special cases + **Commander-in-Chief** office.
- **Charter Act `1793`:** Extended override to all future GGs & Governors; monopoly **+20 yrs**; C-in-C not ex-officio member unless appointed; **Board of Control & staff paid from Indian revenues** (till `1919`).
- **Charter Act `1813`:** Ended EIC Indian trade monopoly **except tea & China**; asserted **Crown sovereignty**; allowed **Christian missionaries**; **₹1 lakh/year** education grant; authorised **local governments to tax persons**.

## 3. Peak Centralisation & Legislative Rise (1833–1853)

- **Charter Act `1833` (Saint Helena Act):** Peak centralisation. **Governor-General of India** (**Lord William Bentinck**); **stripped Bombay & Madras of legislative power**; Regulations → **Acts**; ended EIC commercial role (**purely administrative**, territories held *"in trust for His Majesty"*); **4th Law Member** (**Macaulay**, legislative only) & **First Law Commission (`1834`)** (IPC `1860`, replaced by BNS `1 July 2024`); **`Section 87`** barred discrimination in office; **attempted open ICS competition** (blocked by Directors).
- **Charter Act `1853`:** Last Charter Act. **Separated legislative & executive wings**; added **6 legislative councillors** forming **Indian (Central) Legislative Council ("mini-Parliament")**, **4 from Madras, Bombay, Bengal, Agra** (**first local representation**, British officials); **open ICS competition** (**Macaulay Committee `1854`**; **Satyendranath Tagore** first Indian ICS `1863`); indefinite Company trust; separate Lt.-Governor for Bengal; Directors cut 24 → 18 (6 Crown nominees).

## 4. Crown Rule & Representative Beginnings (1858–1909)

- **Queen's Proclamation (`1 Nov 1858`, Allahabad, Canning):** Ended annexations (Doctrine of Lapse repudiated); respect for princes, religious neutrality, equal legal protection.
- **GoI Act `1858` (Act for the Good Government of India):** Abolished EIC & **Double Government**; GG → **Viceroy** (**Lord Canning**, first); **Secretary of State for India** (**Lord Stanley**, UK Cabinet) + **15-member advisory Council of India**; Secretary of State-in-Council made a **body corporate**. No Indian representation yet.
- **Indian Councils Act `1861`:** Indians nominated as non-officials (**1862**: **Raja of Benares, Maharaja of Patiala, Sir Dinkar Rao**); **legislative devolution** restored law-making to Bombay & Madras (reversing `1833`); new councils for **Bengal (`1862`), NWP (`1886`), Punjab (`1897`)**; recognised **Portfolio System** (Canning `1859`); Viceroy's emergency **Ordinance power (6 months)**.
- **Indian Councils Act `1892`:** Expanded non-officials (Central 10–16) retaining official majority; allowed **budget discussion** (no vote/resolutions) & **questions** (no supplementaries); **indirect election** via **"recommendation"** of provincial councils, Bengal Chamber of Commerce, municipalities, district boards, universities, zamindars (word *"election"* NOT used).
- **Indian Councils Act `1909` (Morley–Minto):** Central Council **16 → 60** (official majority retained); provincial **non-official majority**; allowed **supplementary questions & budget resolutions**; **Satyendra Prasad Sinha** first Indian in **Viceroy's Executive Council** (Law Member); **separate electorates for Muslims** (Simla Deputation `Oct 1906`) → **Lord Minto = "Father of Communal Electorate"**; separate representation for corporations, chambers, universities, zamindars.

## 5. Responsible Government, Dyarchy & Provincial Autonomy (1919–1935)

- **Montagu Declaration (`20 Aug 1917`):** *"Progressive realisation of responsible government in India."*
- **GoI Act `1919` (Montagu–Chelmsford, enforced `1921`):** Separated Central & Provincial subjects (still **unitary**); **Provincial Dyarchy** — **Transferred** (education, health, local self-govt; Governor + responsible ministers) vs **Reserved** (police, justice, finance, land revenue; Governor + Executive Council); **Bicameral Centre** (**Council of State 60** + **Legislative Assembly 145**) & **direct elections**; **3 of 6** Viceroy's Executive Councillors (excl. C-in-C) Indians; communal electorates extended to **Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans**; **High Commissioner** in London (Secretary of State paid from UK Treasury); **Central PSC (`1926`)** via **Lee Commission (`1923–24`)**; **provincial budgets separated**; 10-year review.
- **Road to `1935`:** **Simon Commission (`Nov 1927`, 7 all-British, report `May 1930`)** boycotted → **Nehru Report (`Aug 1928`, Motilal Nehru)**: Dominion Status, joint electorates, 19 FRs, adult suffrage, central residuary powers vs **Jinnah's 14 Points (`1929`)** → **3 RTCs (`1930–32`)** (Gandhi only 2nd; **Ambedkar & Sapru** all 3) → **Communal Award (`16 Aug 1932`, Ramsay MacDonald)** (71 separate seats for Depressed Classes) → **Poona Pact (`24 Sept 1932`, Gandhi–Ambedkar)** kept Hindu joint electorate & raised Depressed Classes reserved seats **71 → 147/148** (+18% Central) → **White Paper (`1933`)** & Joint Select Committee.
- **GoI Act `1935`:** **321 Sections, 10 Schedules**. **All-India Federation** (unborn; princes stayed out); **3 Lists** — Federal (**59**), Provincial (**54**), Concurrent (**36**); **Residuary powers with Viceroy**; abolished provincial dyarchy → **Provincial Autonomy (`1937–39`)**; **Central Dyarchy** (never operational); **bicameralism in 6 of 11 provinces** (`B-B-M-B-A-U`: Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Bihar, Assam, UP); expanded communal/group representation (Depressed Classes via Poona Pact, women, labour); **abolished Council of India**; ~**10% franchise**; **RBI (`1935`)**; **FPSC, PPSC, JPSC**; **Federal Court (`1937`, CJ Sir Maurice Gwyer)**; separated **Burma & Aden**, created **Orissa & Sind**. **1937 Elections:** Congress ministries in **8 of 11 provinces**; resigned `Oct–Nov 1939` over unilateral WWII entry.

## 6. Constitutional Deadlock, Partition & Independence (1940–1947)

- **August Offer (`8 Aug 1940`, Linlithgow):** Explicit Dominion Status & post-war Constituent Assembly (*mainly* Indians); minority veto.
- **Cripps Mission (`March 1942`):** Dominion Status Union; *solely* Indian Constituent Assembly; **provincial opt-out right** (first Partition blueprint); British kept wartime defence → Quit India (`Aug 1942`).
- **CR Formula (`1944`) & Wavell Plan / Shimla (`June–July 1945`):** CR proposed post-war plebiscite; Wavell proposed all-Indian Executive Council (excl. Viceroy & C-in-C) with caste-Hindu/Muslim parity (collapsed over Jinnah's exclusive Muslim nomination demand).
- **Cabinet Mission Plan (`16 May 1946` — Pethick-Lawrence, Cripps, Alexander):** **Rejected Pakistan**; 3-tier Union (**Defence, Foreign Affairs, Communications**), provinces held residuary powers; **compulsory grouping** (**Sections A, B, C**); **389-member Constituent Assembly** (292+4+93); Interim Govt (`2 Sept 1946`). Collapsed over grouping → Direct Action Day (`16 Aug 1946`).
- **Attlee's Statement (`20 Feb 1947`):** Exit by **30 June 1948**; **Mountbatten Plan (`3 June 1947`)** partitioned Punjab & Bengal, referendums in NWFP & Sylhet, exit advanced to `15 Aug 1947`.
- **Indian Independence Act `1947` (Royal Assent `18 July`):** Sovereign Dominions (**India & Pakistan**) from **`15 Aug 1947`** (right to leave Commonwealth); abolished Viceroy & Secretary of State; **Governor-General** & Governors became **constitutional heads**; **Constituent Assemblies sovereign** (repeal any UK Act incl. IIA `1947`; dual legislature role — Rajendra Prasad / G.V. Mavalankar); **lapse of paramountcy**; GoI `1935` interim charter; dropped *"Emperor of India"*; **Radcliffe Award** (`17 Aug 1947`). **Mountbatten** (1st GG) → **C. Rajagopalachari** (`June 1948`, first & only Indian GG).

## 7. Colonial Legacy vs 1950 Transformative Break

- **Structural Borrowings (~250 Articles from `1935`):** Three Lists (`7th Schedule`), Governor, Emergency (`Arts. 352/356`), Ordinances (`1861/1935` → `Arts. 123/213`), Federal Court (`1937` → SC), UPSC/SPSC (`1926/1935`), Portfolio Cabinet (`1861`), CAG (`1935`), Body Corporate (`1858` → `Art. 300`).
- **Transformative Breaks (`1950`):** Popular republican sovereignty (`Art. 395` repealed `1935` & `1947` Acts); **Universal Adult Suffrage (`Art. 326`)** vs 10% in `1935`; **Fundamental Rights & DPSPs** (absent in `1935`; Nehru Report `1928` & Karachi `1931`); **abolition of separate electorates (`Art. 325`)**; residuary powers to **Parliament (`Art. 248`)**; independent SC & ECI.
