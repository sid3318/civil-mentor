## 1. Structure, Composition, Delimitation & Duration (Articles 79–83, 330–334A)

Parliament is the Union's legislative organ and the institutional core of India's representative democracy. Framed under **Part V (`Articles 79` to `122`)**, it reflects India's choice of the **Westminster parliamentary system** rather than the American presidential separation of powers: the Council of Ministers is drawn from Parliament and stays collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha (`Article 75(3)`).

Unlike the United Kingdom—where **Parliamentary Sovereignty** lets Parliament alter any law without judicial veto—India operates under **Constitutional Supremacy**. Parliament functions within a written federal Constitution, constrained by the **Seventh Schedule** division of powers, **Part III Fundamental Rights (`Article 13`)**, **judicial review**, and the **Basic Structure** doctrine (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973). Within its constitutional domain, Parliament acts simultaneously as supreme law-maker, custodian of public finance, and national deliberative forum.

### Organisation of Parliament: The Three Integral Organs (Article 79)

Under **`Article 79`**, the Union Parliament consists of **three** integral organs: (1) the **President of India**, (2) the **Council of States (Rajya Sabha)**, and (3) the **House of the People (Lok Sabha)**. The Hindi names **"Lok Sabha"** (14 May 1954) and **"Rajya Sabha"** (23 August 1954) were adopted in **1954**, though the English text retains "House of the People" and "Council of States".

#### Why Is the President an Integral Part of Parliament?

Though **not a member of either House**, the President is integral to Parliament under India's republican adaptation of the British "Crown-in-Parliament" model—unlike the **US President**, who stands outside the US Congress:

- **Law-Making Assent (`Article 111`):** No Bill passed by both Houses becomes an Act without Presidential assent.
- **Sessions & Dissolution (`Article 85`):** The President summons and prorogues both Houses, dissolves the Lok Sabha, and summons **Joint Sittings (`Article 108`)**.
- **Policy Addresses (`Articles 86–87`):** The President addresses both Houses together after every general election and at the first session each year.
- **Prior Recommendation Gatekeeping:** Needed for Money Bills (`Art. 117(1)`), Financial Bills Category I, Demands for Grants (`Art. 113(3)`), State-reorganisation Bills (`Art. 3`), and Bills affecting State-interest taxes (`Art. 274`).
- **Ordinances (`Article 123`):** Issues Ordinances with full statutory force when either or both Houses are not in session.

```flow
# Organisational Architecture of the Parliament of India (Article 79)
Parliament of the Union (Part V, Articles 79–122) | Supreme Legislative Organ Built on Westminster Interdependence
President of India | Integral organ, NOT a member; assents Bills (Art. 111), summons/prorogues Houses, dissolves LS, issues Ordinances || Council of States (Rajya Sabha) | Art. 80 · Permanent Upper House · Max 250 / Current 245 (225 States + 8 UTs + 12 Nominated) || House of the People (Lok Sabha) | Art. 81 · Directly Elected Lower House · Max 550 / Current 543 (524 States + 19 UTs; 0 Nominated)
Constitutional Accountability Loop | Council of Ministers collectively responsible to Lok Sabha (Art. 75(3)); both Houses enact laws and guard the Constitution
```

#### Why Did the Constituent Assembly Adopt Bicameralism at the Union Level?

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar and N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar defended bicameralism on **four rationales**:

1. **Federal Representation:** The **Rajya Sabha** represents States and Union Territories as federal units, checking population-driven majoritarianism (`Articles 249, 312`).
2. **Revising Chamber:** Cools hasty or populist Lower-House legislation—holding *"dignified debates on important issues"* (Gopalaswami Ayyangar).
3. **Eminent Expertise:** Indirect elections and 12 nominations bring scientists, jurists, writers, and social reformers into law-making without mass campaigning.
4. **Constitutional Continuity:** Because Rajya Sabha never dissolves, it maintains legislative continuity when Lok Sabha is dissolved (including approving Emergencies under `Arts. 352, 356, 360`).

### Rajya Sabha: Composition, Allocation, Election & Nominations (Article 80)

**`Article 80`** fixes the structural blueprint of the Council of States. Its **maximum strength is capped at 250 members**, comprising not more than **238 representatives of the States and Union Territories** (elected indirectly) and **12 members nominated by the President**.

At present, the **current sanctioned strength of the Rajya Sabha is 245 members**, distributed across three streams:

- **225 members** representing India's **28 States**;
- **8 members** representing **three Union Territories** that possess Legislative Assemblies—**Jammu and Kashmir (4)** (all four seats filled in **October 2025** following the 2024 J&K Assembly elections), **National Capital Territory of Delhi (3)**, and **Puducherry (1)**; and
- **12 members** **nominated by the President** of India.

The remaining **five Union Territories**—Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Chandigarh, Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu, Ladakh, and Lakshadweep—have **no representation in the Rajya Sabha** because their populations are small and they lack Legislative Assemblies (the electoral college required to elect Rajya Sabha members).

#### Allocation of Seats: Fourth Schedule vs the American Senate

The **Fourth Schedule** of the Constitution allocates Rajya Sabha seats among the States and Union Territories on the basis of **population**—**NOT equality of states**. Consequently, representation varies dramatically across the federation: **Uttar Pradesh** sends **31** members, **Maharashtra** sends **19**, **Tamil Nadu** sends **18**, **Bihar** and **West Bengal** send **16** each, and **Karnataka** sends **12**, whereas **Goa**, **Sikkim**, and six Northeastern states (**Arunachal Pradesh, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, and Tripura**) send just **1 member each**.

This marks a sharp departure from the **United States Senate**, where every state—whether populous California or sparsely populated Wyoming—receives an exact equal quota of **two senators** (100 senators for 50 states), an entrenched federal guarantee that even a US Constitutional Amendment cannot alter without a state's consent. India's framers rejected equal state representation because Indian states were not pre-existing sovereign republics entering a compact; instead, the Constitution created a "Union of States" where demographic disparities among provinces were enormous. Even so, the Fourth Schedule builds in mild weightage for smaller states so that no state receives zero seats.

#### Indirect Election, PR-STV, Open Ballot & Removal of Domicile

Under **`Article 80(4)`**, the representatives of each State in the Rajya Sabha are elected by the **elected members of the State Legislative Assembly** (nominated MLAs—where they existed previously—and members of State Legislative Councils do **NOT** vote). Elections follow the system of **proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote (PR-STV)**, ensuring that opposition parties in a State Assembly win seats broadly proportionate to their legislative strength rather than suffering a winner-take-all sweep. For Union Territories, `Article 80(5)` empowers Parliament to prescribe the manner of election; Parliament did so under the Representation of the People Act, 1950/1951 through electoral colleges formed by the elected MLAs of Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu and Kashmir.

Two transformative statutory changes enacted via the **Representation of the People (Amendment) Act, 2003** altered Rajya Sabha electoral mechanics permanently:

1. **Abolition of State Domicile Requirement:** Originally, `Section 3` of the **Representation of the People Act (RPA), 1951** required a Rajya Sabha candidate to be a registered elector in the *same State* from which they sought election. The 2003 amendment dropped this state-domicile requirement, permitting any citizen registered as an **elector in any parliamentary constituency in India** to contest a Rajya Sabha seat from any State.
2. **Introduction of the Open Ballot System:** To curb rampant cross-voting and money power in biennial Rajya Sabha polls, the 2003 amendment replaced the secret ballot with an **open ballot** system: an MLA belonging to a political party must **show their marked ballot paper to an authorised agent of their political party** before dropping it into the ballot box. Refusal to show the ballot paper, or showing it to anyone other than the authorised party agent, renders the vote **invalid**. Crucially, **independent MLAs** are **barred** from showing their ballot to any party agent (doing so invalidates their vote).

> [!case]
> ***Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India* (2006) — Five-Judge Constitution Bench**
> - **Issue:** Veteran journalist Kuldip Nayar challenged both 2003 RPA amendments, arguing that dropping state domicile violated **federalism** (a Basic Structure feature) and that the **open ballot** violated the **secrecy of voting** and `Article 19(1)(a)` free expression.
> - **Ratio:** A unanimous five-judge Bench upheld **both amendments**. On domicile, the Court held that the Constitution itself (`Article 84`) does not require state residence for Rajya Sabha MPs—the word "representative" in `Article 80(4)` refers to electors representing the State choosing someone to speak for the State, not residential origin. On open ballot, the Court held that **secrecy of ballot is a statutory right, not an absolute constitutional mandate** in indirect elections, and open voting was a legitimate check against rampant horse-trading and corruption.
> - **Nuance on Anti-Defection:** The Court clarified that while an MLA who cross-votes openly can face **disciplinary action or expulsion by their party**, cross-voting in a Rajya Sabha election **does NOT automatically trigger disqualification under Paragraph 2(1)(b) of the Tenth Schedule** (since voting in an election outside House proceedings is distinct from House voting on legislative business)—though broader conduct abandoning the party can still be scrutinised under Paragraph 2(1)(a).

A further landmark clarification came in ***Shailesh Manubhai Parmar v. Election Commission of India* (August 2018)**, where a three-judge Bench led by CJI Dipak Misra struck down the ECI's circular extending the **"None of the Above" (NOTA)** option to Rajya Sabha elections. Distinguishing *PUCL v. Union of India* (2013)—which introduced NOTA for **direct** Lok Sabha and Assembly elections—the Court ruled that NOTA has no place in **indirect, PR-STV, open-ballot** elections like the Rajya Sabha, because permitting NOTA would nullify the value of a single transferable vote, encourage cross-voting and defection, and subvert party discipline in proportional polls.

#### Presidential Nominations (`Article 80(3)`)

Under **`Article 80(3)`**, the President nominates **12 members** to the Rajya Sabha from persons having **special knowledge or practical experience** in respect of **four specific fields**:

1. **Literature**;
2. **Science**;
3. **Art**; and
4. **Social service**.

The constitutional rationale is to give eminent non-partisan voices—poets, scientists, jurists, artists, sportspersons (interpreted broadly under art/social service, e.g., Sachin Tendulkar, P.T. Usha, Mary Kom), and social workers—direct access to the parliamentary chamber without forcing them through electoral campaigning. In sharp contrast, the **US Senate** has **no nominated members** at all.

> [!prelims]
> **Three High-Frequency Rajya Sabha Prelims Traps:**
> 1. **Four Fields Only — NO "Cooperative Movement":** `Article 80(3)` enumerates ONLY **four fields** (**literature, science, art, social service**). The fifth field—**"cooperative movement"**—appears ONLY in **`Article 171(5)`** for Governor's nominations to **State Legislative Councils (MLCs)**, NEVER for the Rajya Sabha!
> 2. **NOTA Is Barred:** Following ***Shailesh Manubhai Parmar* (2018)**, **NOTA does NOT apply** to Rajya Sabha polls (nor Presidential/Vice-Presidential polls).
> 3. **Open Ballot Nuance:** Open ballot binds **party MLAs** (who must show their ballot only to their authorised party agent); **independent MLAs** must NOT show their ballot to anyone, or their vote becomes void. Also, **nominated MPs of Rajya Sabha** do NOT vote in the Presidential election (`Art. 54`), though they **DO** vote in the **Vice-Presidential election (`Art. 66`)** and in **Presidential impeachment (`Art. 61`)**!

### Lok Sabha: Composition, Direct Election & Representation (Article 81)

The **House of the People (Lok Sabha)** is the directly elected popular chamber representing the people of India as a whole. Under **`Article 81`**, its **maximum strength is 550 members**:

- Not more than **530 members** chosen by direct election from territorial constituencies in the **States**; and
- Not more than **20 members** chosen to represent the **Union Territories** in such manner as Parliament may by law provide.

Originally, the Constitution fixed the maximum strength at 500 (raised to 525 by the `7th Amendment, 1956` and to 545 by the `31st Amendment, 1973`, then 552 under the Goa, Daman and Diu Reorganisation Act, 1987—530 States + 20 UTs + 2 Anglo-Indians). Crucially, the **`104th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019`** (effective **25 January 2020**) **discontinued the provision for nominating up to 2 members of the Anglo-Indian community** under **`Article 331`** (and similarly ended the 1 Anglo-Indian nomination to State Assemblies under `Article 333`) by not extending the 70-year sunset period under `Article 334`. Thus, the Lok Sabha's constitutional ceiling dropped permanently from **552 to 550**, and the House today has **zero nominated members**.

At present, the **current strength of the Lok Sabha is 543 elected members**:

- **524 members** representing India's **28 States** (largest: Uttar Pradesh **80**, Maharashtra **48**, West Bengal **42**, Bihar **40**, Tamil Nadu **39**; smallest: Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim **1** each; Goa, Arunachal Pradesh, Manipur, Meghalaya, Tripura **2** each); and
- **19 members** representing India's **8 Union Territories**: **NCT of Delhi (7)**, **Jammu and Kashmir (5)**, **Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu (2)**, and **1 seat each** for **Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Chandigarh, Ladakh, Lakshadweep, and Puducherry** (`7 + 5 + 2 + 5 = 19`).

To implement `Article 81(1)(b)`, Parliament enacted the **Union Territories (Direct Election to the House of the People) Act, 1965**, placing UT elections on the same **direct election** footing as State constituencies.

#### Universal Adult Suffrage (`Article 326`) & Territorial Representation (FPTP)

Every Lok Sabha constituency is a **single-member territorial constituency** elected by **universal adult suffrage** under **`Article 326`**: every Indian citizen not less than **18 years of age** (reduced from **21 years to 18 years** by the **`61st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1988`**, effective March 1989) who is not disqualified on grounds of non-residence, unsoundness of mind, crime, or corrupt/illegal practice is entitled to be registered as a voter—without discrimination of caste, religion, race, sex, literacy, or property.

Elections follow the **First-Past-the-Post (FPTP)** / simple plurality system: the candidate securing the highest number of valid votes in a constituency wins, even without crossing 50% of votes polled. The Constituent Assembly consciously **rejected Proportional Representation (PR)** for the Lok Sabha (while adopting PR-STV for the President, Vice-President, Rajya Sabha, and State Councils) for **five pragmatic reasons**:

1. **Simplicity & Mass Literacy:** In 1950, with mass illiteracy across a vast electorate, ranking multiple preferences on PR ballots would have led to massive ballot invalidation.
2. **Government Stability:** Pure PR multiplies splinter parties and produces fragile, unstable coalition ministries (as witnessed in Weimar Germany or post-war Italy), whereas FPTP tends to manufacture working majorities.
3. **Direct Constituency Accountability:** FPTP builds an unmistakable link between voters of a defined geographical constituency and a single accountable MP—something party-list PR erodes by shifting loyalty from voters to party bosses.
4. **Absence of By-Elections & Communal Fragmentation:** PR-STV does not accommodate single-seat by-elections cleanly and risked hardening caste, linguistic, and communal vote-banks into permanent micro-parties.
5. **Minority Protection via Reserved Constituencies:** Instead of separate electorates or PR, the Assembly safeguarded Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes through **territorial reserved seats with joint electorates** (`Article 330`).

#### Twin Principles of Uniformity (`Article 81(2)`)

To ensure that every Indian citizen's vote carries roughly equal democratic weight across the republic, **`Article 81(2)`** mandates two rules of territorial uniformity:

1. **Inter-State Uniformity (`Article 81(2)(a)`):** Each State shall be allotted Lok Sabha seats in such manner that the **ratio between the number of seats and the population of the State** is, so far as practicable, the **same for all States**. *Crucial Prelims Exception:* By a proviso inserted by the **`31st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1973`**, this inter-state uniformity rule **does NOT apply to States having a population of less than 60 lakh (6 million)**—preventing small states like Goa, Sikkim, or Northeastern states from losing representation.
2. **Intra-State Uniformity (`Article 81(2)(b)`):** Each State shall be divided into territorial constituencies so that the **ratio between the population of each constituency and the number of seats allotted to it** is, so far as practicable, the **same throughout the State**.

### Delimitation, Constituency Freeze & Seat Reservations (Articles 82, 330–334A)

#### Delimitation Commissions & Constitutional Freezes (`Article 82`)

Under **`Article 82`**, upon the completion of every decennial census, Parliament must provide by law for the **readjustment** of (i) the allocation of Lok Sabha seats among the States and (ii) the division of each State into territorial constituencies, carried out by an independent **Delimitation Commission**. Parliament has enacted four Delimitation Commission Acts—in **1952, 1962, 1972, and 2002** (the fourth headed by retired Supreme Court Judge Justice Kuldip Singh, whose 2008 order governed the 2009, 2014, 2019, and 2024 general elections; additionally, a separate **Jammu & Kashmir Delimitation Commission** chaired by Justice Ranjana Prakash Desai redrew J&K's Assembly and parliamentary constituencies in May 2022 under the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019). Under **`Article 329(a)`**, the orders of a Delimitation Commission have the **force of law and cannot be called in question in any court**—neither can Parliament modify them once notified.

However, unadjusted decennial reallocation soon collided with India's uneven demographic transition. During the 1970s, southern states (Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka) and some smaller states aggressively achieved population stabilisation, while northern Hindi-belt states continued rapid demographic growth. Reallocating Lok Sabha seats every ten years would have **penalised states that succeeded in family planning** by cutting their parliamentary strength while rewarding states with higher fertility rates. To neutralize this perverse incentive, Parliament enacted **three pivotal constitutional amendments**:

- **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`:** Froze the total allocation of Lok Sabha seats to States and the division of each State into constituencies at the **1971 Census** level until the year **2000**.
- **`84th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2001`:** Extended the freeze on **inter-state seat totals** at the **1971 Census** for another 25 years—specifically until the figures of the **first census taken after the year 2026** are published. Simultaneously, it allowed **internal rationalisation** of constituencies within each State (without changing the State's total seat count) based initially on the **1991 Census**.
- **`87th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003`:** Modified the base year for **internal constituency redrawing** (and SC/ST seat recalibration within States) from the 1991 Census to the **2001 Census**, while keeping the **1971 Census freeze** on State-wise total Lok Sabha seats intact.

#### SC/ST Seat Reservations (`Articles 330 & 334`)

Although the Constitution firmly abolished British-era **separate electorates** (where only members of a community voted for their community's candidate), **`Article 330`** preserves **reserved constituencies** in the Lok Sabha for **Scheduled Castes (SCs)** and **Scheduled Tribes (STs)** in proportion to their population in each State/UT. In a reserved constituency, only an SC or ST candidate may contest, **but the entire electorate of the constituency votes jointly**—and conversely, SC/ST candidates remain completely free to contest any unreserved (general) constituency as well.

Following the 2002–2008 Delimitation (on the 2001 Census), **131 out of 543 Lok Sabha seats** are reserved: **84 seats for SCs** (up from 79) and **47 seats for STs** (up from 41). Originally enacted under `Article 334` for ten years (until 1960), these reservations have been extended every decade—most recently by the **`104th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019`** for ten more years until **25 January 2030**. Notably, **there is NO SC/ST or women's seat reservation in the Rajya Sabha** or in State Legislative Councils.

#### Women's Reservation: The 106th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2023

After nearly three decades of stalled legislative efforts (beginning with the 81st Amendment Bill in 1996 and the 108th Amendment Bill passed by Rajya Sabha in 2010), Parliament passed the historic **`106th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2023`**—popularly titled the ***Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam*** (enacted September 2023)—to shatter persistent under-representation of women in Indian legislatures (women comprised just **74 of 543 MPs—13.6%**—in the 18th Lok Sabha elected in 2024). Its key constitutional provisions are:

- **New Articles Inserted:** **`Article 330A`** (Lok Sabha), **`Article 332A`** (State Legislative Assemblies), **`Article 334A`** (commencement, 15-year sunset, and rotation), alongside an amendment to **`Article 239AA`** (Legislative Assembly of NCT of Delhi).
- **One-Third Quota:** Reserves **as nearly as may be one-third (33%)** of all directly elected seats in the **Lok Sabha**, **State Legislative Assemblies**, and the **Delhi Assembly** for women.
- **Horizontal Sub-Quota:** Within seats already reserved for **SCs and STs**, one-third are sub-reserved for SC/ST women. There is **no separate sub-quota for OBC women** under the Act.
- **Exclusions:** Applies **ONLY to directly elected Lower Houses**—**NOT** to the **Rajya Sabha** or **State Legislative Councils**.
- **Sunset & Rotation (`Article 334A`):** Remains in effect for **15 years** from commencement (extendable by Parliament by law), with reserved constituencies **rotating after each subsequent delimitation** exercise as Parliament may prescribe by law.
- **Delimitation Trigger (`Article 334A(1)`):** Expressly stipulates that women's reservation shall take effect **only after a delimitation exercise** is undertaken based on the **first census conducted after the commencement of the Act**.

> [!news]
> **Delimitation, Women's Quota & One Nation, One Election (Verified Status — November 2026)**
> - **106th Amendment Notified (16 April 2026):** The Union Government formally notified the commencement of the **106th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2023** on **16 April 2026**. However, because **`Article 334A`** links activation to delimitation following the first census after commencement, actual seat reservation awaits **Census 2027** (House Listing commenced **1 April 2026**; Population Enumeration scheduled for **February 2027** with reference date **1 March 2027**).
> - **131st Constitutional Amendment Bill, 2026 Defeated (17 April 2026):** Introduced on **16 April 2026** in the Lok Sabha to lift the 1971 freeze early, conduct delimitation on the **2011 Census**, expand the Lok Sabha ceiling to **850 seats** (**815 States + 35 UTs**), and fast-track women's reservation before 2029, the Bill **failed to cross Article 368's two-thirds threshold** in a recorded Lok Sabha division on **17 April 2026**, polling **298 Ayes to 230 Noes** (out of 528 present and voting—short of the 352 needed) amid fierce Southern and Northeastern federal opposition. Companion Delimitation and UT Laws Amendment Bills were consequently withdrawn.
> - **129th Constitutional Amendment Bill, 2024 ("One Nation, One Election"):** Introduced on **17 December 2024** (proposing a new **`Article 82A`** and amending **`Articles 83, 172, and 327`** so that mid-term dissolved Houses serve only the **unexpired term** to preserve simultaneous Lok Sabha and State Assembly polls), the Bill and the companion Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill remain under scrutiny before a **41-member Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC)** chaired by **P.P. Chaudhary**, whose reporting deadline was extended until the **last week of Winter Session 2026**.

> [!debate]
> **The Post-2026 Delimitation Dilemma: "One Person, One Vote" vs Federal Equity**
> - **The Democratic-Equality Argument (Northern States):** Since the 1971 freeze, severe representation distortions have emerged: today, an MP in Uttar Pradesh or Bihar represents roughly **25–30 lakh citizens**, whereas an MP in Tamil Nadu or Kerala represents around **16–18 lakh citizens**. Allowing `Article 81(2)` to remain frozen indefinitely violates the democratic axiom of equal vote value.
> - **The Federal-Equity Argument (Southern & Smaller States):** Strict population-based reallocation after Census 2027 could slash the five southern states' share of Lok Sabha seats from ~24% to under 19% while lifting Hindi-belt states sharply—effectively **punishing states that achieved replacement-level fertility, higher literacy, and stronger fiscal contributions** to the national exchequer.
> - **Constructive Middle Paths (Mains Way Forward):** Scholars (Milan Vaishnav, Yogendra Yadav) and policy analysts suggest three federal compromises: (1) **expand the Lok Sabha size** (e.g., to ~750–850 seats) so that **no state loses absolute seat numbers** while faster-growing states gain proportionally—or pair proportional LS expansion with **upgrading the Rajya Sabha into an equal-representation federal chamber**; (2) **extend the 1971 freeze** further while decoupling the **106th Amendment's women's reservation** from delimitation; and (3) **devolve stronger fiscal and legislative autonomy** to states and urban/rural local bodies so national parliamentary arithmetic is less zero-sum.

### Duration of Both Houses & Comparative Architecture (Article 83)

**`Article 83`** establishes the lifespans of both chambers, creating a deliberate institutional contrast between an **unbroken permanent Upper House** and a **periodically renewed Lower House**:

#### Duration of the Rajya Sabha (`Article 83(1)`)

The Rajya Sabha (first constituted on **3 April 1952**) is a **continuing permanent body** and is **not subject to dissolution**. Instead, **one-third of its members retire every second year** ("as nearly as may be"), and their seats are filled by biennial elections and presidential nominations at the beginning of every third year. Retiring members are **eligible for re-election and renomination any number of times**.

A classic UPSC Prelims trap lies in the **legal source of a Rajya Sabha MP's six-year term**: **the Constitution does NOT fix the term of office of Rajya Sabha members!** `Article 83(1)` merely provides for staggered retirement of one-third of members every two years *"in accordance with provisions made in that behalf by Parliament by law."* Exercising this authority, Parliament fixed the normal term at **six years** under **Section 154 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951**. In 1952, to stagger the initial batch of members whose terms began together, the President issued the *Council of States (Term of Office of Members) Order, 1952*, using a **lottery system** to determine which one-third would retire after two years (1954) and which one-third after four years (1956). A member elected in a **by-election** to fill a casual vacancy serves only the **remainder of the predecessor's unexpired term**.

#### Duration of the Lok Sabha (`Article 83(2)`)

Unlike the Rajya Sabha, the Lok Sabha is **not a continuing chamber**. Its normal term is **five years from the date appointed for its first meeting** (NOT from the date of polling or declaration of election results!), after which it automatically dissolves. However, the President is empowered to **dissolve the Lok Sabha earlier** (ordinarily acting on the aid and advice of a Council of Ministers enjoying majority confidence, or exercising situational discretion when no stable alternative government is viable); such dissolution is irrevocable.

During a **Proclamation of National Emergency (`Article 352`)**, the proviso to **`Article 83(2)`** empowers **Parliament by law** to extend the term of the Lok Sabha beyond five years for a period **not exceeding one year at a time** (for any number of times while the Emergency operates), **provided that such extension cannot continue in any case beyond a period of six months after the Proclamation has ceased to operate**. Historically, the **5th Lok Sabha (1971–1977)** was extended twice by one year each during the 1975–77 Emergency (though dissolved in January 1977 before the second extension ran out). Remember also that the `42nd Amendment (1976)` briefly raised the normal term of the Lok Sabha (and State Assemblies) to **six years**, before the `44th Amendment (1978)` restored it to **five years**.

## 2. Membership: Qualifications, Disqualifications, Vacation & Oath (Articles 84, 88, 99, 101–104)

Representative democracy demands not only free elections but also clear constitutional and statutory filters determining **who may enter Parliament**, **what conduct terminates membership**, **how competing seats are reconciled**, and **under what oath and rules lawmakers operate**. The Constitution sets out the baseline standards in **`Articles 84, 88, 99, and 101–104`**, deliberately leaving detailed electoral qualifications and disqualifications to parliamentary legislation—chief among which is the **Representation of the People Act (RPA), 1951**.

### Qualifications for Membership (Article 84 & RPA, 1951)

#### Constitutional Qualifications (`Article 84`)

A person is qualified to be chosen to fill a seat in Parliament only if they satisfy **four constitutional conditions**:

1. **Citizenship:** Must be a **citizen of India** (whether by birth or naturalisation—unlike the US Presidency, India makes no distinction between natural-born and naturalised citizens for Parliament or even the Presidency).
2. **Oath or Affirmation Before Nomination:** Must make and subscribe before a person authorised by the Election Commission of India (typically the Returning Officer) an oath or affirmation according to the form set out in the **Third Schedule** (bearing true faith and allegiance to the Constitution and upholding the **sovereignty and integrity of India**—words inserted by the `16th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963`).
3. **Minimum Age:** Must be not less than **30 years of age** for the **Rajya Sabha**, and not less than **25 years of age** for the **Lok Sabha**. (Recall the full constitutional age ladder: 21 years for Panchayats/Municipalities, 25 for LS/Vidhan Sabha, 30 for RS/Vidhan Parishad, 35 for President/VP/Governor.)
4. **Statutory Qualifications (`Article 84(c)`):** Must possess such other qualifications as may be prescribed in that behalf by or under any law made by Parliament.

#### Statutory Qualifications Under the Representation of the People Act, 1951

Exercising `Article 84(c)`, Parliament prescribed the following additional qualifications under the **RPA, 1951**:

- **Elector Registration (Both Houses):** Must be **registered as an elector for a parliamentary constituency** anywhere in India. For the Lok Sabha, this has always allowed a citizen registered in one State to contest from any other State (for instance, contesting from Wayanad or Varanasi). For the **Rajya Sabha**, the original requirement of being an elector in the *particular State* was **abolished in 2003**—upheld in *Kuldip Nayar* (2006)—putting both Houses on the exact same national elector rule. *(Contrast with State Legislatures: to contest an MLA or MLC seat, a candidate MUST be registered as an elector in THAT State!)*
- **Reserved Constituencies:** To contest a seat reserved for **Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes**, the candidate must be a member of an SC or ST in any State or Union Territory (with specific local-tribe residence requirements for ST seats in the **autonomous districts of Assam**, **Lakshadweep**, and **Sikkim**). Crucially, however, an SC or ST candidate is **fully eligible to contest any unreserved (general) seat** across India.

### Disqualifications: Constitutional, Statutory & Anti-Defection (Articles 102–103)

Disqualifications from being chosen as, and for being, a Member of Parliament flow from **three distinct legal streams**: (1) **Constitutional grounds** under `Article 102(1)(a)–(d)`; (2) **Statutory grounds** enacted by Parliament under `Article 102(1)(e)` (chiefly the RPA, 1951); and (3) **Anti-Defection disqualification** under `Article 102(2)` read with the **Tenth Schedule**.

#### 1. Constitutional Disqualifications (`Article 102(1)`)

A person stands disqualified for being chosen as, and for being, a member of either House of Parliament if they:

- **`Article 102(1)(a)` — Hold an Office of Profit:** Hold any **"office of profit"** under the Government of India or the Government of any State, *other than* an office declared by Parliament by law not to disqualify its holder.
- **`Article 102(1)(b)` — Unsound Mind:** Are of unsound mind and **stand so declared by a competent court** (a mere medical opinion without a judicial declaration does not disqualify).
- **`Article 102(1)(c)` — Undischarged Insolvent:** Are an undischarged insolvent.
- **`Article 102(1)(d)` — Non-Citizenship / Foreign Allegiance:** Are not a citizen of India, or have **voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign State**, or are under any acknowledgement of allegiance or adherence to a foreign State.
- **`Article 102(1)(e)` — Statutory Disqualification:** Are so disqualified by or under any law made by Parliament.

#### Deep Dive: The "Office of Profit" Doctrine

Few Prelims concepts are tested more subtly than **"office of profit"**. Rooted in the British Act of Settlement (1701) and the principle of **separation of powers**, the bar prevents the Executive from buying legislative loyalty by bestowing lucrative government sinecures, boards, or administrative posts upon sitting MPs, which would compromise their independence in holding ministers accountable.

Crucially, **the Constitution does NOT define the expression "office of profit"**—nor does the RPA, 1951. Its meaning has been crystallised through three pillars:

1. **Constitutional Exemption (`Explanation to Article 102(1)`):** A person shall **NOT** be deemed to hold an office of profit merely by reason of being a **Minister either for the Union or for any State** (and similarly, the President, Vice-President, and Governors are constitutionally insulated via `Articles 58, 66, 158` when contesting presidential polls, though sitting MPs vacate their seats upon taking those offices).
2. **Statutory Exemption (`Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Act, 1959`):** Parliament enacted this comprehensive statute—repeatedly amended (notably in 2006 to exempt 55+ posts including the Chairperson of the National Advisory Council)—listing dozens of offices (Leader of Opposition, Chief Whips, Chairpersons of statutory commissions, vice-chancellors of specified universities, etc.) that do **not** disqualify MPs. A permanent **Joint Committee on Offices of Profit** (15 MPs: 10 LS + 5 RS) continuously reviews which newly created bodies should be exempted.
3. **Judicial Tests (*Shivamurthy Swami* 1971 & *Jaya Bachchan* 2006):** In ***Shivamurthy Swami Inamdar v. Agadi Sanganna Andanappa* (1971)**, the Supreme Court laid down **five functional tests** to determine whether a post is under the government: (i) whether the government makes the appointment; (ii) whether the government has the right to remove or dismiss the holder; (iii) whether the government pays the remuneration; (iv) what functions the holder performs (whether for the government); and (v) whether the government exercises control over the performance of those functions. Later, in ***Jaya Bachchan v. Union of India* (2006)**—upholding the disqualification of MP Jaya Bachchan for chairing the Uttar Pradesh Film Development Council—the Supreme Court delivered its landmark rule: **an "office of profit" is an office *capable* of yielding a profit or pecuniary gain; it is immaterial whether the holder actually draws the salary/perks or voluntarily foregoes them!** Compensatory allowances merely reimbursing out-of-pocket expenses (travelling/daily allowance) do not constitute "profit", whereas honoraria, official bungalows, staff cars, or fees attached to the office do.

#### 2. Statutory Disqualifications Under the Representation of the People Act, 1951

Under **`Article 102(1)(e)`**, Parliament enumerated comprehensive statutory disqualifications in **Sections 8 to 11A of the RPA, 1951**:

1. **Corrupt Electoral Practices (`Section 8A`):** Found guilty of corrupt practices (bribery, undue influence, booth capturing, hate appeals on religion/caste) by a High Court/Supreme Court in an election petition—disqualified for up to **6 years** as determined by the President on ECI opinion.
2. **Criminal Convictions (`Section 8`):**
   - **`Section 8(1)` (Specified Serious Offences):** Conviction (even with a fine alone or any imprisonment) for listed offences—including promoting enmity between groups, bribery, rape, cruelty to women, offences under the **Protection of Civil Rights Act (untouchability)**, **Dowry Prohibition Act**, **Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act**, **UAPA**, **NDPS Act**, **Prevention of Corruption Act**, **FERA/FEMA**, or insulting the National Flag/Constitution—triggers disqualification for **6 years** (from fine) or **sentence + 6 years** (from release).
   - **`Section 8(2)` (Socio-Economic Hoarding/Adulteration Offences):** Conviction with **at least 6 months' imprisonment** for hoarding, profiteering, food/drug adulteration, or dowry crimes triggers disqualification for **sentence + 6 years**.
   - **`Section 8(3)` (General Two-Year Conviction Rule):** Conviction for **any other offence** resulting in imprisonment of **not less than 2 years** triggers disqualification from the date of conviction and continues for **6 years after release**! *(Note: **Preventive detention** under a law does **NOT** trigger disqualification under Section 8!)*
3. **Failure to Lodge Election Expense Accounts (`Section 10A`):** Failing to file accounts of election expenses within the prescribed time and manner without good reason—disqualified by the ECI for **3 years**.
4. **Subsisting Government Contracts (`Section 9A`):** Having a subsisting personal/business interest in a contract with the appropriate government for the supply of goods or execution of works.
5. **Office in Government Corporations (`Section 10`):** Serving as a managing agent, manager, or secretary of any company or corporation (other than a cooperative society) in which the government holds **at least 25% share**.
6. **Dismissal for Corruption or Disloyalty (`Section 9`):** Dismissed from government service for corruption or disloyalty to the State—disqualified for **5 years** from dismissal.

> [!case]
> ***Lily Thomas v. Union of India* (10 July 2013) & *Lok Prahari v. ECI* (2018) — Immediate Disqualification on Conviction**
> - **The Old Shield (`Section 8(4)`, RPA 1951):** Previously, `Section 8(4)` gave *sitting* MPs and MLAs a three-month grace period after a criminal conviction—and allowed them to remain in office indefinitely if they filed an appeal during those three months—whereas an ordinary citizen with an identical conviction stood immediately barred from contesting!
> - **Holding in *Lily Thomas* (2013):** A two-judge Bench (Justices A.K. Patnaik and S.J. Mukhopadhaya) declared **`Section 8(4)` ultra vires the Constitution**, ruling that `Article 102(1)(e)` mandates uniform disqualifications for *"being chosen as, and for being"* an MP; Parliament had no competence to defer disqualification for sitting legislators. Consequently, **a 2-year conviction triggers IMMEDIATE disqualification** from the moment the trial court pronounces sentence.
> - **Clarification in *Lok Prahari v. ECI* (2018):** A three-judge Bench clarified that merely obtaining **bail or a stay on the *sentence*** does NOT lift disqualification—only an appellate court's explicit **stay on the *conviction* itself** suspends disqualification and restores membership (illustrated vividly in August 2023 when Congress leader **Rahul Gandhi's** Lok Sabha membership—lost in March 2023 after a 2-year criminal defamation conviction—was restored once the Supreme Court stayed his conviction).

#### 3. Disqualification on Ground of Defection (`Article 102(2)` & Tenth Schedule)

Inserted by the **`52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985`**, `Article 102(2)` makes a member disqualified under the **Tenth Schedule** (voluntarily giving up party membership, defying a party whip uncondoned within 15 days, an independent MP joining any party, or a nominated MP joining a party after six months). The full Tenth Schedule architecture, judicial milestones (*Kihoto Hollohan*, *Keisham Meghachandra*, *Subhash Desai*, *Padi Kaushik Reddy*), and reform debates are examined exhaustively in **Section 8** of this chapter.

#### Deciding Authority Split: `Article 103` vs Tenth Schedule Paragraph 6

UPSC Prelims tests the **institutional bifurcation of deciding authorities** relentlessly—most recently in **Prelims 2025**:

- **For `Article 102(1)` & RPA 1951 Disqualifications (`Article 103`):** If any question arises as to whether a sitting MP has become subject to any disqualification under `Article 102(1)` (office of profit, unsound mind, insolvency, foreign citizenship, or post-election statutory grounds), the question is referred to the **President**, whose decision is **final** (subject to judicial review for illegality). Crucially, under **`Article 103(2)`**, before giving any decision, **the President SHALL obtain the opinion of the Election Commission of India (ECI) and SHALL act according to such opinion!** *(Note: the `42nd Amendment, 1976` briefly diluted this to merely "consulting" the ECI, before the `44th Amendment, 1978` restored the original binding formula.)* Thus, the President acts on the **binding opinion of the ECI**, **NOT** on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers!
- **For Tenth Schedule Defection (`Article 102(2)` & Paragraph 6):** The question is decided by the **Chairman (in the Rajya Sabha)** or the **Speaker (in the Lok Sabha)**—**NOT** the President, **NOT** the ECI, and **NOT** the Council of Ministers! Their order is subject to judicial review as a Tribunal (*Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992).
- **For Pre-Election Disqualifications:** `Article 103` applies **ONLY to disqualifications arising *after* election** (*Election Commission v. Saka Venkata Rao*, 1953). If a candidate was already disqualified *prior* to election (e.g., underage or holding an office of profit on nomination day), the remedy is an **election petition** before the concerned **High Court** under the RPA, 1951!

> [!prelims]
> **Deciding Authority Traps (Tested in UPSC Prelims 2022 & 2025):**
> 1. **`Art. 102(1)` (Post-Election Office of Profit / Citizenship / RPA):** **President** decides, bound strictly by the **ECI's opinion** (`Art. 103`) — **NOT** Council of Ministers!
> 2. **`Art. 102(2)` (Tenth Schedule Defection):** **Speaker (LS) / Chairman (RS)** decides (Para 6) — **NEVER** the President!
> 3. **Pre-Election Disqualification:** Decided by the **High Court** via an election petition under RPA 1951 (`Art. 329(b)`), NOT by the President under `Art. 103`!
> 4. **Preventive Detention Trap:** Detention under a **preventive detention law** (like NSA) is **NOT** a disqualification under `Section 8` of the RPA, 1951—only *conviction* by a criminal court disqualifies.

### Vacation of Seats (Article 101)

Once elected or nominated, a Member of Parliament vacates their seat in **five constitutionally and statutorily enumerated situations**:

#### 1. Double Membership (`Article 101(1)–(2)`, RPA 1951 & 1950 Rules)

No person can simultaneously be a member of both Houses of Parliament, nor of Parliament and a State Legislature, nor hold two seats in the same House. Because candidates routinely contest multiple seats or transition between legislatures, four precise statutory deadlines govern automatic vacation:

| Double-Membership Scenario | Legal Basis | Deadline to Choose | Default Seat That Lapses If No Choice Made |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Elected to BOTH Houses of Parliament** (LS & RS simultaneously) | `Art. 101(1)` + **RPA 1951, s. 68** | **10 days** | **Rajya Sabha seat** becomes vacant |
| **Sitting MP of one House elected to the OTHER House** | **RPA 1951, s. 69** | **Immediate** (no choice window) | **Seat in the FIRST (old) House** vacates immediately |
| **Elected to TWO seats in the SAME House** (e.g., 2 LS constituencies) | **RPA 1951, s. 70** + Conduct of Election Rules | **14 days** | **BOTH seats** become vacant! |
| **Elected to BOTH Parliament AND a State Legislature** | `Art. 101(2)` + **Prohibition of Simultaneous Membership Rules, 1950** | **14 days** | **Parliament seat** becomes vacant! |

*(Note: Under `Section 33(7)` of the RPA, 1951, inserted in 1996, a candidate can contest from a **maximum of two constituencies** in a single general or by-election. Both the ECI and the Law Commission's 255th Report have repeatedly recommended restricting candidates to one constituency—or requiring winners who vacate the second seat to bear the by-election cost.)*

> [!remember]
> **Fail-Safe Mnemonic for the Four Double-Membership Deadlines:**
> - **10-RS:** Elected to both Houses simultaneously → **10 days**, else **Rajya Sabha** lapses.
> - **0-Old:** Sitting MP wins seat in other House → **Instant**, **old House** lapses.
> - **14-Both:** Two seats in the *same* House → **14 days**, else **BOTH** lapse!
> - **14-Parl:** Parliament + State Legislature → **14 days**, else **Parliament** lapses! (Counter-intuitive UPSC favourite: if an MLA wins a Lok Sabha seat and forgets to resign their Assembly seat within 14 days, they lose their **Lok Sabha seat**, NOT their Assembly seat!)

#### 2. Disqualification (`Article 101(3)(a)`)

If a sitting MP becomes subject to any constitutional or statutory disqualification under `Article 102(1)` or Tenth Schedule defection under `Article 102(2)`, their seat becomes vacant upon the authoritative decision (or immediately upon a 2-year criminal conviction under *Lily Thomas*, 2013).

#### 3. Resignation (`Article 101(3)(b)` & 33rd Amendment, 1974)

A member may resign their seat at any time by writing under their hand addressed to the **Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** or the **Speaker of the Lok Sabha**, as the case may be. Originally, a resignation took effect automatically upon receipt. However, during the 1974 Gujarat Navnirman agitation, legislators complained of being coerced into signing resignation letters under mob intimidation. Parliament responded with the **`33rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1974`**, inserting a crucial proviso to `Article 101(3)(b)` (and `Article 190(3)(b)` for States): **if from information received or otherwise, and after making such inquiry as they think fit, the Chairman or Speaker is satisfied that such resignation is NOT voluntary or genuine, they shall NOT accept the resignation!** Once accepted, a resignation takes immediate effect and cannot be withdrawn.

#### 4. Absence Without Permission (`Article 101(4)`)

A House **may** declare the seat of a member vacant if the member remains **absent from all meetings of the House for a period of 60 days without the House's permission**. Two precision Prelims points govern this clause:

- **Discretionary, Not Automatic:** Notice the word **"may"**—the seat does not automatically lapse on the 61st day; the House must pass a formal motion declaring it vacant (in the Lok Sabha, leave applications are first vetted by the **Committee on Absence of Members from the Sittings of the House**).
- **Computation Rule:** In computing the 60-day period, **no account shall be taken of any period during which the House is prorogued or is adjourned for more than four consecutive days**. (Adjournments of 1, 2, 3, or 4 consecutive days *do* count inside the 60 days; adjournments of 5 or more consecutive days are excluded!)

#### 5. Other Grounds of Vacation

A member's seat also falls vacant if: (i) their **election is declared void** by a High Court (or Supreme Court in appeal) under the RPA, 1951; (ii) they are **expelled by the House** for breach of privilege, contempt, or gross unethical conduct (upheld as a valid power distinct from `Articles 101–102` in ***Raja Ram Pal v. Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha*, 2007**, and invoked in December 2023 to expel TMC MP Mahua Moitra); (iii) they are **elected to the office of President (`Article 59(1)`) or Vice-President (`Article 66(2)`)**; or (iv) they are **appointed as Governor of a State (`Article 158(1)`)**. Note an important procedural rule: if a disqualified person is nonetheless elected, the Constitution leaves the procedure for declaring that election void to Parliament—which vests exclusive jurisdiction in the **High Court** under the RPA, 1951 (with statutory appeal to the Supreme Court).

### Oath/Affirmation, Salaries, Penalties & Ministerial/AG Rights (Articles 88, 99, 104, 106)

#### Oath or Affirmation (`Article 99`) & Penalty (`Article 104`)

Before taking their seat in either House, every newly elected or nominated MP must make and subscribe an **oath or affirmation** (as set out in the **Third Schedule**) before the **President**, or some person appointed in that behalf by the President—in practice, the **Speaker pro tem** at the start of a new Lok Sabha, and thereafter the Speaker or Chairman. In the oath, the MP swears to: (i) bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution of India as by law established; (ii) uphold the **sovereignty and integrity of India** (`16th Amendment, 1963`); and (iii) faithfully discharge the duty upon which they are about to enter.

Until an MP takes the oath, they **cannot participate in House proceedings, cannot vote, are NOT eligible for parliamentary privileges and immunities, and cannot draw parliamentary allowances**. Under **`Article 104`**, if any person sits or votes as a member of either House **before taking the `Article 99` oath**, or **knowing that they are not qualified or are disqualified**, or when prohibited from doing so by parliamentary law, they incur a **penalty of ₹500 for each day** they so sit or vote, recoverable as a debt due to the Union.

#### Salaries, Allowances & Pensions (`Article 106`)

Under **`Article 106`**, Members of Parliament are entitled to receive such salaries and allowances as may from time to time be determined by **Parliament by law**. Notably, **the Constitution makes NO provision for pensions of MPs**; Parliament introduced MP pensions via an amendment in **1976** to the **Salary, Allowances and Pension of Members of Parliament Act, 1954** (subsequently revised periodically, with automatic five-year inflation-linked indexation tied to the Cost Inflation Index introduced via the Finance Act, 2018). Critics frequently point out the conflict of interest in MPs fixing their own remuneration and urge establishing an **independent Emoluments Commission** along UK lines. Meanwhile, the salaries and allowances of the **Speaker, Deputy Speaker, Chairman, and Deputy Chairman** are fixed by Parliament (under separate statutes) but are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (`Article 112(3)(b)`)**, insulating them from annual voting.

#### Rights of Ministers and the Attorney General (`Article 88`)

In a bicameral parliamentary system where the Prime Minister and Union Ministers may belong to either the Lok Sabha or the Rajya Sabha—or even be non-members for up to six months (`Article 75(5)`)—strict single-House participation rules would paralyse governance. **`Article 88`** solves this cleanly by conferring special participatory rights upon **every Union Minister** and the **Attorney General of India**:

- **Right to Speak and Participate Everywhere:** Every Union Minister and the Attorney General has the right to **speak in, and otherwise take part in the proceedings of, either House**, any **joint sitting of the Houses**, and **any committee of Parliament of which they may be named a member**.
- **Strict Limitation on Voting:** `Article 88` expressly states that **nothing in this Article entitles them to vote**. Consequently:
  1. A **Minister** who is a member of the Lok Sabha may pilot Bills and answer questions in the Rajya Sabha, but **can vote ONLY in the Lok Sabha** (and vice versa for a Rajya Sabha Minister, such as Dr. Manmohan Singh or Dr. S. Jaishankar in the Lok Sabha).
  2. A **non-MP Minister** (within the 6-month grace window under `Article 75(5)`) can speak and participate in **both Houses**, but **cannot vote in either House**.
  3. The **Attorney General of India (`Article 76`)**—the Union's chief legal advisor—enjoys the right of audience in both Houses, joint sittings, and named committees (plus full **parliamentary privileges under `Article 105(4)`**), **yet can NEVER vote** in Parliament. *(Contrast with the **Solicitor General of India**: because the Solicitor General is a statutory/non-constitutional officer, they do NOT enjoy `Article 88` rights!)*

## 3. Presiding Officers, Parliamentary Leaders, Whips & Secretariat (Articles 89–98)

Part V (`Articles 89` to `98`) builds an independent presiding architecture for each House—the **Speaker** and **Deputy Speaker** in the Lok Sabha, and the **Chairman** and **Deputy Chairman** in the Rajya Sabha—backed by Panels of Chairpersons/Vice-Chairpersons, House and Opposition Leaders, Whips, and autonomous Secretariats.

### Speaker of the Lok Sabha: Evolution, Election, Tenure & Removal (Articles 93–96)

#### Historical Evolution (1919–1952)

India's presiding offices originated under the **Government of India Act, 1919**, initially titled **President** and **Deputy President** of the Central Legislative Assembly. Appointed in **1921** were **Sir Frederick Whyte** (President) and **Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha** (Deputy President). In **1925**, **Vithalbhai J. Patel** became the **first Indian and first elected President**, championing Chair independence and establishing a separate Secretariat (1928–29). Though the **GoI Act, 1935** introduced the titles **Speaker** and **Deputy Speaker**, they took effect only in 1947. **G.V. Mavalankar**—who chaired the Central Legislative Assembly (1946–47) and Constituent Assembly (Legislative) (1947–50)—became the **first Lok Sabha Speaker (1952–56)** (*"Father of the Lok Sabha"*), with **M. Ananthasayanam Ayyangar** as first Deputy Speaker.

#### Election & Contested Contests (`Article 93`)

Under **`Article 93`**, the Lok Sabha chooses two members as **Speaker** and **Deputy Speaker** *"as soon as may be."* The **President fixes the Speaker's election date** (whereas the Speaker fixes the Deputy Speaker's date). Chosen by **simple majority**, Speakers are normally elected unopposed, with **four contested elections** since 1952:

1. **15 May 1952 (1st LS):** **G.V. Mavalankar** (INC) defeated **Shankar Shantaram More** (PWPI) (**394–55**).
2. **17 March 1967 (4th LS):** **Neelam Sanjiva Reddy** (INC) defeated **Tenneti Viswanatham** (Ind.) (**278–207**).
3. **5 January 1976 (5th LS, Emergency):** After G.S. Dhillon resigned, **Bali Ram Bhagat** (INC) defeated **Jagannathrao Joshi** (BJS) (**344–58**).
4. **26 June 2024 (18th LS):** Ending a 48-year unopposed streak, NDA nominee **Om Birla** (BJP) won re-election by **voice vote** over INDIA bloc nominee **Kodikunnil Suresh** (INC).

> "The Speaker represents the House. He represents the dignity of the House, the freedom of the House and because the House represents the nation, in a particular way, the Speaker becomes the symbol of the nation's freedom and liberty. Therefore, it is right that that should be an honoured position, a free position and should be occupied always by men of outstanding ability and impartiality." — Jawaharlal Nehru, unveiling Vithalbhai Patel's portrait, 8 March 1948

#### Tenure, Survival Beyond Dissolution & Party Non-Resignation Trap (`Article 94`)

Under the second proviso to **`Article 94`**, **dissolution of the Lok Sabha does NOT vacate the Speaker's office**—the Speaker continues until **immediately before the first meeting of the new Lok Sabha**, keeping House administration intact.

Equally critical (**UPSC Prelims 2025 trap**): **the Constitution does NOT require a Speaker to resign from their political party** upon election. Party resignation is purely a British convention; **Tenth Schedule Paragraph 5** merely offers a *voluntary* anti-defection exemption (exercised notably by **Neelam Sanjiva Reddy in 1967**).

Earlier vacation occurs in only **three cases (`Article 94`)**: (1) ceasing to be a Lok Sabha MP (`94(a)`); (2) **resigning in writing to the Deputy Speaker**—**NOT the President** (`94(b)`); or (3) **removal** by an **effective majority** (majority of *all the then members*) (`94(c)`).

#### Procedure for Removal (`Articles 94(c) & 96`)

- **14 Days' Notice & 50-MP Support:** Requires at least **14 days' advance notice** (`Art. 94` proviso) and **50 MPs' support** for leave to move under Lok Sabha Rules.
- **Voting Rights (`Article 96` — Prelims 2024 Trap):** While a removal resolution is under consideration, the Speaker **cannot preside**, retains the **right to speak and participate**, and under **`Article 96(2)`** **votes ONLY in the first instance—NEVER a casting vote!**

> [!news]
> **Removal Motion Against Speaker Om Birla (11 March 2026) & Vacant Deputy Speaker Crisis**
> Signed by **118 Opposition MPs** (only the fourth Speaker removal motion ever debated, after 1954, 1966, and 1987), the resolution against **Om Birla** was **defeated by voice vote on 11 March 2026** after 13 hours of debate. With the **Deputy Speaker post vacant since May 2019**, BJP MP **Jagdambika Pal** (Panel of Chairpersons) presided—exposing procedural gaps when `Article 93`'s second chair stays unfilled.

### Powers, Functions & Pillars of Independence of the Speaker

As the supreme guardian of the rights and privileges of the House, its committees, and individual MPs, the Speaker derives authority from **three sources**: (1) the **Constitution of India**, (2) the **Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business of Lok Sabha**, and (3) **Parliamentary Conventions** (including Directions by the Speaker).

#### Key Constitutional & Procedural Powers

1. **Order, Decorum & Discipline:** Maintains order and decorum for conducting business and regulating proceedings; possesses final authority to order expunction of unparliamentary remarks, direct withdrawal of disorderly MPs (`LS Rule 373`), or initiate/enforce suspensions (`LS Rules 374 and 374A`).
2. **Final Interpreter Inside the House:** Serves as the **final interpreter** within the House of (a) the provisions of the Constitution of India, (b) the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business, and (c) parliamentary precedents.
3. **Quorum Watchdog (`Article 100(4)`):** Adjourns the House or suspends the meeting whenever the mandatory **quorum of one-tenth (55 MPs)** is absent.
4. **Casting Vote (`Article 100(1)`):** Does **not** vote in the first instance in ordinary sittings, but exercises a **casting vote in the case of a tie (equality of votes)** to break a deadlock.
5. **Presides Over Joint Sittings (`Article 108` & `Article 118(4)`):** Presides over a joint sitting of both Houses summoned by the President to resolve deadlocks on Ordinary or Financial Bills.
6. **Secret Sitting (`LS Rule 248`):** Can allow a **secret sitting** of the House at the request of the **Leader of the House** (during which no stranger—including journalists—may remain in the chamber, lobby, or galleries except with the Speaker's permission).
7. **Money Bill Certification (`Article 110(3)–(4)`):** Decides whether a Bill is a **Money Bill**; their decision is declared constitutionally **"final"** (subject to judicial review reference pending before a seven-judge Bench in *Rojer Mathew*, 2019), and their signed certificate is endorsed on transmission to the Rajya Sabha and the President.
8. **Tenth Schedule Adjudicator:** Acts as a **constitutional Tribunal** (*Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992) deciding questions of disqualification of Lok Sabha MPs on grounds of defection under the **Tenth Schedule**.
9. **Master of Parliamentary Committees:** Appoints the **Chairpersons of ALL parliamentary committees of the Lok Sabha** (including the PAC, Estimates, COPU, and 16 DRSCs) and supervises their functioning. Directly serves as **ex-officio Chairperson** of three foundational House panels: the **Business Advisory Committee (BAC)**, the **Rules Committee**, and the **General Purposes Committee**.
10. **Inter-Parliamentary & National Representation:** Acts as the ex-officio **President of the Indian Parliamentary Group (IPG)** (linking India with the IPU and CPA), ex-officio **President of seven of the eight Parliamentary Forums**, and ex-officio **Chairman of the Conference of Presiding Officers of Legislative Bodies in India**.

#### Seven Pillars of Speaker Independence & the British Contrast

To enable fearless impartiality, the Constitution embeds **seven safeguards** protecting the Speaker's office:

1. **Security of Tenure:** Removable only by an **effective majority** after **14 days' notice** and support of 50 MPs—never by executive pleasure or simple majority.
2. **Charged Emoluments (`Article 112(3)(b)`):** Salaries and allowances are fixed by Parliament and **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**, shielding them from annual parliamentary vote.
3. **Conduct Non-Discussable:** The Speaker's work and conduct cannot be discussed or criticised in the Lok Sabha except on a **substantive motion**.
4. **Judicial Immunity (`Article 122(2)`):** Powers of regulating procedure, conducting business, or maintaining order in the House are **not subject to the jurisdiction of any court** (save substantive illegality or *mala fides*).
5. **Casting Vote Restraint:** Abstaining from first-instance voting preserves neutrality above day-to-day partisan divisions.
6. **Elevated Constitutional Status:** Placed at **Rank 6** in the **Union Order of Precedence**—jointly with the **Chief Justice of India**, placing the Speaker higher than all Union Cabinet Ministers (Rank 7) except the Prime Minister and Deputy Prime Minister.
7. **Voluntary Party-Exit Shield (`Tenth Schedule Para 5`):** Legally protects a Speaker who quits their party upon election.

Yet a glaring gap persists between constitutional design and Indian political reality. In the **United Kingdom**, the Speaker of the House of Commons **strictly resigns from their political party**, adopts the norm ***"Once a Speaker, always a Speaker,"*** is **traditionally unopposed** in subsequent general elections by major rival parties in their constituency, and is elevated to the House of Lords upon retirement. In India, Speakers retain party tickets, depend on party bosses for re-nomination, and frequently move into Cabinet posts or governorships after demitting office—fuelling persistent accusations of bias in Money Bill certification, delayed Tenth Schedule rulings, and selective floor recognition. Reform commissions—including the **V.S. Page Committee (1967)**, the **Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2007)**, and the **Law Commission (255th Report, 2015)**—have urged adopting the British uncontested-constituency convention, imposing post-retirement cooling-off bars on political appointments, and transferring anti-defection adjudication out of the Speaker's hands.

> [!prelims]
> **UPSC Prelims 2024 & 2025 Speaker Traps — Master Checklist:**
> 1. **No Mandatory Party Resignation (Prelims 2025):** The Constitution does **NOT** require a newly elected Speaker to resign from their political party!
> 2. **Survives Dissolution (Prelims 2025):** On dissolution of Lok Sabha, the Speaker stays in office until **immediately before the first meeting** of the new Lok Sabha (`Art. 94` proviso).
> 3. **First-Instance Vote During Removal (Prelims 2024):** Under `Art. 96(2)`, during their own removal resolution, the Speaker **CAN vote in the first instance**, **CANNOT cast a tie-breaking vote**, and **CANNOT preside**.
> 4. **Resignation Recipient:** Speaker resigns to the **Deputy Speaker** (and Deputy Speaker resigns to the **Speaker**)—**NEVER to the President**!
> 5. **Who Fixes Election Date?** **President** fixes the date for the **Speaker's** election; **Speaker** fixes the date for the **Deputy Speaker's** election!

### Deputy Speaker, Panel of Chairpersons & Speaker Pro Tem (Articles 93–95)

#### Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha (`Articles 93–95`)

Elected by the Lok Sabha from among its members **after the election of the Speaker** on a date **fixed by the Speaker** (`LS Rule 8`), the **Deputy Speaker** holds a vital constitutional—not merely ceremonial—office:

- **Constitutional Independence — NOT Subordinate:** The Deputy Speaker is **NOT subordinate to the Speaker**; they are **directly responsible to the House**.
- **Powers When Presiding (`Article 95`):** Whenever the Speaker's office is **vacant**, the Deputy Speaker performs all duties of the Speaker; whenever the Speaker is **absent** from a sitting, the Deputy Speaker acts as Speaker with identical powers—including exercising a **casting vote** on a tie (while remaining free to speak, participate, and vote as an ordinary MP whenever the Speaker is in the Chair!).
- **Special Committee Privilege (`LS Rule 258`):** Whenever the Deputy Speaker is appointed as a member of **any parliamentary committee**, they **automatically become its Chairperson** (for example, they traditionally chair the Committee on Private Members' Bills and Resolutions and the Library Committee).
- **Tenure & Removal:** Remains in office during the life of the Lok Sabha (note: unlike the Speaker, the Deputy Speaker does **NOT** continue beyond Lok Sabha dissolution!). Vacates earlier by ceasing to be an MP, resigning in writing to the **Speaker**, or removal by an **effective majority** after **14 days' notice** (with `Article 96` first-instance voting rights). Salaries are **charged on the CFI (`Article 112(3)(b)`)**.
- **The Unfulfilled Constitutional Mandate (2019–2026):** Up to the 10th Lok Sabha, both Speaker and Deputy Speaker typically belonged to the ruling party. From the **11th Lok Sabha (1996)** onwards, a healthy democratic convention emerged whereby the ruling coalition held the Speaker's chair and the **main Opposition party** held the Deputy Speaker's chair. That convention broke down sharply after **May 2019**: the **17th Lok Sabha (2019–2024)** became the **first full-term Lok Sabha in independent India never to elect a Deputy Speaker**, and the post **remained vacant in the 18th Lok Sabha as of November 2026**—an unprecedented seven-year constitutional gap despite pending Supreme Court public interest petitions questioning whether *"as soon as may be"* in `Article 93` permits indefinite executive non-compliance.

#### Panel of Chairpersons of the Lok Sabha (`LS Rule 9`)

Under the Rules of Lok Sabha, the Speaker nominates from amongst the members a **Panel of not more than ten Chairpersons**. Any member of the Panel can preside over the House with the full powers of the Speaker whenever **both the Speaker and the Deputy Speaker are ABSENT**. They hold office until a new Panel is nominated.

**CRITICAL PRELIMS TRAP — ABSENCE vs VACANCY:** A member of the **Panel of Chairpersons** can preside **ONLY during the ABSENCE** of the Speaker and Deputy Speaker—**NEVER when BOTH offices are VACANT!** Under **`Article 95(1)`**, if the offices of both Speaker and Deputy Speaker are **vacant**, the duties of the Speaker are performed by **such member of the Lok Sabha as the PRESIDENT may appoint for the purpose**, and elections are held as soon as possible to fill the vacant offices!

#### Speaker Pro Tem (`Article 95(1)`)

Because the outgoing Speaker vacates office immediately before the **first meeting** of a newly elected Lok Sabha, the **President appoints a member of the newly elected Lok Sabha as Speaker Pro Tem** (*pro tempore* = "for the time being") under `Article 95(1)` and **personally administers the oath of office** to them. By established parliamentary convention, the **senior-most MP** (by number of Lok Sabha terms) is chosen. The Speaker Pro Tem enjoys all powers of the Speaker for two specific temporary tasks: (1) **presiding over the first sitting and administering the `Article 99` oath/affirmation** to all newly elected MPs, and (2) **enabling the House to elect the permanent Speaker**. The instant the permanent Speaker is elected, the office of Speaker Pro Tem **automatically ceases to exist**.

In **June 2024**, the convention stirred sharp political controversy when the President appointed seven-term BJP MP **Bhartruhari Mahtab** (seven *consecutive* terms—six with BJD, seventh with BJP) as Speaker Pro Tem of the 18th Lok Sabha over eight-term Congress MP **Kodikunnil Suresh** (whose eight terms were *non-consecutive*), prompting the Opposition to question executive discretion over seniority criteria.

### Chairman, Deputy Chairman & Panel of Vice-Chairpersons of Rajya Sabha (Articles 89–92)

#### Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (`Articles 64, 89, 92`)

Under **`Articles 64` and `89(1)`**, the **Vice-President of India** (**C.P. Radhakrishnan**, sworn in on **12 September 2025**) is the **ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha**. Because the Chairman is **NOT a member of the Rajya Sabha**, their constitutional position differs from the Lok Sabha Speaker in four decisive ways:

1. **No Separate Removal Procedure:** The Chairman can be removed from the Chair **ONLY by being removed as Vice-President** under **`Article 67(b)`**—a resolution initiated exclusively in the Rajya Sabha after 14 days' notice, passed by an **effective majority of the Rajya Sabha** and **agreed to by the Lok Sabha by a simple majority**.
2. **NO Vote During Own Removal (`Article 92(2)`):** While a removal resolution is under consideration in the Rajya Sabha, the Chairman cannot preside, may speak and participate in proceedings, **but CANNOT VOTE AT ALL—neither in the first instance nor a casting vote!** (In contrast, the Lok Sabha Speaker *can* vote in the first instance under `Article 96(2)` because the Speaker is a member of the Lok Sabha!)
3. **No Money Bill or Joint Sitting Authority:** The Chairman neither certifies Money Bills (exclusive to Speaker under `Art. 110(3)`) nor presides over a Joint Sitting of Parliament (never in the `Art. 118(4)` hierarchy!).
4. **Resignation & Acting President:** The Chairman resigns by addressing the **President** (in their capacity as Vice-President). During any period when the Vice-President acts as President or discharges the functions of the President (`Article 65`), they **do not perform the duties of Chairman** and draw presidential emoluments instead of the Chairman's CFI-charged salary.

In routine sittings, however, the Chairman exercises parallel powers of order, interpretation, quorum, casting vote (`Article 100(1)`), Tenth Schedule adjudication for Rajya Sabha MPs, and appointment of chairs for the **8 RS-controlled DRSCs** and Rajya Sabha committees.

#### Deputy Chairman & Panel of Vice-Chairpersons (`Articles 89–92`)

The **Deputy Chairman** is elected by the Rajya Sabha from amongst its own members (`Article 89(2)`). Just like the Deputy Speaker in the Lok Sabha, the Deputy Chairman is **NOT subordinate to the Chairman** (directly responsible to the House), performs the Chairman's duties whenever the Chair is vacant or the Vice-President is acting as President/absent (`Article 91`), exercises a casting vote when presiding (and ordinary vote when not presiding), draws CFI-charged salary (`Article 112(3)(b)`), resigns in writing to the **Chairman**, and is removable by a **Rajya Sabha effective majority** after **14 days' notice** (retaining first-instance voting rights under `Article 92(2)`).

> [!news]

> On **17 April 2026**, **Harivansh Narayan Singh** was re-elected unopposed as **Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** for an unprecedented **third consecutive term**. Having been nominated to the Rajya Sabha by President Droupadi Murmu after his second elected term from Bihar ended on **9 April 2026**, Harivansh became the **first nominated Member of Parliament in Indian constitutional history** to hold the office of Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.

Similarly, the Rajya Sabha Chairman nominates a **Panel of Vice-Chairpersons** from among RS members to preside ONLY when both the Chairman and Deputy Chairman are **absent** (never when both offices are **vacant**, in which case the **President appoints a Rajya Sabha member** under `Article 91(1)`!).

### Parliamentary Leaders, Whips & Independent Secretariats (Article 98)

#### Leader of the House

The office of **Leader of the House** is **NOT mentioned in the Constitution**, but is defined in the **Rules of Procedure** of each House. Under Lok Sabha Rules, the Leader of the House is the **Prime Minister** (if a member of the Lok Sabha — currently **Narendra Modi**) or a Union Minister who is a member of the Lok Sabha nominated by the Prime Minister (as occurred when Rajya Sabha MP Dr. Manmohan Singh nominated Pranab Mukherjee and later Sushilkumar Shinde as Leader of the Lok Sabha). In the Rajya Sabha, the Leader of the House is a Union Minister who is a member of the Rajya Sabha nominated by the Prime Minister (currently **J.P. Nadda**). The Leader exercises direct influence over the scheduling of government business and parliamentary decorum.

#### Leader of the Opposition (LoP)

In a parliamentary democracy, the **Leader of the Opposition** embodies *"His/Her Majesty's Loyal Opposition"*—holding the government to constructive account and standing ready as an alternative Prime Minister-in-waiting. Like the Leader of the House, the **Leader of the Opposition is NOT mentioned in the Constitution**. However, after first being formally recognised in **1969** (**Dr. Ram Subhag Singh** in LS; **Shyam Nandan Mishra** in RS), Parliament accorded the office **statutory status** via the **Salary and Allowances of Leaders of Opposition in Parliament Act, 1977**, conferring the **salary, allowances, and protocol rank of a Union Cabinet Minister**.

Under the **1977 Act**, the LoP is the leader of the **largest Opposition party** having the greatest numerical strength and recognised as such by the Speaker or Chairman. By long-standing parliamentary convention—traceable to **Speaker's Direction 121(1)** (governing recognition of a "Parliamentary Party" for quorum purposes)—a party must hold **at least one-tenth (10%) of the total strength of the House** (**55 seats in Lok Sabha; 25 seats in Rajya Sabha**) to claim formal LoP recognition. Because no single Opposition party crossed 55 seats in the 16th (2014) or 17th (2019) Lok Sabhas, the Lok Sabha went **ten years without a statutory LoP** (Parliament even amended the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act in 2014 to let the leader of the *single largest Opposition party* sit on the CBI Director panel). Following the 2024 general elections, Congress MP **Rahul Gandhi** was recognised as **Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha on 9 June 2024**, while **Mallikarjun Kharge** serves as **Leader of the Opposition in the Rajya Sabha** (reappointed with effect from **26 June 2026**).

Beyond floor debate, the **LoP (Lok Sabha)** plays an indispensable statutory checks-and-balances role as an ex-officio member of high-powered selection committees for India's premier integrity institutions: **Chief Vigilance Commissioner (CVC)**, **Chief Information Commissioner (CIC)**, **Lokpal**, **CBI Director**, **Chairperson and Members of NHRC**, and **Chief Election Commissioner & Election Commissioners** (under the 2023 CEC Act). Unlike the United Kingdom—where the Opposition forms a formal **"Shadow Cabinet"** mirroring each government department—India has no institutionalised Shadow Cabinet system.

#### The Party Whip

One of the sharpest Prelims traps in Indian polity concerns the legal status of the **Whip**: **the office of Whip is mentioned NEITHER in the Constitution of India, NOR in the Rules of Procedure of either House, NOR in any parliamentary statute!** It is grounded **purely in parliamentary convention** (though the **Leaders and Chief Whips of Recognised Parties and Groups in Parliament (Facilities) Act, 1998** provides administrative facilities, and the Tenth Schedule gives *binding legal effect* to a party's "direction" on voting).

Every political party—ruling or Opposition—appoints its own **Chief Whip** (for the ruling party in India, the **Minister of Parliamentary Affairs** acts as the Government Chief Whip) assisted by deputy whips. Whips ensure quorum and attendance, gauge party mood, coordinate speaker lists, and issue binding voting directives classified by urgency into three Westminster tiers:

- **One-Line Whip:** Informs members of a vote; allows abstention if an MP chooses not to follow the party line.
- **Two-Line Whip:** Directs members to be present in the House during voting without specifying a strict mandate on how to vote.
- **Three-Line Whip:** The strictest directive—commands mandatory presence and strict voting strictly along party lines on crucial Bills, Money Bills, or No-Confidence Motions; **defying a three-line whip without prior permission triggers Tenth Schedule disqualification** unless condoned by the party within **15 days**!

#### Independent Secretariats of Parliament (`Article 98`)

Under **`Article 98`**, each House of Parliament has its own **separate, independent secretarial staff**—the **Lok Sabha Secretariat** and the **Rajya Sabha Secretariat** (though common posts for both Houses are permitted). Parliament regulates the recruitment and service conditions of staff by law, insulating parliamentary officials from Union Executive control. Each Secretariat is headed by a **Secretary-General**—a permanent, non-partisan civil servant enjoying the rank of **Cabinet Secretary**—appointed by the **Presiding Officer** (Speaker for LS; Chairman for RS) and serving as the Presiding Officer's chief procedural adviser and custodian of parliamentary records.

## 4. Sessions, Conduct of Business, Motions & Devices (Articles 85–87, 100, 118–120)

Parliament's power is only as real as the time, calendar, and procedural instruments through which lawmakers summon ministers, challenge fiscal waste, debate national crises, and test executive confidence. **`Articles 85 to 87`, `100`, and `118 to 120`**, supplemented by the detailed Rules of Procedure of both Houses, construct a rich ecosystem of sessions, floor devices, and accountability motions—including several distinctively Indian parliamentary innovations.

### Sessions, Summoning, Recess, Prorogation & Dissolution (Article 85)

#### Summoning & the Six-Month Constitutional Floor (`Article 85(1)`)

Under **`Article 85(1)`**, the **President** from time to time summons each House of Parliament to meet at such time and place as they think fit (acting on the advice of the Cabinet Committee on Parliamentary Affairs). The sole constitutional calendar mandate is the **six-month ceiling**: **six months shall NOT intervene between the last sitting of a House in one session and the date appointed for its first sitting in the next session.** Consequently:

- Parliament must meet **at least twice a year**;
- **The Constitution does NOT prescribe any minimum number of sitting days per year** (a classic Prelims 2020 trap!), nor does it mandate three sessions;
- By well-established custom, India's Parliament convenes **three sessions every year**: (1) **Budget Session** (late January/February to April/May — longest, split into two parts with a 3–4 week DRSC recess); (2) **Monsoon Session** (July to August/September); and (3) **Winter Session** (November to December — shortest).

Understand four distinct calendar terms cleanly:
- **Session:** The entire span from the first sitting of a House until its **prorogation** (or **dissolution** in the case of Lok Sabha).
- **Sitting:** A single day's meeting of a House (normally split into two halves: 11:00 AM–1:00 PM and 2:00 PM–6:00 PM).
- **Recess:** The inter-session interval between the **prorogation** of a House and its **reassembly** in a new session.
- **Lame-Duck Session:** The **final session of an existing Lok Sabha after a new Lok Sabha has already been elected**. Members of the existing Lok Sabha who failed to win re-election to the new Lok Sabha are called **"lame-ducks"**.

#### Adjournment vs Adjournment *Sine Die* vs Prorogation vs Dissolution

UPSC Prelims routinely tests fine distinctions between the four mechanisms that terminate parliamentary meetings—specifically **who exercises the power** and **what happens to pending Bills and notices**:

| Device | Constitutional / Procedural Authority | What Does It Terminate? | Effect on Pending Bills & Notices |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Adjournment** | **Presiding Officer** (Speaker / Chairman) | Only a **sitting** (for hours, days, or weeks—specifies exact time of reassembly) | **None** — all Bills, motions, and notices carry forward untouched |
| **Adjournment *sine die*** | **Presiding Officer** (Speaker / Chairman) | A **sitting indefinitely** (without naming a reassembly date); Presiding Officer can **recall the House** before President prorogues | **None** — Bills and notices stay alive until prorogation |
| **Prorogation** (`Art. 85(2)(a)`) | **President of India** (usually days after *sine die* adjournment; can even prorogue a running session—e.g., Monsoon 2026 ended *sine die* on 13 Aug 2026 without immediate prorogation) | Both the sitting AND the entire **session** | **Pending Bills do NOT lapse** (`Art. 107(3)`)! **ALL pending notices** (except notice for introducing a Bill) **lapse** and must be given afresh next session *(Note: in the **UK**, prorogation lapses pending Bills too!)* |
| **Dissolution** (`Art. 85(2)(b)`) | **President of India** (or automatic expiry of 5-year term; applies **ONLY to Lok Sabha**, NEVER Rajya Sabha) | The very **life of the Lok Sabha** (irrevocable; triggers general election) | Pending notices, motions, resolutions, petitions **lapse**; **Bills pending in LS or passed by LS & pending in RS lapse** (full 6 rules in Section 5!) |

### Quorum, Voting, Language & Presidential Address (Articles 86–87, 100, 120)

#### Quorum (`Article 100(3)–(4)`)

**Quorum** is the minimum number of members required to be present in the House before it can lawfully transact business. Under **`Article 100(3)`**, until Parliament by law otherwise provides, the quorum to constitute a meeting of either House is **one-tenth (1/10th) of the total number of members of the House—INCLUDING the Presiding Officer!** This translates to:

- **Lok Sabha:** **55 members** (1/10th of 543 rounded up / 550);
- **Rajya Sabha:** **25 members** (1/10th of 245 rounded up / 250).

Under **`Article 100(4)`**, if at any time during a meeting there is no quorum, it is the **mandatory constitutional duty of the Presiding Officer** either to **adjourn the House** or to **suspend the meeting until there is a quorum**. *(By informal parliamentary convention, the Chair does not normally check quorum during the lunch hour or during Zero Hour unless a member specifically challenges quorum!) Contrast this with **State Legislatures (`Article 189(3)`)**, where quorum is **10 members OR one-tenth of total membership, WHICHEVER IS GREATER**!*

#### Voting Mechanics (`Article 100(1)–(2)`)

Unless the Constitution explicitly mandates a special or effective majority (such as impeachment under `Art. 61`, Speaker/Chairman removal under `Arts. 67/94`, or constitutional amendment under `Art. 368`), all questions at any sitting of either House or a Joint Sitting are decided by a **simple majority of members present and voting**, excluding the Presiding Officer—who exercises a **casting vote only in the event of an equality of votes** (`Article 100(1)`). Under **`Article 100(2)`**, either House has the power to act notwithstanding any **vacancy** in its membership, and no parliamentary proceeding is invalidated merely because it is later discovered that some person who was **not entitled to do so sat or voted** or otherwise participated! Voting proceeds initially by **voice vote** (*"Ayes"* vs *"Noes"*); if any member challenges the Chair's assessment of the voice vote, a formal **Division** is held (via the Integrated Automatic Vote Recording System, paper slips, or lobby division).

#### Language in Parliament (`Article 120`)

**`Article 120`** declared **Hindi** and **English** to be the languages for transacting parliamentary business. Although `Article 120(2)` originally envisioned discontinuing English after 15 years (in 1965), Parliament enacted the **Official Languages Act, 1963**, allowing English to continue indefinitely alongside Hindi. Crucially, the proviso to `Article 120(1)` empowers the **Chairman or Speaker to permit any MP who cannot adequately express themselves in Hindi or English to address the House in their mother tongue**—supported today by simultaneous real-time interpretation facilities covering all 22 Eighth Schedule languages in the new Parliament building.

#### Ordinary Address (`Article 86`) vs Special Address & Motion of Thanks (`Article 87`)

- **`Article 86`:** Confers a discretionary right on the President to address either House or both Houses assembled together, and to send **messages** to either House with respect to a pending Bill or otherwise.
- **`Article 87` (Special Address):** Imposes a **mandatory constitutional duty** on the President to address **both Houses of Parliament assembled together** on **two occasions**:
  1. At the commencement of the **first session after each general election** to the Lok Sabha; and
  2. At the commencement of the **first session of each calendar year** (usually late January, opening the Budget Session).

Corresponding to the British *"Speech from the Throne,"* the Presidential Address is drafted entirely by the **Union Cabinet** and outlines the government's achievements of the preceding year and its policy/legislative roadmap for the coming year. Following the address, both Houses separately debate and vote upon a **"Motion of Thanks"**. This motion is a direct **test of executive survival in the Lok Sabha**: **if the Motion of Thanks is defeated (or amended against government wishes) in the Lok Sabha, it amounts to a defeat of the government**, compelling the Council of Ministers to resign! In the **Rajya Sabha**, however, passage of Opposition amendments to the Motion of Thanks (which occurred five times—in **1980, 1989, 2001, 2015, and 2016**) inflicts acute political embarrassment **without legally toppling the government**, because the Council of Ministers is responsible exclusively to the Lok Sabha (`Article 75(3)`).

### Question Hour & Zero Hour

#### Question Hour: The Sharpening Edge of Daily Accountability

The **first hour of every parliamentary sitting** (11:00 AM–12:00 Noon in the Lok Sabha; shifted since November 2014 to **12:00 Noon–1:00 PM in the Rajya Sabha** so Zero Hour runs first) is normally reserved for **Question Hour**—the premier daily instrument through which ordinary MPs compel Union Ministers to answer publicly for administrative lapses, expenditure, and policy decisions. *(Exceptions: Question Hour is normally skipped on the first day of a new Lok Sabha, on the day of the President's Special Address, and on Budget Presentation Day.)*

Parliamentary rules recognise **four distinct categories of Questions**, colour-coded traditionally by printed paper tint:

| Question Type | Paper Colour | Nature of Reply | Supplementary Questions? | Key Procedural & Prelims Nuances |
|---|---|---|---|---|

#### Zero Hour: India's Iconic Informal Innovation (1962)

Immediately following Question Hour (from 12:00 Noon in Lok Sabha; shifted to 11:00 AM in Rajya Sabha since 2014) runs **Zero Hour**—the informal slot where MPs raise urgent matters of public concern **without the mandatory 10–15 days' prior notice**.

- **Origin:** A **purely Indian parliamentary innovation** that emerged organically in **1962** (the name was coined by parliamentary journalists because it began at 12:00 "zero" hours).
- **Legal Status (Classic Prelims Trap!):** **Zero Hour is mentioned NEITHER in the Constitution of India NOR in the Rules of Procedure of either House!** It remains an **informal device** regulated purely by the Presiding Officer's discretion (today MPs submit morning notices before 10:00 AM and a lottery/Speaker's selection picks around 20 matters per day).

### Parliamentary Motions: Classification, Closure & Accountability Devices

In parliamentary parlance, **no discussion on a matter of general public importance can take place except on a motion made with the consent of the Presiding Officer**. The House expresses its collective decision or opinion by adopting or rejecting motions moved by either Ministers or Private Members. Motions fall into **three structural categories**:

1. **Substantive Motion:** A self-contained, independent proposal dealing with a very important matter—such as the impeachment of the President, removal of the Chief Election Commissioner or a Supreme Court judge, removal of the Speaker, or a Motion of Thanks.
2. **Substitute Motion:** A motion moved in substitution of an original motion, proposing an alternative policy or wording; if adopted by the House, it completely **supersedes the original motion**.
3. **Subsidiary Motion:** A motion that has **no meaning by itself** and cannot express the decision of the House without reference to the original motion or pending proceedings. It comprises three sub-types:
   - *Ancillary Motion:* Used as the regular procedural step for various kinds of business (e.g., *"That the Bill be taken into consideration"*).
   - *Superseding Motion:* Moved in the course of debate on another issue to supersede that issue (e.g., motion to adjourn the debate or recommit a Bill to a committee).
   - *Amendment:* Seeks to modify or substitute only a part of the original motion.

#### 1. Closure Motions (Four Kinds)

Moved to **cut short debate** and put a matter immediately to vote, Closure Motions take **four forms**:

- **Simple Closure:** Moved when a member proposes that the *"matter having been sufficiently discussed be now put to vote."*
- **Closure by Compartments:** Clauses of a Bill or lengthy resolution are grouped into **parts** before debate; each part is debated and voted as a unit.
- **Kangaroo Closure:** **ONLY important clauses** are debated and voted upon; **intervening clauses are skipped and deemed passed**!
- **Guillotine Closure:** When allotted time expires, **all undiscussed clauses (or undiscussed Budget Demands for Grants)** are put to vote **wholesale without debate**.

#### 2. Privilege Motion & Calling Attention Motion (1954 Indian Innovation)

- **Privilege Motion:** Moved against a **Minister** for breaching House or MP privilege by **withholding facts** or **giving wrong/distorted facts**; aims to **censure the Minister**.
- **Calling Attention Motion (`LS Rule 197`; `RS Rule 180`):** An **Indian innovation introduced in 1954** (NOT borrowed from Britain) that—**UNLIKE Zero Hour—IS expressly codified in the Rules**! It enables an MP, with Chair permission, to call a Minister's attention to an urgent public matter and elicit an **authoritative statement**. It carries **NO censure** and involves **NO vote** (only brief clarificatory questions).

#### 3. Adjournment Motion (Lok Sabha ONLY — Element of Censure)

Available **EXCLUSIVELY in the Lok Sabha** (barred in Rajya Sabha because it carries an **element of censure**), an **Adjournment Motion** interrupts scheduled House business to debate a definite urgent public crisis under strict filters:

- Needs **at least 50 Lok Sabha MPs** for admission; discussion lasts **not less than 2 hours 30 minutes**;
- Must concern a **definite, factual, urgent, recent single issue**;
- Must **NOT** cover **privilege**, revive same-session business, touch **sub judice** matters, or raise issues reachable via a distinct motion.

#### 4. No-Confidence Motion vs Censure Motion vs Confidence Motion

Under **`Article 75(3)`**, the Union Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. **The phrase "No-Confidence Motion" does NOT appear in the Constitution**—**LS Rule 198** operationalises it. Moved **ONLY in the Lok Sabha**, supported by **≥50 MPs**, directed **ONLY against the ENTIRE Council of Ministers**, requiring **NO reasons**, its passage **forces the entire Council to resign**. Since 1952, **28 No-Confidence Motions** have been moved (first: J.B. Kripalani vs Nehru, Aug 1963; peak: 15 vs Indira Gandhi; latest: Gaurav Gogoi vs Modi, Aug 2023)—none directly unseated a government (Morarji Desai resigned mid-debate in July 1979). Instead, three ministries fell on **Confidence Motions**: **V.P. Singh (1990)**, **H.D. Deve Gowda (1997)**, and **Atal Bihari Vajpayee (April 1999, lost by 1 vote: 269–270)**.

A **Censure Motion** (also **Lok Sabha ONLY**) **MUST state specific reasons**, may target an **individual Minister, group, or entire Council**, needs **no 50-MP floor**, and **if passed does NOT legally force resignation** (though the government must reaffirm its majority promptly).

```compare
# No-Confidence Motion vs Censure Motion vs Adjournment Motion
No-Confidence Motion | Censure Motion | Adjournment Motion
Permitted House :: Lok Sabha ONLY (Art. 75(3) + LS Rule 198) | Lok Sabha ONLY (LS Rules) | Lok Sabha ONLY (LS Rules — element of censure)
Reasons Required? :: NO — need NOT state any reasons for adoption | YES — MUST state specific reasons/failures | YES — definite, factual, recent, urgent single matter
Target :: ENTIRE Council of Ministers ONLY | Individual Minister, group of Ministers, OR entire Council | Government failure on a specific urgent public event
Minimum MP Support :: At least 50 Lok Sabha MPs | No 50-MP floor (Speaker's admission) | At least 50 Lok Sabha MPs (debate ≥ 2.5 hrs)
Effect of Passage :: Entire Council of Ministers MUST resign | Does NOT legally force resignation (government must reaffirm majority) | Severe parliamentary censure; does NOT automatically force resignation
```

#### 5. Other Procedural & Deliberative Floor Devices

- **No-Day-Yet-Named Motion:** Admitted by the Speaker, with **no date yet fixed** for debate (allotted later with BAC/Leader of House).
- **Dilatory Motion:** Moves to adjourn or retard debate on a pending Bill/motion; if abused, the Speaker may put it to vote immediately or refuse it.
- **Point of Order (`LS Rule 376` / `RS Rule 258`):** Raised on breach of procedure/Constitution; **immediately suspends proceedings**, allows **NO debate**, and the Chair's ruling is final.
- **Half-an-Hour Discussion (`LS Rule 55` / `RS Rule 60`):** Held **3 days/week** to clarify facts from a recent Question; **NO motion or vote**.
- **Short Duration Discussion (`LS Rule 193` / `RS Rule 176`):** Introduced in **1953** ("Two-Hour Discussion", max **2 hours**); urgent public debate with **NO motion or vote**.
- **Special Mention (`RS Rule 180A`) & Matter Under Rule 377 (`LS Rule 377`):** Residual floor devices for public matters falling outside Question Hour, Adjournment, Calling Attention, or Point of Order rules.

> [!prelims]
> **Four High-Yield Traps on Indian Innovations & Floor Devices:**
> 1. **Zero Hour (1962) vs Calling Attention (1954):** BOTH are **Indian innovations**, BUT **Zero Hour is NOT in the Rules**, whereas **Calling Attention IS in the Rules**! (Two more Indian innovations: **Short Duration Discussion [1953]** and **Committee on Government Assurances [1953]**!)
> 2. **Lok Sabha Exclusives:** **Adjournment Motion**, **No-Confidence Motion**, **Censure Motion**, and **Cut Motions** are permitted **ONLY in the Lok Sabha**—NEVER in the Rajya Sabha!
> 3. **No-Vote Debates:** **Calling Attention Motion**, **Half-an-Hour Discussion**, and **Short Duration Discussion (Rule 193 / 176)** involve **NO formal vote**.
> 4. **Constitutional Silence:** The phrase **"No-Confidence Motion"** is **NOT in the Constitution** (`Art. 75(3)` only states collective responsibility; Rule 198 operationalises it).

### Resolutions & the Youth Parliament Scheme

#### Resolutions vs Motions (`LS Rules 170–183`)

Members can move **Resolutions** to draw the attention of the House or government to matters of general public interest. While discussion rules mirror motions, two razor-sharp distinctions separate a **Resolution** from an ordinary **Motion**:

1. **Every Resolution Is a Substantive Motion, But NOT Every Motion Is a Resolution:** All resolutions fall strictly inside the category of **substantive motions** (self-contained proposals expressing the House's formal opinion or statutory decision).
2. **Mandatory Voting:** Whereas ordinary motions are **not** necessarily put to the vote of the House, **ALL Resolutions are REQUIRED to be voted upon**! Furthermore, once a resolution (or amendment to it) has been moved, it **cannot be withdrawn except with the leave of the House**.

Resolutions are classified into **three categories**:
- **Private Member's Resolution:** Moved by a non-minister MP; discussed only on **alternate Fridays** in the afternoon sitting.
- **Government Resolution:** Moved by a Minister; can be taken up any day from **Monday to Thursday**.
- **Statutory Resolution:** Moved by **either a Private Member OR a Minister**, specifically **tabled in pursuance of a provision of the Constitution or an Act of Parliament** (for example, resolutions under `Art. 61`, `Art. 67(b)`, `Art. 123(2)(a)` disapproving an Ordinance, `Art. 249`, `Art. 312`, `Art. 352`, or `Art. 356`).

#### Youth Parliament Scheme

Conceived on the recommendation of the **Fourth All India Whips' Conference (Bombay, 1962)** and launched by the **Ministry of Parliamentary Affairs** in **1966–67** (initially as the "Mock Parliament Competition Scheme" in Delhi higher secondary schools, before the **Eighth All India Whips' Conference at Bhopal in November 1972** renamed it the **Youth Parliament Scheme**), the programme nurtures parliamentary culture among students across Kendriya Vidyalayas, Jawahar Navodaya Vidyalayas, schools, universities, and the digital **National Youth Parliament Scheme (NYPS)** portal. Its core objectives are to acquaint young citizens with parliamentary procedures, cultivate tolerance for dissenting views, strengthen democratic discipline, and build informed leadership across India's grassroots.

## 5. Legislative Procedure, Kinds of Bills, Lapsing & Joint Sitting (Articles 107–111, 117)

Law-making is Parliament's primary constitutional function, governed by **`Articles 107` to `111`** and **`Article 117`** (with constitutional amendments governed separately by `Article 368`). Every proposed statute enters Parliament as a **Bill**—a legislative draft—and matures into an **Act of Parliament** only after navigating identical procedural stages in both Houses (except Money Bills) and receiving the **President's assent**. Understanding the precise boundaries between the **four classes of Bills**, the **six rules of lapsing on dissolution**, and the **Article 108 Joint Sitting** is indispensable for UPSC Prelims, which tests single-word qualifiers (*"only"*, *"either House"*, *"prior recommendation"*) with surgical regularity.

### Public Bills, Private Member's Bills & Pre-Legislative Consultation

By *mover*, Bills introduced in Parliament fall into **Public (Government) Bills** and **Private Member's Bills** (any MP who is NOT a Minister—**including backbench MPs of the ruling party**—is a "private member"). By *content*, Bills fall into four constitutional categories: **Ordinary Bills**, **Money Bills (`Article 110`)**, **Financial Bills (`Article 117(1)` & `117(3)`)**, and **Constitution Amendment Bills (`Article 368`)**.

| Dimension | Public (Government) Bill | Private Member's Bill |
|---|---|---|
| **Introduced By** | A Union Minister | Any MP other than a Minister (ruling or Opposition) |
| **Reflects** | Policies and legislative agenda of the Government | Stand of an individual MP or Opposition party on public matters |
| **Advance Notice** | **7 days** | **1 month** |
| **Drafting Support** | Drafted by the concerned Ministry in consultation with the **Law Ministry** | Drafted solely by the individual MP (Parliament Secretariat provides technical vetting only) |
| **Scheduling** | Any working day | **Alternate Fridays** (last 2.5 hours of the sitting) |
| **Chance of Passage & Defeat Impact** | High chance of passage; **rejection in Lok Sabha signals loss of confidence** and may compel ministerial resignation | Extremely low chance of passage; **rejection has NO impact** on government survival |

> [!example]
> **Only 14 Private Member's Bills Ever Enacted — None Since 1970!**
> Across more than seven decades of parliamentary democracy, only **14 Private Member's Bills** have ever become Acts of Parliament—and **not a single one has been enacted since 1970**! The last Private Member's Bill to receive Presidential assent was independent MP **Anand Narain Mulla's** *Supreme Court (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Bill, 1968* (assented **9 August 1970**). Earlier landmark enactments included **Feroze Gandhi's** *Parliamentary Proceedings (Protection of Publication) Act, 1956* (the statutory precursor to `Article 361A`). In April 2015, DMK MP **Tiruchi Siva's** *Rights of Transgender Persons Bill, 2014* passed the **Rajya Sabha unanimously** (the first Private Member's Bill to clear the Upper House in 45 years), yet stalled in the Lok Sabha—though it prodded the government to enact its own 2019 Act.

#### Pre-Legislative Consultation Policy (PLCP), 2014

To elevate statutory quality and citizen participation before Bills reach Parliament, the Union Government adopted the **Pre-Legislative Consultation Policy (PLCP)** in **February 2014** (echoing recommendations of the NCRWC and the National Advisory Council). The PLCP directs every Union ministry to place draft Bills and major subordinate legislation in the public domain for **at least 30 days** prior to Cabinet submission, accompanied by a plain-language explanatory note, **financial, environmental, and fundamental-rights impact assessments**, and a published summary of public feedback. Because the PLCP is merely a **non-binding executive guideline**, compliance remains chronically weak: **PRS Legislative Research** documented that **227 of 301 Bills (~75%)** introduced after 2014 bypassed pre-legislative public consultation entirely, over half of the remaining drafts fell short of the 30-day window, and the Law Ministry maintains no central compliance register.

### Ordinary Bills: The Five-Stage Legislative Journey (Articles 107, 111)

Any Bill outside financial subjects (`Articles 110, 117`) and constitutional amendments (`Article 368`) is an **Ordinary Bill**—introducible in **either House** by a Minister or Private MP without prior Presidential recommendation (barring `Art. 3` Bills). It traverses **five stages**:

1. **First Reading:** Mover seeks leave of the House, reads the title and objectives (normally no debate unless legislative competence is challenged), and the Bill is published in the **Gazette of India** *(pre-introduction Gazette publication waives formal leave)*.
2. **Second Reading (Pivotal Scrutiny Stage — 3 Sub-Stages):**
   - *General Discussion:* Principles debated; the House may (i) take it up immediately, (ii) refer it to a **Select Committee** (same House), (iii) refer it to a **Joint Committee** (both Houses, with the other House's concurrence), or (iv) circulate it for public opinion. Separately, the Chair may refer it to a **DRSC**.
   - *Committee Stage:* Clause-by-clause scrutiny with expert testimony without altering core principles.
   - *Consideration Stage:* Clause-by-clause floor debate, amendments, and separate voting.
3. **Third Reading:** Confined to accepting or rejecting the Bill as a whole; **NO substantive amendments** (only formal, verbal, or consequential corrections); passed by **simple majority**.
4. **Second House:** Mirrors all three readings with **four options**: (a) pass as-is, (b) pass with amendments and return, (c) reject, or (d) take no action. Rejection, final disagreement on amendments, or **>6 months' lapse** without passage triggers a **Joint Sitting (`Article 108`)**.
5. **Presidential Assent (`Article 111`):** President may **(a) Assent** (becomes an Act); **(b) Withhold assent (Absolute Veto)** (e.g., PEPSU Bill 1954; Salaries Bill 1991); or **(c) Return once for reconsideration (Suspensive Veto)**—repassage with or without amendments makes **assent mandatory**! Absence of any constitutional deadline also enables a **Pocket Veto** (President Zail Singh on the Indian Post Office Bill, 1986).

```flow
# Five-Stage Legislative Passage of an Ordinary Bill (Articles 107–111)
Stage 1: First Reading | Introduced in EITHER House (Minister or Private MP; no prior Pres. rec.) + Gazette publication
Stage 2: Second Reading | General Discussion → Committee Scrutiny (DRSC / Select / Joint) → Clause-by-Clause Consideration & Amendments
Stage 3: Third Reading | Overall acceptance/rejection ONLY (no substantive amendments); passed by Simple Majority
Stage 4: Second House | Passes as-is → Stage 5 || Amends / Rejects / Delays > 6 Months → Constitutional Deadlock → Joint Sitting (Article 108)
Stage 5: Presidential Assent (Art. 111) | Assent (Becomes Act) || Withhold (Absolute Veto) / Pocket Veto || Return Once (Suspensive Veto — Mandatory Assent on Repassage)
```

### Money Bills & the Constitutional Certification Controversy (Articles 109–110)

Reflecting Lower-House supremacy over public finance, **`Articles 109` and `110`** establish an exclusive legislative track for **Money Bills**, strictly capping Rajya Sabha intervention.

#### Exhaustive Constitutional Definition (`Article 110(1)`)

A Bill is a **Money Bill** if it contains **"ONLY"** provisions dealing with all or any of **six substantive fiscal matters** plus incidental clauses:
- **`(a)`** Imposition, abolition, remission, alteration, or regulation of any **tax**;
- **`(b)`** Regulation of **borrowing of money** or giving of any **guarantee** by the Government of India;
- **`(c)`** Custody of, or payments into/withdrawals from, the **Consolidated Fund of India (CFI)** or **Contingency Fund of India**;
- **`(d)`** **Appropriation** of money out of the CFI;
- **`(e)`** Declaring any expenditure **charged on the CFI** or increasing its amount;
- **`(f)`** Receipt/custody/issue of money on account of the CFI or **Public Account of India**, or **audit of Union or State accounts**;
- **`(g)`** Any matter **incidental** to `(a)`–`(f)`.

#### Three Express Exclusions (`Article 110(2)`)

A Bill is **NOT** a Money Bill merely because it provides for: (1) **fines or pecuniary penalties**; (2) **fees for licences or services rendered**; or (3) **taxes imposed by local authorities for local purposes**. *(Remember: **audit of State accounts** IS a Money Bill matter under `110(1)(f)`, whereas local-body taxes, fines, and licence/service fees are NOT!)*

#### Special Legislative Procedure (`Articles 109, 110(3)–(4), 117(1)`)

1. **Lok Sabha ONLY (`Art. 109(1)`):** Never introduced in Rajya Sabha.
2. **Prior Presidential Recommendation (`Art. 117(1)`):** Mandatory (no recommendation needed to reduce/abolish a tax).
3. **Minister ONLY:** Every Money Bill is a Government Bill.
4. **Speaker's Final Certificate (`Art. 110(3)–(4)`):** Speaker's decision is declared **"final"**, with a signed certificate endorsed on transmission to RS and President.
5. **14-Day Rajya Sabha Cap (`Art. 109(2)–(5)`):** RS **CANNOT amend or reject**—only make **non-binding recommendations within 14 days**, else deemed passed as cleared by LS.
6. **NO Joint Sitting (`Art. 108`)** and **NO Presidential Return (`Art. 111`)** (assent or withhold only).

#### The Money Bill Controversy & Pending Seven-Judge Judicial Review

Because certifying a controversial regulatory statute as a Money Bill allows a Union government lacking a Rajya Sabha majority to sidestep Upper House amendments and scrutiny altogether, the meaning of the word **"ONLY"** in `Article 110(1)` and the "finality" of the Speaker's certificate under `Article 110(3)` erupted into one of modern India's sharpest constitutional disputes.

> "Passing of a Bill as a Money Bill, when it does not qualify for it, damages the delicate balance of bicameralism which is a part of the basic structure of the Constitution." — Justice D.Y. Chandrachud (dissenting), *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India*, 26 September 2018

> [!case]
> ***Puttaswamy (Aadhaar-5)* (2018) & *Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank* (2019) — The Pending Seven-Judge Money Bill Reference**
> - ***K.S. Puttaswamy (Aadhaar-5)* (26 September 2018, 5-Judge Bench):** Challenged the certification of the **Aadhaar Act, 2016** as a Money Bill. By a **4:1 majority** (Justice A.K. Sikri), the Court upheld certification because Section 7 tied Aadhaar authentication to subsidies paid out of the CFI. In a celebrated lone dissent, **Justice D.Y. Chandrachud** held that `Article 110(3)` finality does **not** bar judicial review for constitutional illegality (`Art. 122` shields *procedural irregularity*, not substantive constitutional violations), branding the passage of Aadhaar as a Money Bill a **"fraud on the Constitution"** because the Act regulated private biometric authentication far beyond `Article 110(1)`.
> - ***Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank Ltd.* (13 November 2019, 5-Judge Bench):** Reviewing **Part XIV of the Finance Act, 2017**—which used a Money Bill to restructure 19 statutory judicial tribunals (alongside unrelated amendments to PMLA and electoral bonds)—a co-equal five-judge Bench led by CJI Ranjan Gogoi sharply doubted *Puttaswamy*'s cursory reading of **"only"** in `Article 110(1)` and formally referred the scope of Money Bill certification and judicial review of the Speaker's decision to a **seven-judge Constitution Bench**.
> - **Current Status (November 2026):** Seven years after *Rojer Mathew* (last listed in October 2023), the **seven-judge Constitution Bench reference remains pending**, leaving this crucial bicameral boundary judicially unsettled.

### Financial Bills Category I (Article 117(1)), Category II (Article 117(3)) & Constitution Amendment Bills (Article 368)

Memorise the golden constitutional axiom of Indian fiscal law: **ALL Money Bills are Financial Bills, but NOT all Financial Bills are Money Bills!** Only those Financial Bills containing **"ONLY"** `Article 110(1)` provisions qualify as Money Bills. All other fiscal legislation falls into **Financial Bills Category I (`Article 117(1)`)** and **Financial Bills Category II (`Article 117(3)`)**.

#### Financial Bill — Category I (`Article 117(1)`)

A **Financial Bill (I)** is a Bill that contains **any or all matters enumerated in `Article 110(1)` PLUS other matters of general legislation** (for example, a regulatory Bill imposing a new cess/tax while creating a statutory authority and criminal offences). Its constitutional procedure is razor-sharp:

- **Similar to a Money Bill in ONLY TWO Respects:**
  1. It can be introduced **ONLY in the Lok Sabha** (never in the Rajya Sabha); and
  2. It can be introduced **ONLY on the prior recommendation of the President** *(proviso: NO Presidential recommendation is needed to move an amendment proposing the reduction or abolition of any tax)*.
- **Identical to an Ordinary Bill in ALL OTHER Respects:** Once introduced in the Lok Sabha, it sheds every Money Bill privilege! There is **NO Speaker's certificate**, the **Rajya Sabha is fully co-equal** (can amend or reject it completely—no 14-day cap!), a **Joint Sitting (`Article 108`) CAN be summoned** to resolve deadlocks, and the **President CAN return it** for reconsideration under `Article 111`!

#### Financial Bill — Category II (`Article 117(3)`)

A **Financial Bill (II)** contains provisions involving **expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India**, **but does NOT include any of the matters specified in `Article 110(1)`** (for example, a general social welfare Bill creating new institutions whose operational funding will eventually draw on the CFI without directly appropriating funds in the Bill itself).

- **Identical to an Ordinary Bill at Introduction:** It can be introduced in **EITHER House** (Lok Sabha OR Rajya Sabha) by a Minister or Private Member, and requires **NO prior recommendation of the President for introduction**!
- **The ONE Critical Constitutional Twist:** **NEITHER House can PASS a Financial Bill (II) unless the President has recommended to that House the CONSIDERATION of the Bill!** Thus, the President's recommendation is required at the **consideration stage** (not introduction). In every other respect—co-equal Rajya Sabha power to reject/amend, availability of a **Joint Sitting (`Article 108`)**, and Presidential power to **return once (`Article 111`)**—it follows the Ordinary Bill pathway.

#### Constitution Amendment Bills (`Article 368` — Procedural Profile)

*(Substantive Basic Structure doctrine and the three types of amendments are detailed in the chapter on Amendment of the Constitution.)* Procedurally, an **`Article 368` Bill** stands sharply apart from both Ordinary and Money Bills: it may be introduced in **either House** by a **Minister or Private Member** with **NO prior Presidential recommendation**; it must be passed by a **special majority** (>50% of total membership of the House AND ≥2/3rd of members present and voting) in **each House separately** (**NO Joint Sitting** is permitted!); if it touches entrenched federal features, it requires **ratification by at least half of the State Legislative Assemblies** by simple majority; and under the **`24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971`**, the President **MUST give assent** (no absolute, suspensive, or pocket veto!). A live illustration unfolded on **17 April 2026**, when the **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026** (proposing 850 Lok Sabha seats on the 2011 Census) collapsed on the Lok Sabha floor—polling **298 Ayes to 230 Noes** out of 528 present and voting, falling 54 votes short of `Article 368`'s mandatory two-thirds supermajority (352).

```compare
# Four-Way Legislative Matrix: Ordinary vs Money vs Financial I vs Financial II
Ordinary Bill (Art. 107) | Money Bill (Art. 110) | Financial Bill I (Art. 117(1)) | Financial Bill II (Art. 117(3))
Content :: Non-financial general legislation | ONLY Art. 110(1) fiscal matters | Art. 110(1) matters + general legislation | Expenditure from CFI; NO Art. 110 matters
Introduction House :: EITHER House | LOK SABHA ONLY | LOK SABHA ONLY | EITHER House
Presidential Recommendation :: NOT required (barring Art. 3/274) | MANDATORY at introduction | MANDATORY at introduction (except tax cut/abolition) | NOT at intro; MANDATORY at CONSIDERATION stage!
Speaker's Certificate :: No | YES — declared FINAL (Art. 110(3)) | No | No
Rajya Sabha Power :: Co-equal (amend or reject) | Recommend ONLY within 14 days (cannot amend/reject) | Co-equal (amend or reject) | Co-equal (amend or reject)
Joint Sitting (Art. 108) :: YES | NO | YES | YES
Presidential Return (Art. 111) :: YES (return once) | NO (assent or withhold only) | YES (return once) | YES (return once)
```

*(Quick 5th-column anchor for **Constitution Amendment Bill (`Art. 368`)**: Either House | Minister or Private MP | **No** Presidential recommendation | No Speaker certificate | **Co-equal RS (separate passage)** | **Special Majority** (+ half States if federal) | **NO Joint Sitting** | **Mandatory Presidential Assent — NO veto or return!**)*

### Exact Rules of Lapsing of Bills on Dissolution of Lok Sabha (Articles 107, 108(5))

First lock in the golden precursor rule: **PROROGATION of a House NEVER causes any pending Bill to lapse (`Article 107(3)`)**—only pending notices lapse (a crucial contrast with the UK Parliament, where prorogation kills pending Bills).

Upon **DISSOLUTION of the Lok Sabha**, however, the Constitution prescribes **six airtight rules (`Articles 107(4)–(5)` and `108(5)`)** determining which Bills die and which survive into the next Lok Sabha. Remember the intuitive diagnostic test: ***Did the dissolving Lok Sabha touch the Bill without both Houses completing passage (and without a prior Joint Sitting notification)? If YES, it LAPSES; otherwise it SURVIVES!***

| # | Exact Status of the Bill at Dissolution of the Lok Sabha | Constitutional Fate | Constitutional Basis & Logic |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Pending in the Lok Sabha** (whether it originated in the LS OR was passed by RS and transmitted to LS) | **LAPSES** | `Art. 107(5)` — the dying LS had unfinished business before it |
| **2** | **Passed by the Lok Sabha, but pending in the Rajya Sabha** | **LAPSES** | `Art. 107(5)` — carries the political mandate of the dissolved LS |
| **3** | **Pending in the Rajya Sabha, and NOT passed by the Lok Sabha** | **SURVIVES** | `Art. 107(4)` — the dissolving LS never touched it; RS is permanent |
| **4** | **Deadlock where the President notified intention to summon a Joint Sitting BEFORE dissolution** | **SURVIVES** | **`Art. 108(5)`** — Joint Sitting proceeds even after dissolution! |
| **5** | **Passed by BOTH Houses, awaiting Presidential assent** | **SURVIVES** | Parliamentary legislative stage already completed |
| **6** | **Passed by BOTH Houses, returned by President for reconsideration** | **SURVIVES** | `Art. 111` — the newly constituted Lok Sabha reconsiders it |

**Related Parliamentary Committee Rules on Dissolution:** A Bill pending before a **Lok Sabha Select Committee** lapses on dissolution, as do all pending motions, resolutions, and notices in the Lok Sabha. Conversely, (1) a Bill pending before a **Rajya Sabha Select Committee** (not passed by LS) **survives**, and (2) **pending assurances** to be examined by the **Committee on Government Assurances do NOT lapse** on dissolution of the Lok Sabha!

### Joint Sitting of Both Houses (Article 108) & Delegated Legislation

#### Joint Sitting Machinery (`Articles 108 & 118(4)`)

To break legislative gridlock between two co-equal Houses on non-Money legislation, **`Article 108`** provides an extraordinary deadlock-resolution mechanism: a **Joint Sitting** of both Houses summoned by the **President** after notifying both Houses of their intention to do so.

- **Three Deadlock Triggers (`Article 108(1)`):** Once a Bill has been passed by one House and transmitted to the other House, a deadlock arises if:
  1. The Bill is **rejected** by the other House; OR
  2. The Houses have **finally disagreed** as to the amendments to be made in the Bill; OR
  3. **More than six months** have elapsed from the date of receipt of the Bill by the other House without the Bill being passed by it *(in reckoning six months, **no account is taken of any period during which the other House is prorogued or adjourned for more than four consecutive days**!)*.
- **Strict Scope Limitation:** A Joint Sitting applies **ONLY to Ordinary Bills and Financial Bills (Categories I and II)**—it is **NEVER available for Money Bills (`Article 110`)** (where LS overrides RS automatically) or **Constitution Amendment Bills (`Article 368`)** (which must clear each House *separately* by special majority)!
- **Presiding Hierarchy (`Article 118(4)` — Classic Prelims Trap!):**
  1. **Speaker of the Lok Sabha** presides;
  2. In the Speaker's absence, the **Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha** presides;
  3. In the absence of both, the **Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** presides;
  4. If all three are absent, **such other person as may be determined by the members present at the sitting** presides.
  **CRITICAL RULE: The Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (Vice-President of India) NEVER presides over a Joint Sitting** because the Vice-President is **NOT a member of either House of Parliament**!
- **Quorum, Rules, Voting & Amendment Limits:** Quorum is **one-tenth of the total combined membership of both Houses** (~79 MPs). Proceedings are governed by the **Rules of Procedure of the Lok Sabha** (not Rajya Sabha). Decisions are taken by a **simple majority of the total number of members of both Houses present and voting**—structurally favouring the Lok Sabha (543 seats) over the Rajya Sabha (245 seats). Under **`Article 108(4)`**, **no new amendments** may be proposed at a Joint Sitting except: (i) if the second House rejected or delayed >6 months, only amendments **made necessary by the delay**; (ii) if the second House passed with amendments causing disagreement, only amendments tied to **the matters of disagreement or delay**.

Under `Article 197`, if a State Legislative Council rejects or delays an Ordinary Bill passed by the Assembly, the Assembly simply passes it a second time—capping the Council's maximum delaying power at **3 + 1 = 4 months**! Also remember: the Women's Reservation Bill was NEVER passed via Joint Sitting.)*

#### Delegated (Subordinate) Legislation & Parliamentary Control

Modern governance generates technical regulations—drug safety thresholds, spectrum pricing, environmental emission norms—that a generalist Parliament lacks the time and technical detail to codify directly. Consequently, Parliament passes **"skeleton" parent Acts** stating core policy and delegates authority to the Executive to frame detailed **rules, regulations, bye-laws, and notifications** (**delegated / subordinate legislation**).

In the foundational seven-judge Constitution Bench decision ***In Re: The Delhi Laws Act, 1912* (1951)**, the Supreme Court drew the enduring constitutional boundary: while delegated legislation is constitutionally permissible in India, **Parliament CANNOT abdicate or delegate its "essential legislative function"**—defined as declaring the core legislative policy and formulating binding standards to guide the delegate. Three oversight safeguards check executive rule-making: (1) **Laying before Parliament** (typically for **30 session days**, allowing either House to modify or annul rules); (2) continuous scrutiny by each House's **Committee on Subordinate Legislation** (15 MPs each; audits whether rules stay within the parent Act and urges framing rules within **six months** of enactment); and (3) **Judicial Review** striking down *ultra vires* or manifestly arbitrary rules.

## 6. The Union Budget, Financial Control & Public Funds (Articles 112–116, 265–267)

No legislative power matters more than the **power of the purse**—crystallised in the democratic maxim *"no taxation without representation."* Across **`Articles 112` to `117`** and **`Articles 265` to `267`**, the Constitution constructs a watertight fiscal circuit ensuring that the Union Executive can neither levy a single rupee in tax nor withdraw a single rupee from the Consolidated Fund without explicit parliamentary sanction.

### Constitutional Foundation of Financial Control & the 2017 Reforms (Articles 112, 265–266)

Two ironclad constitutional commands anchor public finance in India:

- **`Article 265` (Rule of Law in Taxation):** *"No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law."* The Executive can **never** impose a tax by an administrative order, executive resolution, or cabinet notification—only an Act passed by a competent legislature can levy a tax. *(Nuance: a **fee** involving direct quid pro quo for a service rendered may be adjusted under validly delegated rules, whereas a compulsory **tax** requires statutory authority.)*
- **`Article 266(3)` (Rule of Law in Expenditure):** *"No moneys out of the Consolidated Fund of India or the Consolidated Fund of a State shall be appropriated except in accordance with law and for the purposes and in the manner provided in this Constitution."* Even after the Lok Sabha votes Demands for Grants, not a single rupee can be drawn until Parliament enacts an **Appropriation Act (`Article 114`)**!

Under **`Article 112`**, the President shall in respect of every **financial year (1 April to 31 March)** cause to be laid before both Houses of Parliament a statement of estimated receipts and expenditure of the Government of India for that year—designated constitutionally as the **"Annual Financial Statement" (AFS)**. **Crucial Prelims Fact: The word "Budget" appears NOWHERE in the Constitution of India!** Under `Article 112(2)`, the AFS must strictly separate (i) **expenditure on revenue account** from other (capital) expenditure, and (ii) **expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** from other (voted) expenditure.

#### Balancing Executive Initiative with Parliamentary Supremacy

Indian budgetary procedure balances two Westminster norms: **only the Executive may initiate taxes or spending** (`Article 113(3)`: no Demand for a Grant can be made except on the **recommendation of the President**; `Article 117(1)`: no Bill imposing or increasing a tax can be moved without Presidential recommendation), whereas **Parliament (chiefly the Lok Sabha) holds veto and reduction power** (it can **reduce or abolish a tax**, or **refuse or reduce a Demand for a Grant**, **but can NEVER increase a tax or increase a Demand**!). Alongside the AFS, the Finance Minister tables the **Finance Bill**, **Demands for Grants**, **Appropriation Bill**, **Expenditure Budget**, **Receipt Budget**, and statutory fiscal statements mandated by the **Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003** (Macro-Economic Framework Statement and Medium-Term Fiscal Policy-cum-Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement).

#### The Three Landmark 2017 Budgetary Reforms

Effective from **FY 2017–18**, three structural reforms transformed the Union Budget's architecture:

1. **Advancement of Presentation to 1 February:** Shifted Budget Day forward by nearly a month from the colonial practice of the last working day of February, allowing Parliament to finish enactment **before 31 March** so ministries can spend immediately from 1 April—eliminating the routine necessity of a **Vote on Account** in non-election years.
2. **Merger of the Railway Budget with the General Budget:** Implementing the recommendation of the **Bibek Debroy Committee (2015)**, the government ended the **92-year separation** of the Railway Budget that had been instituted in **1924** on the recommendation of the **Acworth Committee (1921)**—yielding a unified picture of India's transport and fiscal outlays.
3. **Abolition of Plan vs Non-Plan Classification:** Following the replacement of the Planning Commission by NITI Aayog, the distorting Plan vs Non-Plan split (which starved maintenance of existing assets as "Non-Plan") was replaced with a transparent **Revenue vs Capital** expenditure classification.

### Charged versus Voted Expenditure (Articles 112(3), 113)

To guarantee that vital constitutional guardians and sovereign debt repayments can never be held hostage to partisan parliamentary blackmail, **`Articles 112(3)` and `113`** divide Consolidated Fund outlays into two watertight tracks:

- **Charged Expenditure (`Article 113(1)`):** **Non-votable**—it is **NOT submitted to the vote of Parliament**, **YET IT CAN BE DISCUSSED** freely in either House!
- **Voted Expenditure (`Article 113(2)`):** Submitted in the form of **Demands for Grants** **exclusively to the Lok Sabha**, which has the power to assent, refuse, or reduce any demand.

Under **`Article 112(3)`** (read with allied Articles), expenditure **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** consists of **nine exhaustive items**:

1. **President of India:** Emoluments and allowances of the President and other expenditure relating to the Presidential office;
2. **Presiding Officers of Parliament:** Salaries and allowances of the **Chairman and Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** and the **Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha**;
3. **Union Debt Charges:** Debt charges for which the Government of India is liable—including interest, sinking fund charges, redemption charges, and expenditure connected with raising loans and servicing debt;
4. **Supreme Court Judges:** Salaries, allowances, and **pensions** payable to or in respect of **Judges of the Supreme Court**;
5. **High Court Judges' PENSIONS Only:** **Pensions** payable to or in respect of Judges of any **High Court** (and the pre-1950 Federal Court);
6. **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG):** Salary, allowances, and pension payable to or in respect of the **CAG of India**;
7. **Administrative Expenses of Constitutional Watchdogs:** Administrative expenses—including salaries, allowances, and pensions of staff—of the **Supreme Court (`Article 146(3)`)**, the **Office of the CAG (`Article 148(6)`)**, and the **Union Public Service Commission (`Article 322`)**;
8. **Court Decrees & Arbitral Awards:** Any sums required to satisfy any **judgment, decree, or award** of any court or arbitral tribunal;
9. **Other Constitutionally/Statutorily Charged Sums:** Any other expenditure declared by the Constitution (e.g., statutory Grants-in-Aid to States under `Article 275`) or by Parliament by law to be so charged.

> [!remember]
> **Two Classic UPSC Prelims Traps on Charged Expenditure:**
> 1. **The High Court Judges' Salary vs Pension Split:** Because High Court judges are transferable across States, their **SALARIES AND ALLOWANCES** are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of the STATE (`Article 202(3)(d)`)**, whereas their **PENSIONS** are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of INDIA (`Article 112(3)(d)(iii)`)**!
> 2. **Discussion Permitted, Voting Barred:** Charged expenditure is **NOT shielded from parliamentary debate**—both Houses CAN discuss every charged item; only **voting** is barred! Also note: **salaries of the Prime Minister, Ministers, MPs, and Election Commissioners** are **NOT** charged on the CFI!

### Six Stages of Budget Enactment (Articles 112–117)

From presentation to final tax enactment, the Union Budget moves through **six tightly sequenced constitutional stages**:

```flow
# Six-Stage Enactment of the Union Budget (Articles 112–117)
Stage 1: Presentation (1 Feb) | Finance Minister delivers Budget Speech in Lok Sabha (11 AM) & lays AFS in Rajya Sabha — NO same-day debate
Stage 2: General Discussion | 3–4 days across BOTH Houses on broad fiscal priorities — NO Cut Motions, NO voting; FM replies
Stage 3: Scrutiny by 24 DRSCs | Parliament takes 3–4 week recess; 24 Standing Committees dissect ministry Demands & submit non-binding reports
Stage 4: Voting on Demands for Grants | LOK SABHA ONLY (Art. 113(2)) · 3 Cut Motions (Policy/Economy/Token) · Final-day GUILLOTINE
Stage 5: Appropriation Bill (Art. 114) | Authorises CFI withdrawal (Voted + Charged); NO amendments altering amount/destination; 14-day RS cap
Stage 6: Finance Bill (Art. 117 / 110) | Enacts tax proposals within 75 DAYS (Provisional Collection of Taxes Act, 1931); Money Bill route
```

1. **Stage 1 — Presentation of the Budget:** On **1 February** (11:00 AM), the Finance Minister presents the Budget to the **Lok Sabha** with a Budget Speech; immediately at the conclusion of the speech, the Budget is laid before the **Rajya Sabha** (which can discuss it later, never vote Demands). **No discussion takes place on the day of presentation.**
2. **Stage 2 — General Discussion:** Commencing a few days later and lasting **3 to 4 days in both Houses**, members debate the Budget as a whole and underlying economic philosophy; **no Cut Motions can be moved and no vote is taken**. The Finance Minister gives a general reply at the close.
3. **Stage 3 — Scrutiny by 24 Departmental Standing Committees (DRSCs):** Both Houses adjourn for a **3-to-4 week recess**, during which the **24 DRSCs** examine the ministry-wise **Demands for Grants** in detail, grill ministry secretaries, and submit detailed analytical reports to both Houses (non-binding, yet indispensable for informed debate).
4. **Stage 4 — Voting on Demands for Grants (`Article 113`):** **EXCLUSIVELY a privilege of the Lok Sabha** (the Rajya Sabha has zero voting role on Demands, though it debates ministry workings). Each ministry presents separate Demands (currently ~100+ Demands across civil and defence ministries). Guided by the Business Advisory Committee, the Lok Sabha selects a handful of key ministries for detailed debate (usually ~26 allotted days overall) during which MPs move **Cut Motions**. Once a Demand is voted, it becomes a **Grant**. On the **final allotted day**, the Speaker applies the **Guillotine**, putting ALL remaining undiscussed Demands for Grants to a single wholesale vote!
   - *Empirical Reality (2024–2026):* **PRS Legislative Research** tracks routine over-reliance on guillotining: across the decade 2015–16 to 2024–25, **over two-thirds of voted Union spending** was guillotined annually—hitting **100%** in 2018–19 and 2023–24, and **89%** in 2024–25. In **Budget Session 2026 (Jan–Apr 2026)**, **77% of the Union Budget was guillotined**: of five ministries originally listed for Lok Sabha discussion (Railways, Agriculture, Home Affairs, External Affairs, Power), **only Agriculture and Railways** were actually debated!
5. **Stage 5 — Passing of the Appropriation Bill (`Article 114`):** Under `Article 266(3)`, no money can leave the CFI without an **Appropriation Act**. Accordingly, the government introduces an **Appropriation Bill** aggregating both (a) all **Grants voted by the Lok Sabha** and (b) all **expenditure charged on the CFI**. Under **`Article 114(2)`**, **NO amendment** can be proposed in either House that alters the **amount** or varies the **destination** of any grant or charged sum (the Speaker's decision on admissibility is final). Certified as a **Money Bill**, it goes to the Rajya Sabha for up to **14 days** and becomes the Appropriation Act upon Presidential assent.
6. **Stage 6 — Passing of the Finance Bill (`Article 117` / `Article 110`):** While the Appropriation Bill legalises the *expenditure* side of the Budget, the **Finance Bill** enacts the government's *taxation and revenue* proposals for the new financial year. Under the **Provisional Collection of Taxes Act, 1931**, the Finance Bill **MUST be enacted (including Presidential assent) within 75 days of its introduction**. Unlike the Appropriation Bill, **amendments MAY be moved** to the Finance Bill to **reduce or abolish a tax** (though never to impose or increase a tax without Presidential recommendation under `Article 117(1)`). Certified as a Money Bill (when restricted to `Article 110(1)` subjects), its enactment completes the Union Budget cycle.

### Cut Motions & All Six Special Parliamentary Grants (Articles 115–116)

#### Three Cut Motions (Lok Sabha Only)

During Stage 4 (Voting on Demands for Grants), Lok Sabha MPs may move **three kinds of Cut Motions** to reduce a Demand. Because moving a Cut Motion challenges the government's budgetary authority, **passage of ANY Cut Motion in the Lok Sabha amounts to a vote of no-confidence and forces the Council of Ministers to resign** (hence, in practice, Cut Motions are almost never passed when a government enjoys a majority, yet they serve as razor-sharp tools to spotlight policy flaws and ventilate grievances):

| Cut Motion | Proposed Reduction | Core Constitutional & Political Objective |
|---|---|---|
| **Policy Cut Motion** | Reduce the Demand to **₹1** | Expresses **disapproval of the underlying policy** of the Demand; mover may advocate an alternative policy |
| **Economy Cut Motion** | Reduce the Demand by a **specified amount** (lump sum or specific item) | Highlights wasteful expenditure and seeks **economy** in public spending |
| **Token Cut Motion** | Reduce the Demand by **₹100** | Ventilates a **specific grievance** squarely within the administrative responsibility of the Union Government |

*(Admissibility conditions: a Cut Motion must relate to ONE demand only, be framed clearly without defamatory/ironical language, must NOT seek to amend/repeal an existing law, must NOT touch CFI-charged expenditure, must NOT raise sub judice or privilege issues, and must NOT raise a trivial matter.)*

#### Vote on Account & All Six Special Grants (`Articles 115–116`)

Because unexpected wars, natural disasters, policy expansions, or election schedules often outpace the annual Budget, **`Articles 115` and `116`** empower the **Lok Sabha** to sanction **seven extraordinary grants** (each following the exact same legislative procedure as the regular Budget):

| Grant | Constitutional Basis | Exact Meaning & Prelims Distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Vote on Account** | **`Article 116(1)(a)`** | **Advance grant** enabling the government to draw funds for routine expenditure pending full Budget passage (conventionally **1/6th** of annual estimates for **~2 months**). Since the 2017 shift to 1 Feb, used mainly in **general election years** alongside an **Interim Budget** |
| **Supplementary Grant** | **`Article 115(1)(a)`** | Granted when the sum authorised by the Appropriation Act for a **particular existing service** proves **insufficient** for the current financial year |
| **Additional Grant** | **`Article 115(1)(a)`** | Granted when a need arises during the current financial year for **additional expenditure upon some NEW service** NOT contemplated in that year's annual Budget |
| **Excess Grant** | **`Article 115(1)(b)`** | Regularises money **ALREADY spent on a service in excess** of the amount granted for that service in a financial year; voted by LS **AFTER the financial year ends**—**MUST be vetted and approved by the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) BEFORE submission to Lok Sabha!** |
| **Vote of Credit** | **`Article 116(1)(b)`** | A **"blank cheque"** given by the Lok Sabha to the Executive to meet an unexpected demand upon India's resources whose **magnitude or indefinite character** (e.g., sudden war or national calamity) prevents stating detailed figures |
| **Exceptional Grant** | **`Article 116(1)(c)`** | Granted for a **special purpose** that forms **no part of the current service** of any financial year |
| **Token Grant** | `Art. 115` / Parliamentary Convention | Nominal demand of **₹1** voted by Lok Sabha when funds for a **new service** are met entirely by **reappropriation** (transferring savings from one head to another without extra CFI outflow), preserving parliamentary consent for the new service |

> [!prelims]
> **Three High-Yield Financial Control Traps:**
> 1. **The Rupee-Figure Confusion:** **Policy Cut = reduce *to* ₹1** | **Token Cut = reduce *by* ₹100** | **Token Grant = grant of ₹1** (reappropriation for a *new service*). Never mix up Token Cut (₹100) and Token Grant (₹1)!
> 2. **PAC Pre-Vetting of Excess Grants:** An **Excess Grant** (`Art. 115(1)(b)`) is the ONLY grant that **MUST be examined by the Public Accounts Committee (PAC)** *before* the Lok Sabha votes on it!
> 3. **Vote on Account vs Interim Budget:** A **Vote on Account** deals **ONLY with the expenditure side** of the Budget (authorising temporary withdrawal from CFI), whereas an **Interim Budget** (presented in an election year) presents **both receipts and expenditure** (just like a full Budget, though by convention outgoing governments avoid radical new tax or policy overhauls).

### The Three Public Funds of India (Articles 266–267)

The Constitution establishes **three distinct public accounts** for the Union Government (mirrored identically at the State level for each State Government under `Articles 266` and `267(2)`):

1. **Consolidated Fund of India (`Article 266(1)`):** The primary sovereign treasury into which flow: (a) **all revenues** received by the Government of India (direct/indirect taxes, dividends, non-tax receipts); (b) **all loans raised** by the Government by the issue of treasury bills, internal/external loans, or ways and means advances; and (c) **all moneys received in repayment of loans**. Every legally authorised payment on behalf of the Union is met from this Fund, and **NO money can be appropriated (issued or drawn) out of the CFI except in accordance with a parliamentary Appropriation Act (`Article 266(3)`)**.
2. **Public Account of India (`Article 266(2)`):** Receives **all other public moneys** received by or on behalf of the Government of India—such as **provident fund deposits (EPF/GPF)**, **small savings collections (PPF, National Savings Certificates, Sukanya Samriddhi)**, **judicial deposits**, **departmental deposits**, **remittances**, and **reserve funds** (e.g., Depreciation Reserve Fund). Crucially, these moneys **do NOT belong to the government**—the government holds them purely as a **banker or trustee**, bound to repay them to rightful depositors. Consequently, **payments from the Public Account are operated by EXECUTIVE ACTION and do NOT require parliamentary appropriation or voting!**
3. **Contingency Fund of India (`Article 267(1)`):** Notice the precision wording of `Article 267(1)`—**the Constitution itself did NOT directly establish the Contingency Fund; it empowered Parliament to establish it by law!** Exercising this power, Parliament enacted the **Contingency Fund of India Act, 1950**. Structured as an **imprest** (fixed revolving advance pool) placed **at the disposal of the President** to meet **unforeseen urgent expenditure** pending parliamentary authorisation, its corpus was raised dramatically from ₹500 crore (2005) to **₹30,000 crore** via **Section 127 of the Finance Act, 2021**. The Fund is held on the President's behalf by the **Secretary, Department of Economic Affairs (Ministry of Finance)**—with **40% of the corpus placed directly at the disposal of the Expenditure Secretary** under the 2021 amended Contingency Fund Rules for swift crisis disbursement. Like the Public Account, initial disbursement occurs by **executive action** (President's sanction), **but every rupee drawn MUST subsequently receive *post facto* parliamentary authorisation under `Article 115`/`116`**, followed by replenishment from the Consolidated Fund of India back to the ₹30,000 crore ceiling.

```compare
# Consolidated Fund vs Public Account vs Contingency Fund of India (Articles 266–267)
Consolidated Fund of India (Art. 266(1)) | Public Account of India (Art. 266(2)) | Contingency Fund of India (Art. 267(1))
Legal Creation :: Directly established by the Constitution | Directly established by the Constitution | Empowered by Art. 267(1); created by Contingency Fund of India Act, 1950
Inflows / Corpus :: All Union tax & non-tax revenues, borrowings (T-bills, loans), loan recoveries | All other public moneys: Provident Funds, small savings (PPF), judicial/departmental deposits, remittances | Fixed imprest drawn from CFI — raised to ₹30,000 crore via Sec. 127, Finance Act, 2021
Ownership Nature :: Government's own sovereign revenues & borrowings | Held as BANKER / TRUSTEE (repayable to depositors) | Emergency advance pool for unforeseen urgent expenditure
Parliamentary Control :: PRIOR Appropriation Act MANDATORY (Art. 266(3)) | NO parliamentary appropriation — operated purely by EXECUTIVE ACTION | Executive release first; POST FACTO parliamentary authorisation & CFI replenishment mandatory
Custodian :: Government of India (Parliamentary custody) | Union Executive | Placed at President's disposal; held by DEA Secretary (40% directly with Expenditure Secretary under 2021 Rules)
```

## 7. Parliamentary Committees, Consultative Committees & Forums

A plenary chamber of 543 or 245 lawmakers debating under live television cameras, strict party whips, and compressed session calendars cannot dissect a 300-clause tax code or audit every crore spent by fifty Union ministries. That forensic work belongs to **Parliamentary Committees**—the bipartisan **"mini-parliaments"** where executive officials are grilled behind closed doors, independent economists and citizens testify, and legislative drafts are refined away from partisan theatrics.

> "Congress in session is Congress on public exhibition, whilst Congress in its committee-rooms is Congress at work." — Woodrow Wilson, *Congressional Government*, 1885

### Constitutional Basis, Four-Fold Test & Standing vs Ad-Hoc Classification

The Constitution mentions Parliamentary Committees **indirectly** in **`Article 105`** (extending parliamentary privileges to committees and their members) and **`Article 118`** (empowering each House to frame rules of procedure), without codifying their composition, tenure, or functions. All operational details sit in the **Rules of Procedure** of the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha.

Crucially, **NOT every committee of MPs qualifies as a "Parliamentary Committee"**. Under Lok Sabha Rules (`Rule 2(1)`), a body is a **Parliamentary Committee** ONLY if it satisfies **four cumulative tests**:

1. It is **appointed or elected by the House**, or **nominated by the Speaker / Chairman**;
2. It **works under the direction of the Speaker / Chairman**;
3. It **presents its report to the House or to the Speaker / Chairman**; and
4. It has a **secretariat provided by the Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha Secretariat**.

Broadly, Parliamentary Committees divide into two great streams:

- **Standing Committees:** **Permanent** bodies reconstituted every year (or periodically) that function continuously across six functional families—(1) **Financial Committees** (3), (2) **Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs)** (24), (3) **Committees to Inquire**, (4) **Committees to Scrutinise and Control**, (5) **Committees Relating to Day-to-Day Business**, and (6) **House-Keeping / Service Committees**.
- **Ad-Hoc Committees:** **Temporary** bodies created for a specific purpose that **cease to exist the moment they complete their assigned task and submit their report**. They fall into two types: **Inquiry Committees** (such as Joint Parliamentary Committees [JPCs] investigating major scandals) and **Advisory Committees** (Select Committees of one House or Joint Committees of both Houses examining a specific Bill clause by clause).

---

### The Three Financial Committees: PAC, Estimates Committee & COPU

India's three premier financial watchdogs enforce parliamentary accountability over public spending before and after disbursement. All three share **three identical structural rules**: (i) a **one-year term** (1 May to 30 April); (ii) annual **election by Parliament via proportional representation by single transferable vote (PR-STV)** (ensuring every party receives representation proportional to its floor strength); and (iii) **strict exclusion of Union Ministers** (a Minister cannot be elected as a member, and an existing member appointed as a Minister automatically ceases to be a member!).

#### 1. Public Accounts Committee (PAC)

- **Origin & Composition:** First constituted in **1921** under the provisions of the **Government of India Act, 1919**, making it the **oldest parliamentary financial committee**. Originally 15 members from Lok Sabha, it was expanded in **1954** to **22 members: 15 elected from Lok Sabha + 7 elected from Rajya Sabha** (RS members are strictly associated on equal terms).
- **Chairperson:** Appointed by the **Lok Sabha Speaker** from among its members. Up to 1966–67, the Chair belonged to the ruling party; since **1967**, an unbroken democratic **convention** reserves the **PAC Chairmanship for an Opposition MP** (currently **K.C. Venugopal**, INC, 2024–27).
- **Mandate & Link with CAG:** Examines the **three annual audit reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)** laid before Parliament under `Article 151`—(i) Audit Report on Appropriation Accounts, (ii) Audit Report on Finance Accounts, and (iii) Audit Report on Public Undertakings. It verifies whether funds were legally available, spent strictly for the authorised purpose, and free from waste, extravagance, or corruption; examines accounts of autonomous bodies and state corporations; and **compulsorily vets all Excess Grants (`Article 115(1)(b)`) before the Lok Sabha votes to regularise them**. Because the CAG attends PAC meetings and supplies forensic audit briefs, the CAG is celebrated as the PAC's **"friend, philosopher and guide"**.
- **Limitations:** Strictly ***post-mortem*** (examines money already spent); cannot question broader government policy; cannot intervene in day-to-day administration; recommendations are **advisory, not binding** on ministries; and lacks statutory power to disallow expenditure.

#### 2. Estimates Committee

- **Origin & Composition:** Traceable to the pre-Independence **Standing Financial Committee (1921)**, the first post-Independence **Estimates Committee** was constituted in **1950** on the recommendation of Finance Minister **John Mathai**. Originally 25 members, its strength was raised in **1956** to **30 members—ALL 30 drawn EXCLUSIVELY from the Lok Sabha!** (**NO Rajya Sabha representation whatsoever**—making it both the **largest parliamentary committee** and a strictly Lower-House prerogative mirroring `Article 113(2)`!)
- **Chairperson:** Appointed by the **Speaker** from among its members—invariably from the **ruling party** (currently **Dr. Sanjay Jaiswal**, BJP, 2024–27).
- **Mandate ("Continuous Economy Committee"):** Examines the estimates included in the Budget and suggests **"economies"** in public expenditure. Uniquely—in sharp contrast to the PAC—the Estimates Committee **CAN suggest ALTERNATIVE POLICIES** to bring about efficiency and economy in administration! It also recommends improvements in administrative organisation, checks whether money is well laid out within policy limits, and suggests the form in which estimates should be presented to Parliament.
- **Limitations:** Examines budget estimates only **after they have already been voted by the Lok Sabha** (not before!); scrutinises only a few selected ministries each year; **does NOT receive expert audit assistance from the CAG**; works in a *post-mortem* mode; and its recommendations remain advisory.

#### 3. Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU)

- **Origin & Composition:** Created in **1964** on the recommendation of the **Krishna Menon Committee (1959)** to relieve the PAC and Estimates Committee of the growing burden of auditing Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs). Originally 15 members (10 LS + 5 RS), it was enlarged in **1974** to **22 members: 15 elected from Lok Sabha + 7 elected from Rajya Sabha**.
- **Chairperson:** Appointed by the **Speaker ONLY from among its Lok Sabha members** (a Rajya Sabha member cannot chair COPU; currently **Baijayant Panda**, BJP, 2024–27).
- **Mandate:** Examines the reports and accounts of PSUs (Central Public Sector Enterprises) and CAG audit reports on PSUs; evaluates whether PSU affairs are managed in accordance with **sound business principles and prudent commercial practices**; and exercises such PAC/Estimates functions over PSUs as allotted by the Speaker.
- **Limitations:** Cannot take up more than 10–12 PSUs annually; explicitly **barred from examining (a) matters of major government policy** distinct from commercial functions, **(b) matters of day-to-day administration**, and **(c) matters falling under statutory machinery created by the PSU's parent Act**; reports are advisory.

### The 24 Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs)

To bridge the widening gap between executive scale and parliamentary scrutiny, the Rules Committee of the Lok Sabha pioneered three subject-based standing committees in **1989** (Agriculture, Science & Technology, Environment & Forests). Building on their success, Parliament established **17 Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs)** in **April 1993**, expanding the network in **July 2004** to **24 DRSCs** covering every single Union Ministry and Department.

- **Administrative Jurisdiction Split:** Out of the **24 DRSCs**, **16 committees work under the Lok Sabha Speaker** (including Finance, Defence, External Affairs, Railways, Agriculture, Energy, Petroleum, Labour, Social Justice, Rural Development, Urban Affairs, Water Resources, Chemicals & Fertilizers, Communications & IT, Consumer Affairs, Coal/Mines/Steel), while **8 committees work under the Rajya Sabha Chairman**:
  1. **Commerce**;
  2. **Home Affairs**;
  3. **Education, Women, Children, Youth and Sports**;
  4. **Industry**;
  5. **Science & Technology, Environment, Forests and Climate Change**;
  6. **Transport, Tourism and Culture**;
  7. **Health and Family Welfare**; and
  8. **Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice**.
- **Composition & Mode of Selection (Classic Prelims Trap!):** Each DRSC consists of **31 members — 21 from Lok Sabha and 10 from Rajya Sabha** (raised from 45 when 17 panels expanded to 24 in 2004). Crucially, **DRSC members are NOMINATED by the Speaker (LS) and Chairman (RS)—NOT elected by PR-STV** (unlike Financial Committees!). Their term is **one year**, and **Union Ministers are strictly barred from membership** (becoming a Minister terminates membership automatically).
- **Four Core Functions:** (1) Scrutinise **Demands for Grants** of the assigned ministries during the Budget recess and submit reports (without proposing any Cut Motion); (2) examine **Bills** referred by the Speaker or Chairman; (3) review **Annual Reports** of ministries; and (4) examine **national basic long-term policy documents** presented to the Houses.
- **Boundaries & ATRs:** DRSCs **do NOT consider matters of day-to-day administration** and avoid overlapping with other parliamentary committees. Their reports are **persuasive (non-binding)**, yet ministries must table formal **Action Taken Reports (ATRs)** explaining acceptance or reasons for rejection of every recommendation.

### Ad-Hoc Committees, Consultative Committees, Parliamentary Forums & IPG

#### Ad-Hoc Committees: Inquiry JPCs & Legislative Select/Joint Committees

**Ad-hoc committees** dissolve once their report is submitted and encompass two vital bodies:

1. **Inquiry Committees (Investigative JPCs):** Constituted by a motion passed in one House and concurred in by the other (typically in a **2:1 LS:RS ratio**) to probe urgent public controversies—beginning with India's **first JPC on the Bofors Contract (1987)** chaired by B. Shankaranand, followed by **Harshad Mehta Securities Scam (1992)**, **Ketan Parekh Stock Market Scam (2001)**, **Pesticide Residues in Soft Drinks (2003)**, **2G Telecom Spectrum Allocation (2011)**, and **VVIP Chopper Deal (2013)**. A JPC can summon bureaucrats, experts, and records (summoning a Union Minister requires the Speaker's consent); its reports are recommendatory.
2. **Advisory Committees (Select & Joint Committees on Bills):** Scrutinise a specific complex Bill clause by clause. Notably, while routine **DRSC referrals** stayed low in the 18th Lok Sabha, **2024–2026 witnessed a marked revival of high-profile legislative JPCs and Select Committees**:
   - **Waqf (Amendment) Bill, 2024:** 31-member JPC chaired by **Jagdambika Pal**; submitted its report on **30 January 2025** amid Opposition dissent notes, leading to enactment of the **Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025** on **5 April 2025**;
   - **Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill, 2024 ("One Nation, One Election") & companion UT Bill:** 41-member JPC chaired by **P.P. Chaudhary**; tenure extended on **29 July 2026** until the last week of **Winter Session 2026**;
   - **Constitution (130th Amendment) Bill, 2025** (proposing automatic removal of a PM, CM, or Minister detained in custody for 30 consecutive days on serious charges): 31-member JPC chaired by **Aparajita Sarangi**, extended until **Winter Session 2026**;
   - **Income-Tax Bill, 2025** (Select Committee of Lok Sabha) and **FCRA (Amendment) Bill, 2026** (referred to a Joint Committee during Monsoon Session 2026).

#### Consultative Committees: NOT Parliamentary Committees!

**Consultative Committees** are attached to individual Union Ministries to provide an informal, non-partisan forum for policy dialogue between Ministers and MPs. However, **Consultative Committees are STRICTLY NOT Parliamentary Committees** because they fail all four tests:

- Constituted by the **Ministry of Parliamentary Affairs** (executive ministry), NOT by the House or Presiding Officer;
- **Chaired by the concerned Union Minister / Minister of State in-charge**;
- Membership is **voluntary** (minimum **10**, maximum **30 MPs** from both Houses);
- Secretarial support comes from the concerned Ministry; and they are normally constituted after every general election and **dissolve upon dissolution of the Lok Sabha**.

#### The Eight Parliamentary Forums (2005–Present)

Pioneered by Lok Sabha Speaker **Somnath Chatterjee** on **12 August 2005** (starting with Water Conservation), **eight Parliamentary Forums** currently operate to sensitise MPs to cross-cutting developmental issues through expert interactions and field workshops:

1. **Water Conservation and Management** (2005);
2. **Youth** (2006 — includes **4 sub-forums**: Sports & Youth Development, Health, Education, Employment);
3. **Children** (2006);
4. **Population and Public Health** (2006);
5. **Global Warming and Climate Change** (2008);
6. **Disaster Management** (2011);
7. **Artisans and Craftspeople** (2013); and
8. **Millennium Development Goals** (2013).

Each Forum comprises up to **31 nominated MPs (21 LS + 10 RS)** (excluding ex-officio heads—Deputy Speaker, Deputy Chairman, concerned Ministers, and concerned DRSC Chair serve as ex-officio Vice-Presidents; LS Secretary-General is Secretary). They work **independently of DRSCs** and **do NOT submit formal reports**. **Critical Prelims Exception:** The **Lok Sabha Speaker is ex-officio President of SEVEN Parliamentary Forums**—the **SOLE exception is the Parliamentary Forum on Population and Public Health**, where the **Chairman of the Rajya Sabha is ex-officio President** and the Lok Sabha Speaker is ex-officio Co-President!

#### Indian Parliamentary Group (IPG)

Established in **1949** pursuant to a motion adopted by the Constituent Assembly (Legislative) on **16 August 1948**, the **Indian Parliamentary Group (IPG)** is an **autonomous body** open to all sitting MPs and former MPs (former MPs join as *Associate Life Members* with limited privileges). The **Lok Sabha Speaker** is its ex-officio **President**, the **Deputy Speaker (LS)** and **Deputy Chairman (RS)** are ex-officio **Vice-Presidents**, and the **Lok Sabha Secretary-General** serves as ex-officio **Secretary-General**. The IPG acts as India's global parliamentary diplomacy bridge—functioning simultaneously as **(a) the National Group of the Inter-Parliamentary Union (IPU)** and **(b) the Main Branch of the Commonwealth Parliamentary Association (CPA)** in India. It sets up bilateral Parliamentary Friendship Groups, organises addresses by visiting Heads of State, and since **1995** confers the prestigious annual **Outstanding Parliamentarian Award** (through a Speaker-appointed committee).

## 8. Parliamentary Privileges, Anti-Defection Law, Position of Rajya Sabha & Reforms

A legislature can scrutinise a mighty modern executive only when its members speak without fear of litigation, remain accountable to citizens rather than corrupt inducements or political horse-trading, and operate inside an upper-and-lower-house balance backed by strong sitting calendars and technical research capacity. This final section integrates India's four interlocking institutional pillars—**Parliamentary Privileges (`Article 105`)**, the **Tenth Schedule Anti-Defection Law**, the **Constitutional Position of the Rajya Sabha**, and the **Decline & Reform of Parliament**—anchored in landmark 2024–2026 Supreme Court rulings and empirical PRS/ADR data.

### Parliamentary Privileges: Sources, Scope & Boundaries (Article 105)

**Parliamentary privileges** are special functional rights, immunities, and exemptions conferred upon both Houses of Parliament, their committees, and their members—not as elitist personal perks, but as constitutional shields guaranteeing fearless deliberation, institutional independence, and legislative dignity. **`Article 105`** codifies privileges for Parliament (mirrored verbatim for State Legislatures in **`Article 194`**).

> "Parliamentary privilege, codified in Articles 105 and 194 of the Constitution, is integral to deliberative democracy in facilitating the functioning of a parliamentary form of governance." — Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud, *Sita Soren v. Union of India*, 4 March 2024

#### Who Enjoys Parliamentary Privileges? (`Article 105(4)`)

Under **`Article 105(4)`**, privileges extend to **both Houses collectively, their committees, all MPs, Union Ministers, and the Attorney General of India**—i.e., every person constitutionally entitled to speak and participate in proceedings (`Article 88`). **Classic Prelims Trap:** Even though the **President of India** is an integral part of Parliament under `Article 79`, **the President does NOT enjoy parliamentary privileges under `Article 105`**! Presidential immunities flow separately from **`Article 361`** (similarly, State Governors stand outside `Article 194`, whereas State Advocates General are covered).

#### Five Sources & the 44th Constitutional Amendment Shift (1978)

Because Parliament has never enacted a comprehensive statute codifying privileges under `Article 105(3)`, Indian privileges derive from **five sources**: (1) **Constitutional provisions** (`Articles 105, 122, and 361A`); (2) **statutes made by Parliament** (chiefly **Section 135A of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908**); (3) **Rules of Procedure** of both Houses; (4) **parliamentary conventions**; and (5) **judicial interpretations**.

Originally, `Article 105(3)` stipulated that other uncodified privileges shall be those of the **British House of Commons** as on 26 January 1950. The **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`** deleted this colonial verbal reference (keeping the *substantive* scope identical to privileges existing immediately before Section 15 of the 44th Amendment took effect) and simultaneously inserted **`Article 361A`**, granting constitutional protection to media publication of **substantially true reports** of parliamentary and state legislative proceedings without malice—**expressly EXCLUDING secret sittings**!

#### Collective Privileges vs Individual Privileges

| Category | Core Privileges & Legal Anchor | Essential Constitutional Boundaries & Prelims Traps |
|---|---|---|
| **Collective** (Each House) | Right to **publish debates/proceedings** and prohibit outside publication (`Art. 105(2)` & `Art. 361A`) | `Art. 361A` protects substantially true media reports without malice, **EXCEPT secret sittings** |
| **Collective** | Right to **exclude strangers** and hold **secret sittings**; right to frame internal rules (`Art. 118`) | Under **`Article 122`**, courts are barred from questioning proceedings on mere **procedural irregularity**, BUT CAN review **substantive illegality, unconstitutionality, or *mala fides*** (*Raja Ram Pal*, 2007) |
| **Collective** | Power to **punish members or outsiders** for **breach of privilege** or **contempt of the House** (admonition, reprimand, imprisonment, suspension, expulsion) | Imprisonment lapses upon **prorogation**; suspension **CANNOT extend beyond the remainder of the ongoing session** (*Ashish Shelar*, 2022) |
| **Collective** | Right to receive **immediate information** of an MP's arrest, detention, conviction, imprisonment, or release; **no person (MP or outsider) can be arrested and no legal process (civil or criminal) served within Parliament precincts** without the Presiding Officer's permission | Precinct bar covers **both civil and criminal** service inside House boundaries |
| **Individual** (MPs, Ministers, AG) | **Absolute freedom of speech & vote** inside Parliament and committees (`Art. 105(1)–(2)`)—immune from civil or criminal court liability for anything said or any vote given | Subject to **`Article 121`** (barring discussion of SC/HC judges' conduct except on a removal motion) and House rules; **ZERO immunity for BRIBERY** (*Sita Soren*, 2024!) |
| **Individual** | **Freedom from arrest in CIVIL cases ONLY** during a session and **40 days before and 40 days after** a session (**Section 135A, CPC**) | **ZERO immunity in criminal cases or preventive detention!** |
| **Individual** | **Exemption from jury service** and refusal to give evidence or appear as a court witness while Parliament is in session | May waive witness immunity only with the House's permission |

*Distinguishing Breach of Privilege vs Contempt of the House:* A **breach of privilege** occurs when an individual or authority disregards or infringes a *specific recognised privilege* of a House or MP (e.g., arresting an MP in a civil case during session). **Contempt of the House** is a **much broader offence**—any act or omission that obstructs or impedes either House or its officers in performing their duties, or lowers the dignity and authority of the House, *even if no specific named privilege was violated* (for instance, shouting slogans from the visitors' gallery or casting aspersions on the impartiality of the Chair).

### Privileges vs Fundamental Rights, Sita Soren (2024) & Codification Debate

Because India rests on **constitutional supremacy** rather than British parliamentary sovereignty, uncodified privileges have collided repeatedly with **Part III Fundamental Rights** (`Articles 19(1)(a)` and `21`) and criminal law across six defining Supreme Court milestones:

1. ***M.S.M. Sharma v. Sri Krishna Sinha* (*Searchlight I*, 1959):** Held that specific constitutional privileges under `Article 194(3)` prevail over general free speech under `Article 19(1)(a)` (upholding an Assembly's ban on publishing expunged portions of a speech), while confirming that **`Article 21` (life and personal liberty)** continues to govern privilege action.
2. ***In Re: Powers, Privileges and Immunities of State Legislatures* (*Keshav Singh / UP Assembly Reference*, 1964, 7-Judge Bench):** Resolving a dramatic constitutional crisis where the UP Assembly ordered the arrest of two High Court judges who had granted bail to a citizen jailed for contempt, the Supreme Court held that legislatures **cannot punish High Court judges** for judicial acts and that general House warrants remain subject to **`Article 21`** and writ review under **`Articles 32` and `226`**.
3. ***Raja Ram Pal v. Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha* (2007, 5-Judge Bench):** Upheld Parliament's power to **expel 11 MPs** caught taking bribes in the 2005 "cash-for-query" sting, distinguishing `Article 105(3)` self-protection expulsion from `Articles 101–102` disqualifications—while firmly ruling that `Article 122` shields *procedural irregularity* only, leaving **substantive illegality, unconstitutionality, and *mala fides*** open to judicial review.
4. ***State of Kerala v. K. Ajith* (2021):** Rejecting criminal immunity for MLAs who smashed microphones and furniture inside the Kerala Assembly, the Court declared that ***"privileges and immunities are not gateways to claim exemptions from the general law of the land, particularly criminal law."***
5. ***Ashish Shelar v. Maharashtra Legislative Assembly* (January 2022):** Struck down the one-year suspension of 12 BJP MLAs as unconstitutional and irrational, ruling that suspension is purely a **"self-security"** tool to maintain immediate order and **CANNOT extend beyond the remainder of the ongoing session** (a longer suspension effectively leaves constituencies unrepresented in violation of `Article 190(4)` / `101(4)`).
6. ***Sita Soren v. Union of India* (4 March 2024):** The defining modern watershed ending criminal bribery immunity for legislators.

> [!news]
> ***Sita Soren v. Union of India* (4 March 2024, `2024 INSC 161`) — Seven-Judge Unanimous Overruling of *P.V. Narasimha Rao* (1998)**
> - **The 1998 Paradox:** In ***P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State* (1998, 3:2 JMM Bribery Case)**, the Supreme Court had ruled that MPs who accepted bribes and *actually voted* inside Parliament enjoyed `Article 105(2)` immunity from prosecution, whereas a lawmaker who took a bribe and *did not vote* (like Jharkhand MLA Sita Soren in the 2012 Rajya Sabha poll) could be prosecuted—an absurd premium on consummated corruption!
> - **Unanimous Seven-Judge Reversal:** Led by **CJI D.Y. Chandrachud**, a seven-judge Constitution Bench **unanimously overruled *P.V. Narasimha Rao* (1998)**, declaring that **bribery is NOT protected by `Articles 105(2)` or `194(2)`**!
> - **The Two-Fold Test & Completion Rule:** To claim immunity under `Articles 105/194`, an act must be (i) **tethered to the collective functioning of the House**, and (ii) **necessary** to discharge a legislator's essential duties. Bribery destroys deliberative integrity, fails both limbs completely, and **the criminal offence of bribery is COMPLETE at the exact moment a bribe is offered or accepted**, completely independent of whether or how the legislator subsequently speaks or votes—and applies equally to **Rajya Sabha elections**!

> [!debate]
> **Should Parliament Codify Privileges Under Article 105(3)?**
> - **Case FOR Codification:** Provides legal certainty and transparency for citizens, journalists, and lawmakers; harmonises privileges with **Part III Fundamental Rights** because any codified statute becomes "law" subject to **`Article 13`**; prevents arbitrary contempt notices against media critics; strongly recommended by the **NCRWC (2002)** and Press Commissions.
> - **Case AGAINST Codification:** Subjecting every internal parliamentary privilege to routine `Article 13` litigation could chill fearless floor scrutiny of ministers and invite excessive judicial intrusion into legislative autonomy; uncodified flexibility handles unforeseen emergencies (both the UK Joint Committee on Parliamentary Privilege and the **Lok Sabha Privileges Committee [2008]** rejected blanket codification).
> - **Balanced Mains Synthesis:** Preserve robust constitutional immunity for genuine legislative speech and voting (as refined by *Sita Soren*'s two-fold necessity test), while enacting a **narrowly tailored law defining "contempt of the House"** against outsiders/media and codifying fair hearing (natural justice) rules inside Privileges Committees.

### Anti-Defection Law: Tenth Schedule Architecture, Judicial Milestones & Reforms

Following the 1967 general elections, India was swept by unprincipled floor-crossing—immortalised as ***"Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram"*** after Haryana MLA Gaya Lal switched political parties **three times in a single fortnight**—destabilising dozens of State ministries. To arrest this democratic pathology, Parliament enacted the **`52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985`**, inserting the **Tenth Schedule** and amending **`Articles 101, 102(2), 190, and 191(2)`**. *(Crucial Prelims 2025 Fact: The 52nd Amendment inserted the exact phrase **"political party"** into the text of the Constitution for the very first time—subsequently echoed in `Articles 75(1B), 164(1B), and 361B`!)*

> "The evil of political defections has been a matter of national concern. If it is not combated, it is likely to undermine the very foundations of our democracy and the principles which sustain it." — Statement of Objects and Reasons, Constitution (52nd Amendment) Bill, 23 January 1985

#### Tightening via the 91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003

Because original **Paragraph 3** exempted "splits" by **one-third** of a legislature party—turning retail defections into orchestrated wholesale coups—Parliament enacted the **`91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003`** (based on the Dinesh Goswami and Law Commission 170th Reports), introducing three decisive reforms:

1. **Deleted Paragraph 3 (One-Third Split Exception):** Splits by one-third factions are **no longer protected** from disqualification!
2. **15% Cap on Councils of Ministers (`Articles 75(1A)` & `164(1A)`):** Capped the total size of the Union Council of Ministers (including PM) at **15% of Lok Sabha strength** and State Councils (including CM) at **15% of Assembly strength** (with a minimum of 12 in smaller states), removing the ministerial patronage fuel behind defections.
3. **Bar on Ministerial & Remunerative Posts (`Articles 75(1B), 164(1B), 361B`):** Barred any legislator disqualified under the Tenth Schedule from being appointed as a **Minister** or holding any **remunerative political post** (wholly/partially government-funded board/corporation post) until the expiry of their term OR until they are **re-elected**, whichever is earlier!

#### Four Grounds of Disqualification & Two Exceptions (`Paragraphs 2, 4 & 5`)

| Member Category / Exception | Exact Tenth Schedule Rule | Binding Precedents & High-Yield Prelims Traps |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Party Member — Voluntary Exit** (`Para 2(1)(a)`) | **Voluntarily gives up membership** of the political party on whose ticket they were elected | **Wider than formal resignation!** May be inferred from conduct inside or outside the House—e.g., publicly accompanying rival leaders to the Governor or campaigning for a rival party (***Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India*, 1994**). An **expelled member** deemed "unattached" does not lose their seat automatically, **but stands disqualified if they join another party** (***G. Viswanathan*, 1996**) |
| **2. Party Member — Whip Defiance** (`Para 2(1)(b)`) | **Votes or abstains from voting** in the House contrary to any **direction (whip)** issued by their political party without prior permission, AND such act is **NOT condoned by the party within 15 days** | Applies strictly to votes inside the House; **does NOT apply** to **Presidential / Vice-Presidential elections** (secret ballot—whips barred) or automatic whip disqualification in **Rajya Sabha elections** (*Kuldip Nayar*, 2006) |
| **3. Independent Member** (`Para 2(2)`) | Disqualified if they **join ANY political party** after election | May extend *outside support* to a government—or even become a Minister—without formally joining a political party |
| **4. Nominated Member** (`Para 2(3)`) | Disqualified if they join any political party **AFTER the expiry of six months** from the date they take their seat | Explicitly granted a **6-month grace window** after taking their seat to join a political party cleanly! |
| **Exception A — Merger** (`Para 4`) | **NO disqualification** if the original political party **merges** with another party AND **not less than two-thirds (≥ 2/3rd)** of the legislature party members agree to the merger | Protects **BOTH** the ≥2/3rd majority that merges AND any dissenting minority who choose to function as a separate group! *(1/3rd split deleted in 2003!)* |
| **Exception B — Presiding Officers** (`Para 5`) | **Exempt** if elected **Speaker / Deputy Speaker** (LS/Assembly) or **Deputy Chairman** (RS) / **Chairman & Deputy Chairman** (Council) voluntarily resigns from their party and does not join another party, or rejoins their original party after demitting office | Designed to nurture British-style neutrality of the Chair (rarely exercised in India) |

#### Adjudicating Authority, Judicial Review & 2023–2026 Landmark Rulings

Under **Paragraph 6**, any question regarding Tenth Schedule disqualification is decided by the **Chairman (RS)** or **Speaker (LS)**—**NOT the President or ECI** (contrast sharply with `Article 103`!). Because Speakers in India remain active ruling-party politicians, chronic partisan delays—sitting on disqualification petitions for years until an Assembly's term expires—provoked four landmark Supreme Court interventions:

```flow
# Tenth Schedule Adjudication & Judicial Review Pathway
Defection Trigger (Para 2) | Voluntary Exit · Whip Defiance (Uncondoned >15 Days) · Independent Joining · Nominated Joining >6 Months
Petition Filed Before Speaker / Chairman (Para 6) | Presiding Officer Acts as a Constitutional TRIBUNAL (Kihoto Hollohan, 1992) — NOT President/ECI!
Mandatory 3-Month Decision Timeline | Keisham Meghachandra (2020) & Padi Kaushik Reddy (July 2025): Adverse inference on delaying MLAs; Contempt for inaction || Political Party Appoints Whip | Subhash Desai (May 2023): Organisational party (NOT rebel MLA faction) issues binding whip; Nabam Rebia sent to 7-Judge Bench
Post-Decision Judicial Review (Arts. 136, 226, 227) | Para 7 Ouster Struck Down (Kihoto Hollohan) — Reviewable for Mala Fides, Perversity, Constitutional Breach, or Denial of Natural Justice
```

1. ***Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu and Others* (1992, 5-Judge Bench, 3:2):** Upheld the Tenth Schedule's constitutional validity (does not violate Basic Structure or `Article 105` free speech), **but STRUCK DOWN Paragraph 7** (which had barred court jurisdiction without State ratification under `Article 368(2)` proviso). Held that the Speaker/Chairman acts as a **Tribunal** whose **final order** is subject to judicial review under **`Articles 136, 226, and 227`** on grounds of *mala fides*, perversity, constitutional violation, or breach of natural justice—ordinarily **barring judicial intervention before the Speaker decides** (quia timet bar).
2. ***Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Hon'ble Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly* (January 2020):** Confronting prolonged Speaker inaction, a three-judge Bench (Justice R.F. Nariman) ruled that absent exceptional reasons, **Speakers must decide disqualification petitions within a reasonable period of THREE MONTHS**, and explicitly urged Parliament to amend the Constitution to replace the Speaker with an **independent permanent tribunal** headed by a retired Supreme Court judge or retired High Court Chief Justice.
3. ***Subhash Desai v. Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra* (11 May 2023, 5-Judge Bench):** Resolving the Maharashtra Shiv Sena split crisis, a unanimous five-judge Constitution Bench (CJI D.Y. Chandrachud) drew a sharp legal line between the **"political party"** (organisational wing) and the **"legislature party"** (elected MLAs), holding that **the political party—NOT a legislative faction—possesses authority to appoint the whip and legislature party leader**! Critically, it referred the 2016 five-judge ruling in ***Nabam Rebia*** (which had barred a Speaker facing a removal notice from deciding Tenth Schedule petitions—a loophole rebel MLAs exploited by filing sham removal notices to freeze defection hearings) to a **seven-judge Constitution Bench**, which remained **pending as of November 2026**.
4. ***Padi Kaushik Reddy v. State of Telangana* (31 July 2025, `2025 INSC 912`) & March 2026 Follow-Up:** Addressing more than a year of inaction by the Telangana Speaker on disqualification petitions against **10 Bharat Rashtra Samithi (BRS) MLAs** who crossed over to the ruling Congress in 2024, a Supreme Court Bench of **CJI B.R. Gavai and Justice A.G. Masih** overturned a High Court Division Bench order and issued stern binding directions: (i) the Speaker acts as a tribunal and **cannot frustrate the Tenth Schedule through deliberate delay**; (ii) directed the Speaker to **conclude all 10 petitions within 3 months**, empowering the Chair to draw **adverse inferences** against MLAs using dilatory tactics; and (iii) reiterated Parliament's urgent duty to reconsider entrusting defection adjudication to partisan Presiding Officers. Following contempt proceedings and a firm judicial ultimatum, the Speaker decided all 10 petitions, leading the Court to **close contempt proceedings on 12 March 2026**—though a Speaker dismissal was subsequently overturned on merits by the Telangana High Court where an MLA had even contested Lok Sabha polls on a rival ticket!

#### Critique & Committee-Backed Reforms of the Tenth Schedule

While curbing retail single-MP crossovers, four structural flaws blunt the Tenth Schedule: (1) **stifling representative conscience**, reducing MPs into rubber-stamp voting machines unable to voice constituency interests on ordinary Bills; (2) **rewarding wholesale defections** via Paragraph 4's ≥2/3rd merger clause and "resort politics"; (3) the **resignation-and-reelection bypass** (Karnataka 2019, Madhya Pradesh 2020), where rebel MLAs resign to collapse a ministry and immediately re-contest on rival tickets (in ***Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil v. Hon'ble Speaker, Karnataka*, 2019**, the Supreme Court upheld disqualification despite resignation, yet struck down the Speaker's bar preventing disqualified MLAs from re-contesting until the Assembly's full term expired); and (4) **partisan Speaker bias and delay**.

Authoritative reform proposals—by the **Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990)**, **Law Commission 170th Report (1999) & 255th Report (2015)**, **NCRWC (2002)**, and **Second ARC (2007)**—converge on four surgical fixes: (a) **restrict whip-triggered disqualification strictly to Confidence/No-Confidence Motions, Money Bills, and Financial Bills** so MPs can debate and vote freely on routine legislation; (b) **transfer adjudication** from Presiding Officers to an **independent permanent judicial tribunal** (*Keisham Meghachandra*) or the **President/Governor acting on binding ECI opinion**; (c) **codify a strict 3-month statutory timeline**; and (d) **tighten the 2/3rd merger loophole** and bar resigning rebels from ministerial office for the rest of the term.

### Constitutional Position of the Rajya Sabha: Equal, Unequal & Four Exclusive Powers

Evaluating India's Upper House requires avoiding two caricatures: the Rajya Sabha is **neither as weak as the British House of Lords** (which can merely delay non-money Bills by one year) **nor as powerful as the American Senate** (which holds exclusive treaty-ratification and executive-confirmation powers). Its constitutional stature falls cleanly into **three distinct spheres**:

#### 1. Equal Status with the Lok Sabha (11 Co-Equal Domains)

1. Introduction and passage of **Ordinary Bills** (`Article 107`);
2. Introduction and passage of **Constitution Amendment Bills** (`Article 368` — separate special majority in EACH House; zero Lok Sabha override!);
3. Introduction (for Cat. II) and passage/amendment/rejection of **Financial Bills (Category I & Category II)** (`Article 117`);
4. Election and **impeachment of the President** (`Articles 54 & 61`);
5. Election of the **Vice-President** (`Article 66`);
6. Making recommendations to the President for the **removal of the Chief Justice and Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, Chief Election Commissioner, and CAG**;
7. Approval of **Ordinances** issued by the President (`Article 123`);
8. Approval of **all three types of Emergencies** (`Articles 352, 356, and 360`);
9. Selection of **Union Ministers including the Prime Minister** (who may belong to either House);
10. Consideration of reports of constitutional bodies (**Finance Commission, UPSC, CAG, NCSC, NCST, NCBC**); and
11. Enlargement of the jurisdiction of the **Supreme Court** and the **UPSC**.

#### 2. Unequal Status (Areas of Lok Sabha Primacy)

1. **Money Bills (`Articles 109–110`):** Introduced ONLY in LS; Speaker's certificate final; RS cannot amend or reject (14-day recommendatory cap);
2. **Financial Bill Category I (`Article 117(1)`):** Can be introduced ONLY in LS (though RS is co-equal thereafter);
3. **Joint Sitting (`Article 108`):** Presided over by the LS Speaker; LS's 543 numerical strength overwhelmingly outweighs RS's 245;
4. **Union Budget (`Article 113(2)`):** RS can discuss the Budget, **but CANNOT vote on Demands for Grants** or move Cut Motions;
5. **Discontinuance of National Emergency (`Article 352(7)–(8)`):** A resolution to **discontinue** a National Emergency can be passed **ONLY by the Lok Sabha** (added by the 44th Amendment);
6. **Ministerial Accountability (`Article 75(3)`):** The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible **ONLY to the Lok Sabha**—hence RS cannot remove the government via a **No-Confidence Motion**, **Censure Motion**, or **Adjournment Motion**, nor topple it by amending the Motion of Thanks.

#### 3. Four Exclusive Special Powers of the Rajya Sabha (Federal & Constitutional Safeguards!)

Because the Rajya Sabha represents India's federal States and never dissolves, the Constitution vests it with **four exclusive powers NOT enjoyed by the Lok Sabha**:

1. **Authorising Parliament to Legislate on a State List Subject (`Article 249`):** By a resolution supported by **not less than two-thirds of members present and voting**, the Rajya Sabha can declare it necessary or expedient in the **national interest** for Parliament to make laws on any matter in the **State List** (remains in force for up to **one year**, renewable any number of times by one year at a time).
2. **Creating New All-India Services (`Article 312`):** By a resolution supported by **not less than two-thirds of members present and voting**, the Rajya Sabha alone can authorise Parliament to create one or more new **All-India Services** (including an All-India Judicial Service) common to the Union and the States in the national interest.
3. **Sole Initiation of Vice-President's Removal (`Article 67(b)`):** A resolution for the **removal of the Vice-President of India** can be moved **ONLY in the Rajya Sabha** (passed by RS effective majority + agreed to by LS simple majority).
4. **Keeping Emergencies Alive When Lok Sabha Is Dissolved (`Articles 352, 356, 360`):** If a Proclamation of National Emergency (`Art. 352`), President's Rule (`Art. 356`), or Financial Emergency (`Art. 360`) is issued or awaits parliamentary approval at a time when the **Lok Sabha stands dissolved**, the **Rajya Sabha ALONE** can approve the Proclamation—keeping it constitutionally valid until **30 days from the first sitting of the newly reconstituted Lok Sabha**!

Notice that `Arts. 249` and `312` require **2/3rd of members present and voting** (NOT 2/3rd of total membership!), whereas `Art. 67(b)` requires an **effective majority** in RS!

### Decline of Parliament & Comprehensive Mains Reform Blueprint

Across GS Paper II Mains, examiners consistently probe whether India's Parliament is eroding from a supreme deliberative check into a hurried rubber stamp for executive bills. Verified multi-decade empirical tracking by **PRS Legislative Research (up to Monsoon Session 2026)** and the **Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR, June 2024)** exposes six measurable institutional stresses:

| Parliamentary Health Indicator | Historical Benchmark (1950s–2000s) | Verified Recent Reality (PRS / ADR, up to Nov 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| **Annual Sitting Days** | **135 days/year** in 1st LS (677 total sittings); 116 days/yr in 2nd LS | Fell to **55 days/year** in **17th LS** (274 total—**shortest full-term LS since 1952**; 11 of 15 sessions adjourned early); **62 days in 2025** |
| **Bills Referred to Committees** | **60%** (14th LS); **71%** (15th LS) | Plunged to **25%** (16th LS) → **16%** (17th LS) → only **21%** in **18th LS up to Monsoon 2026** (routine DRSC referrals rare) |
| **Speed & Depth of Law-Making** | Extended floor & clause-by-clause scrutiny | **58%** of 17th LS Bills passed within 2 weeks; **35%** with **<1 hour** of LS debate; in **Monsoon 2026**, **9 of 11 LS Bills** passed with only the Minister speaking |
| **Question Hour & Floor Productivity** | Core daily accountability window | **17th LS:** Question Hour functioned **60% (LS) / 52% (RS)**; only **24%** of starred questions answered orally in LS. **Monsoon 2026:** LS worked **15%** (QH **1%**!), RS **33%** |
| **Budget Guillotining** | Broad ministry-by-ministry voting | **89%** of voted expenditure guillotined in 2024–25; **77%** in **Budget Session 2026** (only Agriculture & Railways of 5 listed ministries debated) |
| **Criminalisation & Wealth (LS)** | 23% (2004), 30% (2009), 34% (2014) criminal cases | **18th LS (June 2024, ADR):** **251 of 543 MPs (46%)** with declared criminal cases; **170 (31%)** with **serious criminal charges**; **93%** crorepatis |

#### Five Interlocking Structural Causes Behind Parliamentary Decline

1. **Chronic Disruptions & Mass Suspensions:** Planned well-of-the-House protests routinely wipe out Question Hour—paradoxically hurting the Opposition's sharpest accountability weapon. In response, Chairs have increasingly deployed **LS Rules 373, 374, and 374A** (`Rule 374A`, inserted 2001, triggers automatic 5-sitting suspension without a House motion) and **RS Rules 255–256**. The 17th Lok Sabha recorded **206 suspension instances**, peaking in **December 2023 (Winter Session)** when **146 MPs (100 LS + 46 RS)** were suspended in one week while major criminal-law Bills passed—followed by 8 fresh suspensions in February 2026.
2. **Committee Bypass, Short Tenures & Money Bill Friction:** Discretionary Bill referral lets controversial legislation skip DRSC expertise; annual **1-year DRSC tenures** prevent MPs from building domain mastery; attendance often hovers around 40–50%; ATRs are rarely debated on the floor; and certifying non-tax laws as Money Bills remains pending before the ***Rojer Mathew* 7-judge Bench**.
3. **Ordinance Raj:** Heavy resort to executive law-making under `Articles 123` and `213` (45 Union ordinances in 16th LS, ~35 in 17th LS, and **127 state ordinances in 2025** alone) persists despite Supreme Court strictures in ***D.C. Wadhwa* (1987)** (re-promulgation is a *"fraud on the Constitution"*) and ***Krishna Kumar Singh* (2017, 7-judge Bench)** (mandatory laying; unapproved ordinances create no automatic enduring rights).
4. **Institutional Vacancies & Vanishing Private Member Initiative:** Leaving the **Deputy Speaker (`Article 93`)** vacant for **over seven years (since May 2019)** weakens bipartisan House leadership, while zero Private Member's Bills enacted since **1970** illustrates executive monopoly over the legislative timetable.
5. **Criminalisation & Research Capacity Deficit:** Nearly half of Lok Sabha MPs face pending criminal cases, while individual MPs lack dedicated legal, economic, and fiscal research staff comparable to the **US Congressional Budget Office (CBO)** or Congressional Research Service.

#### Comprehensive Six-Point Reform Roadmap (Way Forward)

1. **Statutory Minimum Sitting Calendar (`NCRWC, 2002`):** Mandate at least **120 sitting days/year for Lok Sabha and 100 for Rajya Sabha** on a predictable annual schedule rather than ad-hoc executive summoning.
2. **Institutionalise UK-Style "Opposition Business Days":** Allot fixed days each session (e.g., 15–20 days/year or one day weekly) where the **Opposition sets the agenda** for urgent motions and debates—channelling protest from the Well back into structured deliberation.
3. **Make Pre-Legislative & Committee Scrutiny the Default:** Grant **statutory backing to the 2014 PLCP** (30-day multi-lingual public drafts + mandatory **Legislative Impact Assessments**—fiscal, federal, environmental, rights); make **DRSC referral mandatory** for all major Bills unless waived by recorded reasoned vote; extend **DRSC tenures to 2–2.5 years** with published attendance records and mandatory floor debates on top ministries' Demands and PAC/DRSC **Action Taken Reports (ATRs)**.
4. **Depoliticise the Chair & Overhaul the Tenth Schedule:** Move toward the British non-partisan Speaker convention (**V.S. Page Committee, 1967**); promptly fill the **Deputy Speaker** post from the Opposition; shift Tenth Schedule adjudication to an **independent permanent tribunal** (*Keisham Meghachandra*) or **President/Governor on binding ECI opinion** (**2nd ARC / Law Commission 255th Report**); and confine whips to confidence and financial votes (**Dinesh Goswami, 1990 / Law Commission 170th Report**).
5. **Build Independent Fiscal & Research Capacity:** Establish a non-partisan **Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO)** to provide neutral macroeconomic forecasts and independent Bill costings, paired with professional legislative research fellowships for MPs.
6. **Enforce Decorum & Clean Electoral Politics:** Pair an enforceable **MP Code of Conduct** (respecting *Ashish Shelar* limits on suspensions while enforcing zero tolerance for legislative vandalism under *K. Ajith*) with fast-track MP/MLA special criminal courts and transparent campaign finance.

## 1. Structure, Composition, Delimitation & Duration (Articles 79–83, 330–334A)

- **Three Organs (`Art. 79`):** **President + Rajya Sabha + Lok Sabha** (Hindi names adopted 1954). President is NOT a member yet integral (assent `Art. 111`, sessions/dissolution `Art. 85`, Ordinances `Art. 123` — unlike US President).
- **Rajya Sabha (`Art. 80`):** Max **250** (238+12); Current **245** = **225 States + 8 UTs** (**J&K 4** [Oct 2025], **Delhi 3**, **Puducherry 1**; 5 UTs unrepresented) + **12 Nominated**. Population-based (**4th Schedule**: UP 31; Goa/Sikkim/6 NE states 1 each — unlike US Senate's 2/state).
- **RS Polls & Nominations:** Indirect by **elected MLAs** via **PR-STV**. **2003 RPA Amend:** **open ballot** (party MLAs show party agent; independents barred) + no state domicile — upheld in ***Kuldip Nayar* (2006)**. **NO NOTA** (***Shailesh Parmar*, 2018**). **12 Nominated (`Art. 80(3)`):** ONLY **4 fields** (**literature, science, art, social service**) — **NO cooperative movement** (MLCs only, `Art. 171(5)`!).
- **Lok Sabha (`Arts. 81–82`):** Max **550** (530+20 — **`104th Amend, 2019`** ended 2 Anglo-Indian seats under `Art. 331`). Current **543** = **524 States + 19 UTs** (Delhi 7, J&K 5, DNH&DD 2, A&N/Chandigarh/Ladakh/Lakshadweep/Puducherry 1 each). Direct **FPTP**, universal suffrage (`Art. 326`; `61st Amend`: 21→18). `Art. 81(2)` exempts states **<60 lakh** (`31st Amend 1973`).
- **Delimitation & Reservations (`Arts. 82, 330–334A`):** 4 Commissions (**1952/1962/1972/2002**; court-immune `Art. 329(a)`). **`42nd`/`84th` Amends** froze State totals at **1971 Census** till post-2026; **`87th` Amend** internal redrawing on **2001 Census**. **SC/ST (`Art. 330`):** **131** (**84 SC + 47 ST** till **2030**; joint electorates; NO RS quota). **`106th Amend, 2023`** (`Arts. 330A/332A/334A/239AA`: 33% women in LS/Assemblies/Delhi, NOT RS/Councils, 15 yrs) **notified 16 Apr 2026**, linked to **Census 2027** (House Listing 1 Apr 2026; Enumeration Feb 2027; ref 1 Mar 2027). **`131st Bill, 2026`** (850 LS seats, 2011 Census) **defeated 17 Apr 2026 (298–230)**. **`129th Bill` (ONOE)** with 41-MP JPC (P.P. Chaudhary) till Winter 2026.
- **Duration (`Art. 83`):** **RS permanent** (1/3rd retire every 2 yrs; **6-yr term is in RPA 1951 s. 154, NOT Constitution!**). **LS 5 yrs** from first sitting; `Art. 352` Emergency extends **1 yr at a time**, max **6 mos** post-Emergency.

## 2. Membership: Qualifications, Disqualifications, Vacation & Oath (Articles 84, 88, 99, 101–104)

- **Qualifications (`Art. 84`, RPA 1951):** Citizen; 3rd Schedule oath (`16th Amend 1963`); age **30 (RS) / 25 (LS)**; elector in **ANY parliamentary constituency** in India (both Houses). SC/ST can contest reserved or general seats.
- **Disqualifications (`Arts. 102–103`, RPA 1951):** `102(1)`: **Office of Profit** (undefined; Ministers exempt via **Explanation to `102(1)`**; 1959 Exemption Act; ***Jaya Bachchan* 2006**: *capability* of pecuniary gain suffices), court-declared unsound mind, undischarged insolvent, non-citizen, or **RPA 1951** (`s. 8A` corrupt practices, **`s. 8(3)` ≥2-yr conviction = sentence + 6 yrs**, `s. 10A` accounts, `s. 9A` govt contract, `s. 10` ≥25% PSU, `s. 9` corruption dismissal — **preventive detention does NOT disqualify**). ***Lily Thomas* (2013)** struck down `s. 8(4)` → **immediate disqualification** unless **conviction** itself is stayed (*Lok Prahari*, 2018).
- **Deciding Authority Split (Prelims 2025):** **`Art. 102(1)` / post-election RPA** → **President bound by ECI (`Art. 103`)** (NOT CoM); **Tenth Schedule (`102(2)`)** → **Speaker/Chairman**; **Pre-election** → **High Court** election petition.
- **Vacation (`Art. 101`) — 4 Double-Membership Rules:** Both Houses → **10 days** else **RS** lapses (`s. 68`); Sitting MP wins other House → **old House** lapses **instantly** (`s. 69`); 2 seats same House → **14 days** else **BOTH** lapse (`s. 70`); Parliament + State → **14 days** else **Parliament seat** lapses (`101(2)` + 1950 Rules). Resignation (`101(3)(b)`): **`33rd Amend 1974`** lets Chair reject non-voluntary/non-genuine resignation. Absence (`101(4)`): **60 days** unpermitted (excl. prorogation / >4 consecutive day adjournment). Also void election, expulsion (*Raja Ram Pal*), President/VP/Governor election.
- **Oath, Penalty & `Art. 88`:** Oath (`Art. 99`) before President/appointee; **₹500/day (`Art. 104`)**; salaries by Parliament (`Art. 106`; pensions 1954/1976 Act). **`Art. 88`:** Ministers & **AG** (NOT Solicitor General) speak in either House/joint sitting/named committees, voting ONLY where members (AG never votes).

## 3. Presiding Officers, Leaders, Whips & Secretariat (Articles 89–98)

- **Speaker (`Arts. 93–96`):** Origin **GoI Act 1919** (1921 Whyte/Sinha; 1925 **Vithalbhai Patel**; 1952 **G.V. Mavalankar**). Election date by **President**; **4 contested polls**: 1952 (Mavalankar vs More), 1967 (Sanjiva Reddy vs Viswanatham), 1976 (Bali Ram Bhagat vs Joshi), **26 June 2024** (**Om Birla** vs K. Suresh). **Survives LS dissolution** till new LS first sitting (`Art. 94`); **NO constitutional duty to resign party (Prelims 2025!)**; resigns to **Deputy Speaker**. Removed by **effective majority + 14-day notice** (50 MPs in Rules); **`Art. 96`:** cannot preside, **votes in FIRST INSTANCE — NO casting vote! (Prelims 2024)**. **11 Mar 2026:** Removal motion vs **Om Birla** (118 MPs) **defeated** (**Jagdambika Pal** presided). Casting vote (`Art. 100`), Money Bills (`110(3)`), Joint Sitting (`108`), Tenth Schedule, chairs **BAC/Rules/General Purposes**, Rank 6 with CJI, CFI-charged salary (`112(3)(b)`).
- **Deputy Speaker, Panel & Pro Tem (`Arts. 93–95`):** **Deputy Speaker** date by **Speaker**; not subordinate; resigns to Speaker; **auto-chairs committees joined**; **vacant since May 2019** (17th & 18th LS as of Nov 2026). **Panel of Chairpersons (max 10):** presides **ONLY on ABSENCE, NEVER on VACANCY** (President appoints under `Art. 95(1)`). **Speaker pro tem (`95(1)`):** appointed/sworn by President (**June 2024:** 7-term consecutive **Bhartruhari Mahtab** over 8-term non-consecutive K. Suresh).
- **RS Chairman & Deputy Chairman (`Arts. 89–92`):** Ex-officio Chairman = **VP** (**C.P. Radhakrishnan**, 12 Sept 2025); NOT RS member; removed only via `Art. 67(b)`; **`Art. 92`: NO vote at all during removal**; no Money Bill/Joint Sitting role. **Deputy Chairman:** **17 Apr 2026: Harivansh Narayan Singh** won 3rd term — **first nominated MP ever** as RS Deputy Chairman.
- **Leaders, Whip & Secretariat (`Art. 98`):** **Leader of House** = Rules (**Modi** LS, **Nadda** RS). **LoP** = statutory (**1977 Act**, Cabinet rank, 10% convention [55 LS/25 RS], first 1969; **Rahul Gandhi** LS [9 Jun 2024], **Mallikarjun Kharge** RS [26 Jun 2026]; CVC/CIC/Lokpal/CBI/NHRC/EC panels). **Whip** = **pure convention** (NOT in Constitution/Rules/statute). **`Art. 98`:** Independent Secretariats (Secretary-General).

## 4. Sessions, Conduct of Business, Motions & Devices (Articles 85–87, 100, 118–120)

- **Sessions & Endings (`Art. 85`):** **President** summons; **max 6-month gap** (no constitutional minimum sitting days; Budget/Monsoon/Winter; **lame-duck session**). **Adjournment / *sine die*** (Chair; ends sitting; no lapse) vs **Prorogation** (President; ends session; **Bills do NOT lapse `107(3)`**, notices lapse; Monsoon 2026 *sine die* 13 Aug 2026) vs **Dissolution** (President; ends LS only; specified Bills lapse).
- **Quorum, Language & Special Address:** **Quorum (`100(3)`)** = **1/10th incl. Chair** (**55 LS, 25 RS**; States `189(3)` = 10 or 1/10th greater). **Language (`120`):** Hindi/English + mother-tongue permission. **Special Address (`87`):** Post-election & annual first session; **Motion of Thanks** LS defeat fells govt (RS amendments in 1980/89/2001/15/16 do not).
- **Question Hour & Zero Hour:** **Starred** (green, `*`, oral + supplementaries), **Unstarred** (white, written), **Short Notice** (pink, `<10 days`, oral, Minister consent), **Private Members** (yellow, Rule 40). **Zero Hour (1962):** Indian innovation, **NOT in Rules**.
- **Motions & Resolutions:** Substantive / Substitute / Subsidiary. **4 Closures:** Simple, Compartments, **Kangaroo** (key clauses only), **Guillotine** (undiscussed voted wholesale). **Calling Attention (1954):** Indian innovation **IN Rules** (no vote/censure). **LS-ONLY Censure Tools:** **Adjournment** (50 MPs, ≥2.5 hrs, bars privilege/sub judice), **No-Confidence** (`Art. 75(3)` + LS Rule 198, 50 MPs, **no reasons**, entire CoM, forces resignation; V.P. Singh/Deve Gowda/Vajpayee fell on Confidence votes), **Censure** (**reasons required**, individual/group/whole CoM, no legal resignation). **Point of Order:** suspends business, no debate, Chair final. **Half-an-Hour** & **Short Duration (1953, Rule 193/176, 2 hrs):** no vote. **Special Mention (RS) / Rule 377 (LS)**. **Resolutions:** ALL are substantive motions & **MUST be voted upon** (Private/Govt/Statutory). **Youth Parliament:** 1962 Whips' Conf. → 1966–67 Min. of Parl. Affairs → renamed 1972.

## 5. Legislative Procedure, Kinds of Bills, Lapsing & Joint Sitting (Articles 107–111, 117)

- **Public vs Private Bills & PLCP:** Public (Ministers, **7 days**) vs Private (non-minister MPs, **1 month**, alternate Fridays). Only **14 Private Member's Bills** enacted — **last 1970** (Anand Narain Mulla). **PLCP (Feb 2014):** Non-binding **30-day** consultation; **75% (227/301)** bypassed it (PRS).
- **Ordinary Bill (`Arts. 107, 111`) — 5 Stages:** First Reading (intro + Gazette) → Second Reading (General → Committee → Clauses) → Third Reading (overall vote, **no substantive amendments**, simple majority) → Second House (4 options; >6 mos = deadlock) → President (Assent / Absolute / Suspensive return once — mandatory assent on repassage / Pocket).
- **Money Bill (`Arts. 109–110`):** **"ONLY"** `Art. 110(1)` (a)–(g) (taxes, Union borrowing/guarantees, CFI/Contingency flows, appropriation, charged sums, CFI/Public Account receipt or **Union/State audit**, incidental). **NOT Money Bill (`110(2)`):** **fines, licence/service fees, local taxes**. **LS ONLY**, **Minister ONLY**, **prior Pres. rec.**, **Speaker's certificate "final"**, **RS 14-day recommend-only cap**, **NO Joint Sitting**, **NO Presidential return**. ***Puttaswamy* (2018, 4:1)** vs Chandrachud dissent; ***Rojer Mathew* (Nov 2019)** sent Art. 110 / Speaker finality to **7-judge Bench** (**pending Nov 2026**).
- **Financial Bills & `Art. 368`:** **All Money Bills are Financial Bills, NOT vice versa.** **Fin I (`117(1)`):** Art. 110 + general; **LS only + prior Pres. rec.** (except tax cut/abolition); otherwise Ordinary Bill. **Fin II (`117(3)`):** CFI spending, no Art. 110; **Either House, NO rec. at intro**, **MANDATORY Pres. rec. at CONSIDERATION stage!** **`Art. 368`:** Either House, no rec., **special majority separately**, **NO Joint Sitting**, half-States if federal, **mandatory Presidential assent** (`24th Amend`).
- **6 Dissolution Lapsing Rules (`Arts. 107, 108(5)`):** **Prorogation NEVER lapses Bills (`107(3)`).** On LS dissolution: **LAPSES** if (1) pending in LS or (2) passed by LS & pending in RS. **SURVIVES** if (3) pending in RS only, (4) Joint Sitting notified before dissolution (`108(5)`), (5) awaiting assent, or (6) returned by President (`111`). LS Select Committee lapses; RS Select Committee & Govt Assurances survive.
- **Joint Sitting (`Art. 108`) & Delegated Law:** Rejection, amendment disagreement, or **>6 mos** (excl. prorogation / >4-day adjournment). **ONLY Ordinary & Financial (I & II)** — **NEVER Money or `Art. 368`**. **Chair (`118(4)`):** **Speaker → Dy Speaker → Dy Chairman (RS) → chosen MP** (**VP/RS Chairman NEVER presides!**). Quorum 1/10th combined; LS Rules; simple majority. **3 instances:** **1961** Dowry (Ayyangar), **1978** Banking Service Commission Repeal (Hegde), **2002** POTA (**Dy Speaker P.M. Sayeed**). **States have NO Joint Sitting** (`Art. 197`). ***In Re Delhi Laws* (1951):** cannot delegate **"essential legislative function"**.

## 6. The Union Budget, Financial Control & Public Funds (Articles 112–116, 265–267)

- **Anchors & 2017 Reforms:** **`Art. 265`** (no tax without law); **`Art. 266(3)`** (no CFI draw without Appropriation Act); **`Art. 112`** (**Annual Financial Statement** — word **"Budget"** absent!). **2017:** **1 Feb** date; **Rail Budget merged** (**Bibek Debroy 2015**, ending **1924 Acworth** split); **Plan vs Non-Plan ended**.
- **Charged vs Voted (`Arts. 112(3), 113`):** **Charged** = **non-votable YET discussable** (President, LS/RS chairs, Union debt, SC judges, **HC judges' PENSIONS ONLY** [salaries on **State CFI `202(3)(d)`**!], CAG, SC/CAG/UPSC office costs, court decrees, Art. 275 grants). **Voted** = Demands for Grants (**LS ONLY**, President's recommendation `113(3)`).
- **6 Budget Stages:** Presentation (1 Feb, no debate) → General Discussion (both Houses, no vote) → **24 DRSCs** (3–4 wk recess) → Demands Voting (**LS ONLY**; Cut Motions → **Guillotine**: **89%** 2024–25, **77% Budget 2026**) → **Appropriation Bill (`114`)** (no amount/destination edits, Money Bill) → **Finance Bill (`117/110`)** (**75 days** under 1931 Act; may cut, not raise tax).
- **Cut Motions (LS ONLY; passage = resignation) & 7 Grants (`Arts. 115–116`):** **Policy** (to **₹1**), **Economy** (specific sum), **Token** (by **₹100**). **Vote on Account (`116(1)(a)`):** advance ~1/6th (spending only vs **Interim Budget**); **Supplementary (`115(1)(a)`)** shortfall; **Additional (`115(1)(a)`)** new service; **Excess (`115(1)(b)`)** past overspend (**PAC vetting MANDATORY first!**); **Vote of Credit (`116(1)(b)`)** "blank cheque"; **Exceptional (`116(1)(c)`)**; **Token Grant** (**₹1** reappropriation for new service).
- **3 Public Funds (`Arts. 266–267`):** **CFI (`266(1)`)** prior Appropriation Act mandatory; **Public Account (`266(2)`)** banker/trustee, **executive action (NO vote!)**; **Contingency Fund (`267(1)` + 1950 Act)** President's imprest, **₹30,000 cr** (**Finance Act 2021**; DEA Secy / 40% Expenditure Secy), **post facto** authorisation & replenishment.

- **4-Fold Test & Consultative Trap:** House/Chair-constituted, Chair-directed, reports to House/Chair, LS/RS Secretariat. **Consultative Committees** (Min. of Parl. Affairs, **Minister chairs**, 10–30 MPs) are **NOT Parliamentary Committees**.
- **3 Financial Committees (PR-STV Elected, 1-Yr, NO Ministers):** **PAC** (**1921**, **22 [15+7]**, **Opposition Chair since 1967** [K.C. - **24 DRSCs:** 17 (**1993**) → **24 (2004)**: **16 LS + 8 RS** (Commerce, Home, Education/Women/Youth, Industry, S&T/Env, Transport/Tourism/Culture, Health, Personnel/Law); **31 NOMINATED MPs (21+10)**, 1-yr, **NO Ministers**; Demands, Bills, Annual Reports, policy (ATRs).

## 8. Parliamentary Privileges, Anti-Defection Law, Position of Rajya Sabha & Reforms

- **Privileges (`Art. 105` / States `194`):** Cover Houses, panels, MPs, Ministers & **AG (`105(4)`)** — **NOT President (`361`)**. **44th Amend** dropped "House of Commons" wording & added **`Art. 361A`** (true media reports protected, **NOT secret sittings**). **Collective:** publish/ban debates, exclude strangers, **`Art. 122`** bars court probe of procedural irregularity (NOT illegality/*mala fides* — *Raja Ram Pal* 2007), punish breach/contempt (suspension ≤ **remainder of session** — *Ashish Shelar* 2022), no arrest/process in precincts without Chair consent. **Individual:** speech/vote immunity (`105(1)–(2)`, subject to `Art. 121` judges bar); **40-day pre/post-session arrest shield ONLY in CIVIL cases (`s. 135A CPC`) — ZERO criminal/preventive detention immunity**; jury/witness exemption.
- ***Sita Soren* (4 Mar 2024, `2024 INSC 161`, 7-Judge Unanimous):** Overruled *P.V. Narasimha Rao* (1998); **NO bribery immunity**; two-fold **necessity & collective-functioning** test; offence completes on offer/acceptance. Also *Searchlight I* (1959), *Keshav Singh* (1964), *K. Ajith* (2021: vandalism ≠ privilege).
- **Tenth Schedule (`52nd Amend 1985` — first mention of "political party" [Prelims 2025] & `91st Amend 2003` — deleted 1/3rd split, 15% CoM cap, barred defectors from ministership/`Art. 361B`):** **4 Grounds:** voluntary exit (*Ravi S. Naik* 1994; expelled joining other party *G. Viswanathan* 1996); whip defiance uncondoned **>15 days**; Independent joining party; Nominated joining **>6 mos**. **Exceptions:** **≥2/3rd merger (Para 4)** & **Presiding Officers (Para 5)**. NO whip in Pres./VP polls or automatic RS whip bar (*Kuldip Nayar*).
- **Rulings:** ***Kihoto Hollohan* (1992):** Para 7 struck down; Speaker = reviewable Tribunal (`136/226/227`). ***Keisham Meghachandra* (2020):** **3-month** norm + independent tribunal call. ***Subhash Desai* (May 2023):** **political party** appoints whip; *Nabam Rebia* sent to 7-judge Bench (**pending Nov 2026**). ***Padi Kaushik Reddy* (31 Jul 2025, `2025 INSC 912`):** Telangana Speaker ordered to decide 10 BRS MLAs in **3 mos**; contempt closed **12 Mar 2026**. Reforms: **Dinesh Goswami (1990)** & **Law Comm. 170th/255th** (confine whips to confidence/Money Bills; independent tribunal / ECI-advised decision).
- **4 Exclusive RS Powers:** **`Art. 249`** (State List law — 2/3rd present & voting), **`Art. 312`** (new All-India Services — 2/3rd present & voting), **`Art. 67(b)`** (sole VP removal initiation), **`Arts. 352/356/360`** (solo Emergency approval during LS dissolution).
- **Decline & Reforms (PRS/ADR 2024–26):** Sittings **135 → 55 (17th LS, 274 total) → 62 (2025)**; referrals **71% → 16% → 21% (18th)**; Monsoon 2026 LS **15% (QH 1%)**, 9/11 Bills minister-only; **206 suspensions** (17th LS; **146 in Dec 2023**); **77% Budget 2026 guillotined**; **46% 18th LS MPs** criminal cases (**31% serious**); Ordinances (*D.C. Wadhwa*, *Krishna Kumar Singh*). **Way Forward:** **120/100 sittings (NCRWC)**, **Opposition Days**, mandatory **DRSC (2–2.5 yr)** + statutory **PLCP** + Impact Assessments, **independent defection tribunal**, **PBO**, prompt **Deputy Speaker**, enforceable Code of Conduct.
