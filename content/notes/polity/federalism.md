## 1. "Union of States": the constitutional nature of Indian federalism

In political theory, **unitary** states concentrate sovereignty in one national government, while **federal** states divide constitutional authority between a Centre and autonomous regions. India operates a federal polity, yet **Article 1** declares that *"India, that is Bharat, shall be a **Union of States**."* The word **"federation"** appears **nowhere** in the Constitution.

Introducing the Draft Constitution on **4 November 1948**, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar explained that the word "Union" was chosen deliberately for **two reasons**: India's federation was **not** formed by an agreement among sovereign states, and consequently **no state has the right to secede**.

> "The Drafting Committee wanted to make it clear that though India was to be a Federation, the Federation was not the result of an agreement by the States to join in a Federation and that the Federation not being the result of an agreement no State has the right to secede from it. The Federation is a Union because it is indestructible." — B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 4 November 1948

Political scientist **Alfred Stepan** therefore classifies India as a **"holding-together" federation** rather than an American **"coming-together" federation**. Because **Article 3** lets Parliament alter state boundaries or names without state consent, India is an **"indestructible Union of destructible States"**—unlike America's *"indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States"* (*Texas v. White*, 1869). Ambedkar also stressed its elasticity: the Constitution works as **both unitary and federal according to time and circumstances**—federal in normal times, smoothly unitary in an emergency.

### Scholarly characterisations and *S.R. Bommai* (1994)

Because India marries a federal core to strong central safeguards, scholars coined classic descriptions frequently tested as Prelims pairs: **K.C. Wheare** called India **"quasi-federal"** (characterising it as a unitary state with subsidiary federal features rather than a federal state with subsidiary unitary features); **Granville Austin** termed it **"cooperative federalism"**; **W.H. Morris-Jones** called it **"bargaining federalism"**; **Sir Ivor Jennings** described a **"federation with a strong centralising tendency"**; **Paul H. Appleby** surprisingly termed it **"extremely federal"** because the Centre relies on State administration to implement national programmes; **C.H. Alexandrowicz** rejected Wheare to call India ***sui generis*** (a federation of its own kind); and **K. Santhanam** highlighted financial centralisation and the former Planning Commission as unitary levers.

Judicial doctrine matured alongside coalition politics. Early rulings in ***State of West Bengal v. Union of India* (1962)** (over Justice Subba Rao's federalist dissent) and ***State of Rajasthan v. Union of India* (1977)** emphasised central dominance. The landmark nine-judge bench in ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994)** reversed that tilt, holding that **federalism is part of the basic structure**: States are **not mere appendages of the Centre** and enjoy independent constitutional existence within their Seventh Schedule sphere—later reaffirmed in ***Kuldip Nayar* (2006)** and ***NCT of Delhi* (2018 and 2023)**.

> **Prelims pointer:** Watch three traps: (1) **"federation"** appears nowhere in the Constitution; (2) **Paul Appleby** called India **"extremely federal"** (not unitary), whereas **Wheare** called it **"quasi-federal"** and **Alexandrowicz** ***sui generis***; (3) ***S.R. Bommai* (1994)** cemented federalism as **basic structure**.

## 2. Federal system with a unitary bias

Drafted amid Partition, princely-state integration and developmental urgency, the Constitution—drawing structurally on **Canada** and the **Government of India Act, 1935**—established a **federal system with a unitary bias**. (Detailed Centre–State Articles and Emergency mechanics are covered in neighbouring notes; the table below contrasts the structural balance.)

| Federal features | Unitary / non-federal features |
|---|---|
| **Dual polity:** Union and States derive powers directly from the Constitution | **Strong Centre:** Longest Union List; central primacy on Concurrent List (Art. 254); **Union residuary powers** (Art. 248) |
| **Written Constitution:** Codified demarcation of jurisdictions | **Destructible States:** Parliament may redraw boundaries/names by simple majority (**Article 3**) |
| **Division of powers:** **Seventh Schedule** (Union, State, Concurrent Lists) | **Single Constitution & single citizenship:** Uniform national text and citizenship |
| **Constitutional supremacy:** Binds both Union and States | **Integrated machinery:** Single **judiciary**, **ECI**, **CAG**, and **All-India Services** (Art. 312) |
| **Rigidity on federal clauses:** Art. 368 requires special majority + ratification by **half the States** | **Flexibility elsewhere:** Most clauses amendable by Parliament alone |
| **Independent judiciary:** SC adjudicates federal disputes (**Art. 131**) | **Centrally appointed Governors (Art. 155)** and **reservation of state bills** (**Arts. 200–201**) |
| **Bicameralism:** **Rajya Sabha** represents States at the Centre | **Unequal Rajya Sabha seats** (Fourth Schedule) and **Emergency powers** (Arts. 352, 356, 360) |

## 3. Asymmetric federalism and *In Re: Article 370*

Indian federalism is deliberately **asymmetric**: rather than treating every unit identically, it grants tailored safeguards to accommodate ethnic, tribal, linguistic and historical diversity inside a single Union. Four pillars anchor this design:

1. **Part XXI (Articles 371 to 371J)** covering **12 states**: **Art. 371** (Maharashtra & Gujarat—regional development boards); **371A** (Nagaland—13th Amendment, 1962, protecting Naga customary law and land/resource ownership); **371B** (Assam—tribal Assembly committee); **371C** (Manipur—Hill Areas committee); **371D–371E** (Andhra Pradesh & Telangana—equitable employment/education and Central University); **371F** (Sikkim—36th Amendment, 1975); **371G** (Mizoram—customary law and land); **371H** (Arunachal Pradesh—Governor's law-and-order duty); **371I** (Goa—minimum 30 Assembly seats); and **371J** (Karnataka—98th Amendment, 2012, Kalyana-Karnataka board).
2. **Scheduled and Tribal Areas:** The **Fifth Schedule** covers **10 states**, while the **Sixth Schedule** establishes **Autonomous District Councils** in **4 northeastern states**—**Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram** (mnemonic **AMTM**, notably *excluding* Manipur).
3. **Union Territories with legislatures:** **NCT of Delhi** (**Article 239AA**, 69th Amendment, 1991—litigated over control of services in *NCT of Delhi*, May 2023, followed by the **GNCTD Amendment Act, 2023** creating the NCCSA; Delhi's new government withdrew its Supreme Court challenge on **23 May 2025** without judicial ruling), **Puducherry** (Article 239A), and **Jammu & Kashmir** (J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019).
4. **Representational and fiscal asymmetry:** Population-weighted Rajya Sabha seats and differentiated funding patterns for Northeastern and Himalayan states.

On **11 December 2023**, a five-judge Constitution Bench in ***In Re: Article 370*** unanimously upheld the August 2019 abrogation of Jammu & Kashmir's special status. The Court held that Article 370 was a **temporary, transitional provision** (J&K retained no separate internal sovereignty after accession); while using **Article 367** (CO 272) to equate the State Assembly with the Constituent Assembly was improper, the President independently held valid power under **Article 370(3)** (CO 273) after the State Constituent Assembly dissolved in 1957. Crucially, the Bench reaffirmed that **asymmetric federalism** endures across Articles 371–371J, left open whether Article 3 allows downgrading a State into a UT given the Centre's assurance, directed Assembly elections by September 2024, and urged **restoration of statehood at the earliest**.

> **In the news:** Jammu & Kashmir held Assembly elections in September–October 2024, with Chief Minister Omar Abdullah taking office in October 2024. With statehood still pending two years later, the J&K Assembly passed a formal resolution on **28 September 2026** seeking immediate restoration of full statehood.

## 4. Cooperative and competitive federalism

India's federal polity runs on a dual engine: **cooperative federalism** (Centre and States partnering in national governance) and **competitive federalism** (States competing horizontally for investment, governance rankings and fiscal strength).

```text
           INDIAN FEDERALISM: DUAL ENGINE
                  ┌──────────────┐
                  │ UNION CENTRE │
                  └──────┬───────┘
        Vertical         │         Fiscal / Policy
        Cooperation      │         Transfers & NITI
       (GST Council,     ▼         Benchmarking (FHI)
        ISC, Art.263) ┌──┴───┐
          ┌───────────┼──────┼───────────┐
          ▼           ▼      ▼           ▼
       ┌─────┐     ┌─────┐ ┌─────┐    ┌─────┐
       │St. A│◄───►│St. B│ │St. C│◄──►│St. D│
       └─────┘     └─────┘ └─────┘    └─────┘
        Horizontal Cooperation     Competitive Federalism
        (Zonal Councils, Rivers)   (Investment, SDG, FHI)
```

**Cooperative federalism** (Granville Austin) operates vertically and horizontally. Its institutional axis shifted in **January 2015** when **NITI Aayog** replaced the top-down Planning Commission, bringing all Chief Ministers into its **Governing Council** ("Team India"). Alongside it, the **GST Council** (101st Amendment, 2016—whose recommendations ***Mohit Minerals* (May 2022)** held non-binding on legislatures, praising **"uncooperative federalism"** and lawful federal contestation alongside cooperation), the **Inter-State Council** (Article 263) and **Zonal Councils** provide formal consensus forums.

**Competitive federalism** accelerated after 1991 liberalisation as States vied for private investment via investor summits and Ease of Doing Business reforms. NITI Aayog institutionalised outcome benchmarking through the **Aspirational Districts Programme** (2018, 112 districts), **Aspirational Blocks Programme** (2023, 500 blocks), **SDG India Index**, **Export Preparedness Index**, and the **Fiscal Health Index (FHI)**. Following the inaugural **FHI 2025** (January 2025), **FHI 2026** (**March 2026**, evaluating FY 2023–24 across 18 major states on expenditure quality, revenue mobilisation, fiscal prudence and debt indicators) ranked **Odisha** (73.1), **Goa** (54.7) and **Jharkhand** (50.5) as top *Achievers*, while **Punjab, Andhra Pradesh, West Bengal and Kerala** formed the bottom tier under persistent deficits and debt stress. Complementing this performance shift, the **16th Finance Commission** (Chairman **Dr. Arvind Panagariya**; submitted **17 November 2025**, tabled **1 February 2026** for **2026–31**) kept vertical devolution at **41 per cent** while introducing a new **10 per cent horizontal weight for "Contribution to GDP"** (replacing the 2.5 per cent tax-effort metric).

Yet unchecked competition carries serious risks: a **"race to the bottom"** (competitive tax breaks, diluted labour/environmental safeguards and unsustainable freebies), widening **inter-state divergence** between richer coastal/southern states and poorer landlocked states, and shrinking state flexibility under tied **Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS)**. Sustainable federalism requires **collaborative-competitive balance**—outcome competition cushioned by cooperative equalisation.

## 5. Commissions on Centre–State relations: First ARC, Sarkaria and Punchhi

The end of single-party dominance in 1967 ignited disputes over Governors, Article 356 and fiscal sharing, prompting five landmark reviews.

### Pre-Sarkaria milestones (1966–1977)

The **First ARC (1966–69)** (chaired by Morarji Desai, later K. Hanumanthaiya; assisted by the **M.C. Setalvad Study Team**) issued **22 recommendations** (1969) urging an Article 263 Inter-State Council, non-partisan Governors and wider state financial powers. More radically, Tamil Nadu's **Rajamannar Committee (1969–71)** (Dr. P.V. Rajamannar) proposed abolishing **All-India Services**, deleting **Articles 356, 357 and 365**, replacing the Planning Commission with a statutory body, and vesting **residuary powers in States**—rejected by the Centre. Similarly, the Akali Dal's **Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973)** sought to restrict the Centre to **four subjects** (defence, foreign affairs, communications and currency), while West Bengal's **1977 Memorandum** urged replacing "Union" with "Federal", deleting Article 356 and devolving **75 per cent** of central taxes.

### Sarkaria Commission (1983–1988) and Punchhi Commission (2007–2010)

Constituted in **June 1983** under Justice **R.S. Sarkaria** (with B. Sivaraman and Dr. S.R. Sen), the **Sarkaria Commission** reported in **October 1987** (released January 1988) with **247 recommendations** (~180 implemented, creating the **Inter-State Council in 1990** and tax-pooling via the **80th Amendment, 2000**). Rejecting structural dismantling, Sarkaria affirmed a **strong Centre** paired with functional decentralisation and constitutional restraint; the **NCRWC (Venkatachaliah Commission, 2002)** later endorsed Sarkaria's ISC framework and urged an Article 307 Inter-State Trade Commission. Reflecting post-1991 liberalisation, coalition politics and internal security, the Centre appointed the **Punchhi Commission** (**27 April 2007**) under former CJI **Madan Mohan Punchhi** (with Dhirendra Singh, Vinod Kumar Duggal, N.R. Madhava Menon and Vijay Shanker succeeding Amaresh Bagchi), whose seven-volume report of **March 2010** offered **273 recommendations** and went well beyond Sarkaria.

| Dimension | Sarkaria Commission (1983–88; 247 recs) | Punchhi Commission (2007–10; 273 recs) |
|---|---|---|
| **Governor's office** | Eminent politically inactive outsider; **consult Chief Minister**; remove only for compelling reasons | **Fixed 5-year tenure** (end pleasure-doctrine arbitrariness); removal by **State Legislature impeachment**; **end ex-officio University Chancellor role** |
| **Assent to State Bills (Arts. 200–201)** | President should communicate reasons when withholding assent | Mandatory **6-month timeline** for Governor and President to decide on bills |
| **Article 356** | Strictly **last resort** after warning; do not dissolve Assembly before parliamentary approval | **"Localised emergency"** (amend Arts. 355–356) for a troubled district up to **3 months** without dismissing the State Government |
| **Residuary & Concurrent** | Tax residuary with Centre; **non-tax residuary** to List III; consult states on List III | Mandatory **ISC consultation** on List III; enact law regulating **Article 253 treaties** affecting State List |
| **Inter-State Council & RS** | Create permanent **"Inter-Governmental Council"** (led to 1990 ISC) | Strengthen ISC as **continuing auditing/management forum**; **equal Rajya Sabha seats** for states |
| **All-India Services & Forces** | Strengthen & expand AIS; Centre may deploy forces suo motu (consultation desirable) | Expand AIS (health, education, engineering); allow brief **suo motu deployment** in communal conflagrations |

> **Remember:** Two signature Punchhi Commission innovations regularly tested in UPSC: (1) **State Legislature impeachment** of Governors and ending their **University Chancellor** role; and (2) **"localised emergency"** under Articles 355–356 without dismissing an elected state government.

## 6. Contemporary federal jurisprudence (2024–2026), flashpoints and way forward

### Landmark Supreme Court jurisprudence (2024–2025)

Three Constitution Bench verdicts between 2024 and 2025 reshaped fiscal and executive federalism:

1. ***Mineral Area Development Authority (MADA) v. SAIL* (25 July 2024):** A nine-judge bench (**8:1**, CJI D.Y. Chandrachud majority; Justice B.V. Nagarathna dissenting) overruled *India Cement* (1989), holding mineral **royalty is contractual, not a tax**. States retain independent power to tax mineral rights and lands under **Entries 49 and 50 of List II**, undiminished by the MMDR Act, 1957. On **14 August 2024**, the Court allowed recovery of past dues from **1 April 2005** in staggered 12-year instalments starting 1 April 2026.
2. ***State of UP v. Lalta Prasad Vaish* (23 October 2024):** Another nine-judge bench (**8:1**, Justice Nagarathna dissenting) overruled *Synthetics and Chemicals* (1990), holding **"intoxicating liquor"** in **Entry 8 of List II** includes **industrial alcohol**, curbing Union List Entry 52 encroachment.
3. ***State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu* & Presidential Reference No. 1 of 2025:** On **8 April 2025**, a two-judge bench declared ten stalled Tamil Nadu bills "deemed assented" under Article 142 and imposed 1–3 month timelines for Articles 200–201. Answering President Droupadi Murmu's **13 May 2025** Article 143 reference (14 questions), a five-judge Constitution Bench (CJI B.R. Gavai et al.) opined on **20 November 2025** (*In re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills*, **2025 INSC 1333**) that courts **cannot impose rigid timelines** or grant **"deemed assent"** via Article 142—**yet** clarified Governors hold **no indefinite pocket veto**: prolonged, unexplained inaction invites a limited judicial **mandamus** directing timely action without dictating the outcome.

### Persistent flashpoints and way forward

Four structural tensions persist in 2026. First, **post-2026 delimitation**: the **84th Amendment (2001)** froze Lok Sabha seat shares until the first Census after 2026. With **Census of India 2027** notified on **16 June 2025** (Cabinet approved December 2025; Houselisting April–September 2026, Enumeration February 2027) and the SC upholding the State embargo in ***Prof. K. Purushottam Reddy* (25 July 2025)**, southern states fear population-based reallocation will penalise early demographic stabilisation. The fear decided a vote in Parliament: the **131st Amendment Bill, 2026**, which would have raised the Lok Sabha to 850 seats and redrawn them on the 2011 Census, was defeated in the Lok Sabha on **17 April 2026** (298 to 230, short of two-thirds). Second, rising Union **Article 271 surcharges** and specific-purpose **cesses**—outside the divisible pool—compress the 16th Finance Commission's **41 per cent** vertical devolution. Third, under **Section 6 of the DSPE Act, 1946**, several opposition-ruled states withheld **CBI general consent**, forcing case-by-case approval (even as political turnovers led Maharashtra, Chhattisgarh, Rajasthan and **West Bengal on 8 June 2026** to restore it). Fourth, **One Nation, One Election** (pending **129th Amendment Bill, 2024**) tests whether synchronised "unexpired terms" dilute autonomous state electoral mandates.

> **In the news:** Three 2025–2026 markers define Indian federalism up to **November 2026**: the SC's **20 November 2025** Presidential Reference opinion rejecting judicial deadlines/deemed assent while curbing indefinite gubernatorial inaction; the **16th Finance Commission** report tabled on **1 February 2026** (41% devolution + new 10% GDP-contribution weight); and **NITI Aayog's Fiscal Health Index 2026** (March 2026) highlighting growing inter-state fiscal divergence.

Strengthening India's federal compact demands four pragmatic steps: **revitalising the Inter-State Council** into a permanent standing forum per Punchhi; **codifying Sarkaria–Punchhi Governor reforms** (CM consultation, secure tenure, ending the Chancellor role); **rebuilding fiscal and electoral trust** by capping non-sharable cesses/surcharges, untying CSS grants and forging a consensus **delimitation formula** (such as freezing interstate shares or expanding Rajya Sabha weight); and pairing competitive benchmarking with **cooperative equalisation** so federal competition lifts lagging states too.

> **Mains angle:** Mains 2026 asked how far the federal framework has accommodated regional and cultural diversity, the role of asymmetric federalism, and how to make dispute resolution work better. Section 3 answers the first two; for the third, use the Inter-State Council (Article 263), the Zonal Councils, river water tribunals (Article 262) and the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction (Article 131), and say what each lacks. Anchor GS-2 answers across four pillars: (1) **Constitutional character** (Art. 1 "Union of States", Ambedkar's unitary-federal elasticity, *S.R. Bommai* basic structure); (2) **Asymmetric & cooperative-competitive evolution** (Arts. 371–371J, *In Re: Art. 370*, FHI 2026, 16th FC); (3) **Recent rulings & flashpoints** (*MADA*, *Lalta Prasad Vaish*, Nov 2025 Governor Reference, Census 2027 delimitation, cesses, CBI consent); and (4) **Sarkaria–Punchhi institutional reforms**.
