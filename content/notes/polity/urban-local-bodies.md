## 1. Evolution and the 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 (Part IXA)

India's modern municipal tradition dates back to the **first Municipal Corporation at Madras (1687–88)**, followed by **Bombay and Calcutta (1726)**. Urban local bodies evolved through **Lord Mayo's Resolution (1870)** (financial decentralisation), **Lord Ripon's Resolution (1882)** (*"Magna Carta of local self-government"*), the **Royal Commission on Decentralisation (1907)**, the **Government of India Act, 1919** (making local government a **"transferred" subject**), and the **Government of India Act, 1935** (provincial subject; now **Entry 5, State List**).

Yet after 1950, urban local bodies (ULBs) had no constitutional protection and faced routine supersession by State Governments. After Rajiv Gandhi's **65th Amendment Bill (1989)** fell in the Rajya Sabha, P.V. Narasimha Rao's government enacted the **74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992** (Presidential assent **20 April 1993**; enforced **1 June 1993**). It inserted **Part IXA** (*"The Municipalities"*, **Articles 243P to 243ZG**) and the **Twelfth Schedule** (**18 functional items**, **Article 243W**), giving cities constitutional status as India's third tier of government.

### Three types of municipalities and core provisions (Articles 243Q–243ZG)

Under **Article 243Q**, every state must constitute three types of municipalities: **Nagar Panchayat** (transitional rural-to-urban area), **Municipal Council** (smaller urban area), and **Municipal Corporation** (larger urban area). The **Governor** notifies each category based on five constitutional criteria: population, population density, revenue generated for local administration, percentage of non-agricultural employment, and economic importance. Under the proviso to **Article 243Q(1)**, however, the Governor may exempt an **Industrial Township** where an industrial establishment supplies municipal services (e.g., **Jamshedpur** under Tata Steel).

- **Composition & voting (Art. 243R):** All seats are filled by **direct election** from territorial wards. States may add (a) persons with **special knowledge or experience in municipal administration**—crucially, **such nominated experts have NO right to vote** in municipal meetings (contrasting with ex-officio Panchayat members under Art. 243C(4))—(b) overlapping MPs, MLAs, and MLCs, and (c) committee chairpersons. The mode of electing the **Chairperson/Mayor** (direct or indirect) is left to the **State Legislature**.
- **Wards Committees (Art. 243S):** Mandatory in every municipality with population **>= 3 lakh**, decentralising citizen oversight across one or more wards.
- **Reservations (Art. 243T):** SC and ST seats in proportion to population (rotated); **>= 1/3rd** of total seats (including SC/ST women seats) reserved for **women**; chairperson reservation left to State law. Under **Article 243T(5)**, SC/ST reservation lapses with **Article 334 (25 January 2030)**, **whereas women's reservation has NO sunset clause**. **OBC reservation (Art. 243T(6))** is voluntary and bound by the Supreme Court's **Triple Test** (*K. Krishna Murthy*, 2010; *Vikas Kishanrao Gawali*, 2021; *Suresh Mahajan*, 2022): dedicated empirical commission, body-wise quota, and total SC+ST+OBC **<= 50 per cent**.
- **Tenure, age, SFC, SEC & judicial bar (Arts. 243U–243ZG):** Fixed **five-year term** (*"and no longer"*, held strictly mandatory in ***Kishansing Tomar v. Municipal Corporation of Ahmedabad*, 19 October 2006**); re-poll within **six months** of dissolution (reconstituted council serves only the **remainder**; no poll if remainder **< 6 months**). Minimum age is **21 years** (**Art. 243V**). **Article 243W** uses permissive **"may"** for devolving the **18 Twelfth Schedule functions** (urban planning, land-use regulation, roads, water supply, public health, solid waste, fire services, slum improvement); **Article 243X** covers taxation/grants. Crucially, ULBs share the **same State Finance Commission** (**Art. 243Y** / 243-I) and **same State Election Commission** (**Art. 243ZA** / 243K) as Panchayats. **Article 243ZG** bars courts in delimitation or seat allotment; polls can be challenged **only by election petition**.
- **Exempted areas & missing MESA (Art. 243ZC):** Part IXA does **not** apply to Fifth Schedule (Scheduled Areas), Sixth Schedule (Tribal Areas), or the Darjeeling Gorkha Hill Council. While Parliament may extend Part IXA to Scheduled Areas by law, **unlike rural PESA (1996), no urban equivalent (MESA Bill, 2001) has ever been enacted**.

### Constitutional planning committees: DPC versus MPC

| Feature | District Planning Committee (DPC — Art. 243ZD) | Metropolitan Planning Committee (MPC — Art. 243ZE) |
|---|---|---|
| **Where mandatory** | **Every State** at the **district level** | Every **Metropolitan Area** (**Art. 243P(c)**: pop. **>= 10 lakh**, **1+ districts**, **2+ ULBs/PRIs**) |
| **Mandate** | Consolidate plans of **Panchayats AND Municipalities** into a draft district plan | Prepare a draft development plan for the **Metropolitan Area** |
| **Min. elected share** | **Not less than 4/5ths (80%)** of total members | **Not less than 2/3rds (66.7%)** of total members |
| **Electoral college** | Elected by & from elected members of **District Panchayat & Municipalities** in rural:urban pop. ratio | Elected by & from elected members of **Municipalities & Chairpersons of Panchayats** in pop. ratio |

> **Prelims pointer:** Five Part IXA traps: (1) Nominated municipal experts (**Art. 243R**) have **NO voting right**; (2) **Wards Committees** are mandatory at **>= 3 lakh** pop.; (3) **DPC = >= 4/5ths elected** vs **MPC = >= 2/3rds elected** (Metro area **>= 10 lakh**); (4) PRIs and ULBs share the **same SEC and SFC**; (5) **No urban PESA (MESA)** exists yet.

## 2. Eight types of urban local governments in India

Beyond the three constitutional municipalities, urban administration in India operates through **eight types** of local bodies:

| Type of Urban Body | Legal Basis & Control | Structure & Key Prelims Trap |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Municipal Corporation** | State Act (Parliament Act for UTs); metros & big cities | 3 authorities: **Council** (ceremonial **Mayor**, 1–5 yr term), **Standing Committees**, and **Municipal Commissioner** (**IAS officer** appointed by State = real executive!) |
| **2. Municipality** *(Council / Nagar Palika)* | State Act; towns & smaller cities | **Council** (**President/Chairman**), **Standing Committees**, and State-appointed **Chief Executive / Municipal Officer** |
| **3. Notified Area Committee (NAC)** | **State gazette notification** (NOT separate Act); fast-developing towns | **Entirely NOMINATED** by State Government—**no elected members**; exercises only notified powers |
| **4. Town Area Committee (TAC)** | Separate State Act; small towns | Semi-municipal body (drainage, roads, streetlighting); wholly elected, wholly nominated, or partly both |
| **5. Cantonment Board** | **Cantonments Act, 2006**; **Union Ministry of Defence** (NOT State!) | 4 categories (I–IV); partly elected + partly nominated; **station military commander** = ex-officio **President**; Executive Officer appointed by President of India (IDES) |
| **6. Township** | Established by large **PSUs** (SAIL, BHEL, NTPC) for staff colonies | **No elected members**; administered by a company-appointed **Town Administrator** |
| **7. Port Trust / Major Port Authority** | **Major Port Authorities Act, 2021** (replaced 1963 Act); major ports | Statutory board managing port operations and civic amenities within port limits |
| **8. Special Purpose Agency (SPA)** | State Act/order (Housing/Water Boards, DDA, BDA, MMRDA) | **Function-based (NOT area-based)** single-purpose bodies outside ULB accountability |

> **In the news:** **Excision of Cantonment Civil Areas (2023–2026):** Moving away from the British-era enclave model, the Ministry of Defence denotified **Khas Yol Cantonment** (Himachal Pradesh) on **27 April 2023**—converting the military area into a military station and merging civil areas with state local bodies (reducing cantonments from **62 to 61**). In July 2023 and March 2024, the MoD initiated excision across up to **58 cantonments**—including draft notifications for 10 (Dehradun, Clement Town, Fatehgarh, Babina, Shahjahanpur, Mathura, Deolali, Ramgarh, Ajmer, and Nasirabad)—in consultation with States.

## 3. The 3Fs framework: Functions, Funds and Functionaries

Across **both rural Panchayats and urban Municipalities**, the gap between constitutional promise and ground reality is best diagnosed through the **3Fs framework**: **Functions**, **Funds**, and **Functionaries**.

```text
          THE 3Fs DEVOLUTION GAP (PRIs & ULBs)

  CONSTITUTIONAL PROMISE            GROUND REALITY
 ┌──────────────────────┐       ┌────────────────────────┐
 │ FUNCTIONS            │       │ Discretionary "may";   │
 │ 29 Rural (11th Sch.) │ ──▶   │ parallel parastatals,  │
 │ 18 Urban (12th Sch.) │       │ SPVs & CSS silos       │
 ├──────────────────────┤       ├────────────────────────┤
 │ FUNDS                │       │ MC rev ~0.6% GDP; PRI  │
 │ Local taxes, SFCs,   │ ──▶   │ own tax ~1–2%; weak &  │
 │ Union FC grants      │       │ delayed SFCs           │
 ├──────────────────────┤       ├────────────────────────┤
 │ FUNCTIONARIES        │       │ State deputationists;  │
 │ Dedicated local      │ ──▶   │ 1 urban planner per    │
 │ administrative staff │       │ 75,000 urban citizens  │
 └──────────────────────┘       └────────────────────────┘
```

### Functions: discretionary "may" and parallel parastatals

Both **Article 243G** (29 rural items) and **Article 243W** (18 urban items) state that State Legislatures *"may"* devolve functions—leaving transfer optional and **Activity Mapping** incomplete. In cities, core Twelfth Schedule powers—**urban planning, land-use regulation, water supply, public transport, and slum redevelopment**—are retained by State line departments, statutory **Development Authorities**, Water Boards, or **Smart City SPVs** (registered under the Companies Act), relegating elected municipalities to sanitation and streetlighting. Per **NITI Aayog's *Reforms in Urban Planning Capacity in India* (September 2021)** and **PRS (2025)**, nearly half of India's **7,933 urban settlements** are **Census Towns** still governed as rural panchayats, and only **28 per cent (2,216 towns)** had approved statutory **Master Plans**.

### Funds and local finances: own revenue, bonds, SFCs, and Union FC grants

#### Own-revenue crisis (RBI data, 2022–2024)

The **RBI's maiden *Report on Municipal Finances* (November 2022)** showed overall municipal revenues stagnating near **1 per cent of GDP** (against **7.4% in Brazil** and **6% in South Africa**). Its **second *Report on Municipal Finances* (13 November 2024**, covering **232 Municipal Corporations** for 2019–20 to 2023–24 BE) revealed deeper fiscal stress: municipal corporation revenue receipts stand at just **0.6 per cent of GDP** (vs **9.2%** for Centre and **14.6%** for States); the **top 10 corporations** account for **over 58 per cent** of receipts; own taxes supply only **~30 per cent** of revenue, and most MCs cannot cover day-to-day expenditure from own sources. **Property tax**—over **60 per cent of own tax revenue**—yields a mere **0.15 per cent of GDP** (vs **0.3–0.6%** in developing peers and **1.7%** in OECD economies) due to unmapped properties, outdated valuations, and weak enforcement. Abolition of **octroi** and subsumption of local levies into **GST (2017)** further eroded urban tax buoyancy without guaranteed GST sharing. Rural finances are thinner still: the **RBI's *Report on Finances of PRIs* (January 2024, FY 2022–23)** found only **~1–2 per cent own tax revenue** (₹737 crore of ₹35,354 crore) and **~95 per cent grant dependence** (~80% Union, ~15% State).

#### Municipal bonds and capital markets

Following **SEBI's 2015 framework** and **AMRUT / AMRUT 2.0 incentives** (₹13 crore per ₹100 crore for first-time issuers), Pune (2017), Hyderabad, Ahmedabad, Surat, Vadodara, Lucknow, Ghaziabad, and **Indore** (**India's first retail green municipal bond**, February 2023) entered the debt market.

> **In the news:** **Record FY 2025–26 Municipal Bonds & RBI Repo Boost:** In **October 2025**, the **RBI** permitted municipal bonds as eligible collateral for **repo transactions**. Backed by AMRUT 2.0 incentives and Budget 2025–26's **₹1 lakh crore Urban Challenge Fund**, **FY 2025–26** set a record with **12 ULBs raising ~₹1,756 crore across 14 issues** (including Greater Chennai, Nashik, Surat, Pimpri-Chinchwad, and first-timers Agra, Prayagraj, Varanasi, and Bhavnagar). Yet municipal bonds remain just **0.06 per cent of India's corporate bond market** (September 2025), and Pune Municipal Corporation moved to return its 2017 ₹200 crore bond proceeds due to weak project-execution capacity.

#### State Finance Commissions (Articles 243-I and 243-Y)

Constituted every **five years** by the **Governor**, each **State Finance Commission (SFC)** recommends state tax sharing, assigned local taxes, grants-in-aid from the State Consolidated Fund, and fiscal reforms for both PRIs and ULBs—tabled in the State Legislature with an **Action Taken Memorandum**. Chronic failings persist: delayed constitution, **non-synchronisation** with the Union Finance Commission, poor local fiscal data, missing permanent secretariats, and ignored reports. Notably, the **16th Finance Commission (2025–26)** found SFC outputs so uneven that it recommended **amending Articles 280(3)(bb) and 280(3)(c)** to drop *"on the basis of the recommendations made by the Finance Commission of the States"*, while urging **NITI Aayog** to publish a compendium of SFC best practices.

#### Union Finance Commission local-body grants: 15th FC versus 16th FC

Under **Articles 280(3)(bb) and (c)**, the **16th Finance Commission (2026–31)**—chaired by **Dr. Arvind Panagariya** and accepted on **1 February 2026**—recommended India's largest-ever local-body package:

| Dimension | 15th Finance Commission (2021–26) | 16th Finance Commission (2026–31; Accepted 1 Feb 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| **Total Local Body Grants** | **₹4,36,361 crore** | **₹7,91,493 crore** *(part of ₹9.47L cr total grants)* |
| **Rural Local Bodies (RLBs)** | **₹2,36,805 crore** | **₹4,35,236 crore** (Basic: **₹3,48,188 cr**; Performance: **₹87,048 cr**) |
| **Urban Local Bodies (ULBs)** | **₹1,21,055 crore** (incl. Million-Plus Challenge Fund) | **₹3,56,257 crore** (Basic: **₹2,32,125 cr**; Performance: **₹58,032 cr**; **Wastewater: ₹56,100 cr**; **Urbanisation Premium: ₹10,000 cr**) |
| **Special Windows** | **₹70,051 cr** Health grants + **₹8,000 cr** new-city incubation + **₹450 cr** shared services | **₹56,100 cr** wastewater management (**10–40 lakh cities**); **₹10,000 cr Urbanisation Premium** (one-time ₹2,000/person, 2011 Census, for rural-to-urban transition) |
| **Grant Structure** | RLB:ULB **67:33 → 65:35**; **60% tied** + **40% untied** | Basic+Performance **60:40** RLB:ULB (overall urban **~45%**); **80% Basic** (**50% untied**, **50% tied** to sanitation, solid waste & water) + **20% Performance** |
| **Entry Conditions** | Elected bodies, online accounts, property-tax floor, SFC by Mar 2024 | **3 entry criteria:** (1) duly constituted **constitutional local bodies**, (2) online **provisional & audited accounts**, (3) **timely SFCs** |

The 16th FC also recommended **GIS-based property tax IT systems**, strengthening **Local Fund Audit Departments (LFADs)** under **CAG Technical Guidance and Supervision**, expanding the **Urban Data platform**, and disclosing all local transfers in **Appendix III of State Finance Accounts**.

### Functionaries: the missing third tier of bureaucracy

Most states lack dedicated **Municipal and Panchayat Cadres**. Key officers (Municipal Commissioner, BDO, Panchayat Secretary, Zila Parishad CEO) serve on deputation from State or All-India Services, answering vertically to State ministries rather than elected Mayors or Sarpanches. Technical capacity is critically thin: **NITI Aayog (2021)** counted only about **5,000 registered urban planners** nationwide—roughly **one planner per 75,000 urban residents**—alongside widespread vacancies in municipal engineering and accrual accounting.

## 4. Structural challenges of local governance and the way forward

Across both PRIs and ULBs, **six structural challenges** blunt the promise of the 73rd and 74th Amendments:
1. **Unfunded 3Fs & discretionary "may":** States retain core functions and funds, reducing local bodies to implementing agencies for central/state schemes.
2. **Weak Mayor vs strong IAS Commissioner:** The colonial **Commissioner system** splits executive authority from democratic mandate, leaving ceremonial, short-term Mayors unable to lead city transformation.
3. **Parallel parastatals, SPVs & defunct DPCs/MPCs:** Unaccountable Development Authorities, Water Boards, and Smart City SPVs bypass ULBs while **DPCs (Art. 243ZD)** and **MPCs (Art. 243ZE)** lie dormant across most metros.
4. **Delayed polls & State administrators:** Repeated postponements over delimitation or OBC quotas drew stern Supreme Court rebukes in ***Kishansing Tomar* (2006)** and ***Suresh Mahajan* (2022)** (*"bordering on breakdown of rule of law"*).
5. **Proxy capture & urban apathy:** ***Pradhan Pati*** erodes rural women's leadership, while weak Wards Committees alienate urban citizens from municipal politics.
6. **Fiscal fragility, weak audits & climate vulnerability:** Low property-tax buoyancy (~0.15% GDP), opaque accounts, and missing Master Plans leave cities exposed to floods, heatwaves, and waste crises.

### Second ARC (Sixth Report) and a balanced way forward

In its **Sixth Report, *Local Governance: An Inspiring Journey into the Future* (October 2007, chaired by M. Veerappa Moily)**, the **Second ARC** grounded reform in the **Principle of Subsidiarity**: *what can be done effectively at a lower tier must not be centralised upward*. Combining Second ARC with **RBI (2024)**, **NITI Aayog (2021)**, and **16th FC (2026)** insights yields five actionable priorities:

- **Subsidiarity & Activity Mapping:** Codify clear **activity mapping** across all 29 rural and 18 urban subjects; gradually bring urban parastatals and Smart City SPVs under elected ULBs; and enact **MESA** for Scheduled Area towns.
- **Empowered executive Mayors & active MPCs:** Adopt Second ARC's model of a **directly elected Mayor** with a **fixed five-year term** exercising executive authority over the Municipal Commissioner, while operationalising **DPCs** and **MPCs** for integrated regional planning.
- **Synchronised SFCs & local tax buoyancy:** Synchronise **SFCs** with the Union Finance Commission, enforce the **16th FC's three entry criteria**, roll out **GIS-based property tax** rolls with unit-area valuation, deepen **municipal bonds** and state **pooled finance** (e.g., Tamil Nadu's TNUDF), and examine a dedicated **local-body share of GST**.
- **Dedicated local cadres:** Establish professional **State Municipal and Panchayat Cadres** (planners, engineers, accountants) accountable to local bodies, backed by **CAG Technical Guidance and Supervision**.
- **Deepened citizen oversight:** Operationalise **Area Sabhas** below Wards Committees (Model Nagar Raj Bill) to mirror rural Gram Sabhas; enforce anti-proxy penalties in PRIs; and expand social audits and digital transparency (**eGramSwaraj, SabhaSaar, Urban Data platforms**).

> **Mains angle:** For GS-2 answers on ULBs, 3Fs, SFCs, or local-governance challenges, contrast **constitutional status** with **fiscal-administrative subordination**. Cite **RBI (Nov 2024): MC revenue 0.6% GDP, property tax 0.15% GDP**; **RBI (Jan 2024): PRI own tax ~1–2%**; **NITI Aayog (2021): 28% towns with Master Plans & 1 planner/75,000 citizens**; **16th FC (2026–31): ₹7,91,493 cr local grants & 3 entry criteria**; **Second ARC (6th Report)**; and ***Kishansing Tomar* / *Suresh Mahajan***—concluding with **subsidiarity, directly elected 5-year Mayors, synchronised SFCs, GIS property tax, and local cadres**.
