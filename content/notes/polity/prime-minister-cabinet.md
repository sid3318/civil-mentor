## 1. The Prime Minister: Appointment, Tenure, Powers and Constitutional Role

In the parliamentary scheme of government adopted by the Constitution of India, executive authority is deliberately bifurcated between two distinct offices. The **President of India** is the **nominal (*de jure*) executive**—the Head of the State who symbolises the unity, dignity, and constitutional continuity of the Republic. The **Prime Minister**, by contrast, is the **real (*de facto*) executive**—the Head of the Government who commands the legislative majority, chairs the Union Cabinet, directs national policy, and wields the substance of Union executive power. While all formal actions of the Government of India are expressed in the name of the President under `Article 77(1)`, the constitutional machinery moves almost entirely on the binding aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister under `Article 74(1)`.

Introducing the Draft Constitution in the Constituent Assembly on 4 November 1948, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** explained this dual executive with remarkable candour. Rejecting the American presidential model in favour of daily parliamentary accountability over fixed-term stability, Ambedkar emphasised that real executive gravity in India's Constitution vests not in Rashtrapati Bhavan but in the Prime Minister's chair:

> "If any functionary under our Constitution is to be compared with the U.S. President, he is the Prime Minister and not the President of the Union." — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, 4 November 1948

This distinction is foundational to both Prelims and Mains. Unlike the American President—who combines ceremonial and governing authority, sits outside Congress, and governs through non-legislator secretaries—the Indian Prime Minister is simultaneously the leader of the ruling legislative coalition and the supreme political executive. Yet unlike the British Prime Minister—whose office rests almost wholly on unwritten constitutional conventions—the Indian Prime Minister operates within a written constitutional architecture anchored primarily in **`Articles 74`, `75`, and `78`** (supplemented by `Articles 77`, `88`, and `352(3)`), enriched by seven and a half decades of parliamentary conventions and landmark Supreme Court rulings.

```keyfacts
# The Prime Minister: Constitutional & Political Anchors

Either House or Non-MP | Eligibility Flexibility | Unlike UK (Commons only), India's PM may sit in LS or RS, or be a non-MP for 6 months (Art. 75(5); S.P. Anand, 1996)
No Separate Oath | Third Schedule (Forms I & II) | Constitution prescribes NO separate oath for PM; PM swears the standard oath of a Union Minister of office and secrecy
Dissolution Trigger | Automatic Collapse of CoM | Death or resignation of the PM automatically dissolves the entire Council of Ministers; another minister's exit only creates a vacancy
```

### Appointment of the Prime Minister: Conventions and Situational Discretion

One of the most frequently tested facts in UPSC Prelims is that **the Constitution does not lay down any specific procedure for the selection and appointment of the Prime Minister**. **`Article 75(1)`** states with stark economy: *"The Prime Minister shall be appointed by the President and the other Ministers shall be appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister."*

#### The Ordinary Convention: Leader of the Lok Sabha Majority

The bare language of `Article 75(1)` does not mean the President enjoys unfettered personal discretion to appoint anyone as Prime Minister. Because `Article 75(3)` renders the Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the **House of the People (Lok Sabha)**, parliamentary convention strictly binds the President to invite the **leader of the majority party or the pre-poll majority coalition in the Lok Sabha** to form the government. When a single party or pre-poll alliance wins a clear majority (more than 272 seats in the 543-member Lok Sabha), the President's role is purely formal and ceremonial—there is zero scope for personal choice.

#### Situational Discretion: Hung Lok Sabha and Sudden Vacancy

Genuine **situational discretion** (as distinguished from constitutional discretion, which the Indian President does not possess) arises in **two exceptional circumstances**:

1. **Hung Lok Sabha (No Clear Majority):** When general elections produce a fractured verdict in which no single party or pre-poll alliance commands an absolute majority—or when a sitting ministry collapses mid-term—the President must exercise reasoned constitutional judgment to identify the person most likely to command a stable majority in the Lok Sabha. Over decades of coalition politics, Indian Presidents have evolved a settled hierarchy of parliamentary conventions (endorsed by the **Sarkaria Commission [1988]** and **Punchhi Commission [2010]** for States and mirrored at the Centre):
   - First, invite the leader of the **largest pre-poll alliance** (if any);
   - Second, invite the leader of the **single largest party** if it stakes a credible claim with demonstrable support;
   - Third, invite a **post-poll coalition** commanding written letters of support from constituent parties (with an insistence on securing a **vote of confidence on the floor of the Lok Sabha** within a stipulated period, typically 10 to 30 days).
2. **Sudden Death of an Incumbent Prime Minister with No Obvious Successor:** When a Prime Minister dies suddenly in office, the President faces an immediate constitutional vacuum because the death of the PM automatically dissolves the Council of Ministers. In **1964** (following Jawaharlal Nehru's death) and **1966** (following Lal Bahadur Shastri's death), President **Dr. S. Radhakrishnan** appointed the senior-most Cabinet Minister, **Gulzarilal Nanda**, as interim/acting Prime Minister until the Congress Parliamentary Party formally elected its permanent leader (Shastri in 1964 and Indira Gandhi in 1966). However, when **Indira Gandhi** was assassinated on 31 October 1984, President **Giani Zail Singh** departed from that precedent and immediately swore in **Rajiv Gandhi** as full Prime Minister (ignoring the senior-most minister, Pranab Mukherjee), relying on the Congress Parliamentary Board's endorsement; the Congress Parliamentary Party subsequently ratified Rajiv Gandhi unanimously. Had the ruling party already elected a new leader upon the PM's death, the President would have had no choice but to appoint that elected leader.

> [!example]
> **Evolution of Presidential Situational Discretion in Hung Lok Sabhas (1979–1998):**
> - **1979 (First Exercise of Discretion):** After Morarji Desai's Janata Party government collapsed, President **Neelam Sanjiva Reddy** first sounded out Leader of the Opposition Y.B. Chavan (who declined), then scrutinised supporter lists and appointed coalition leader **Charan Singh** as PM—directing him to seek a **vote of confidence** in the Lok Sabha within three weeks. Charan Singh resigned on 20 August 1979 before facing the House after Congress (I) withdrew support, becoming the only Indian PM never to face a parliamentary floor vote.
> - **1989:** After the 9th Lok Sabha elections produced a hung House, President **R. Venkataraman** followed the "single largest party" rule by first asking Rajiv Gandhi (Congress, 197 seats); when Gandhi declined to form a government, Venkataraman appointed **V.P. Singh** (National Front, supported from outside by BJP and Left) subject to proving majority within 30 days.
> - **1990 & 1991:** Following V.P. Singh's defeat on a confidence motion (Nov 1990), Venkataraman appointed **Chandra Shekhar** (with outside Congress support); in 1991, after Rajiv Gandhi's assassination, he appointed **P.V. Narasimha Rao** to head a minority Congress government, instructing him to prove majority within four weeks.
> - **1996 (Two Contrasting Appointments):** President **Dr. Shankar Dayal Sharma** first applied the single-largest-party principle by swearing in **Atal Bihari Vajpayee** (BJP, 161 seats) with a 15-day floor-test deadline; Vajpayee resigned after 13 days without winning support. Sharma then appointed **H.D. Deve Gowda** (United Front coalition with outside Congress backing), followed by **I.K. Gujral** in 1997.
> - **1998 (Institutionalisation of Written Support Letters):** Learning from the 13-day 1996 episode, President **K.R. Narayanan** established a crucial new constitutional safeguard: before swearing in **Atal Bihari Vajpayee** in March 1998, Narayanan refused to act merely on single-largest-party status and demanded **written letters of support** from coalition partners demonstrating prima facie majority viability, coupled with a mandatory **10-day floor test**.

#### Judicial Principles Governing Prime Ministerial Appointment

Four landmark judicial rulings have decisively settled recurring constitutional controversies over who can be appointed Prime Minister and when majority must be proven:

1. **No Pre-Appointment Majority Proof Required (*Dinesh Chandra Pande v. Chaudhury Charan Singh*, Delhi High Court, 1980):** Challenging Charan Singh's 1979 appointment, petitioners argued that a candidate must prove Lok Sabha majority *before* swearing-in. The **Delhi High Court** rejected this contention, holding that the Constitution does **not** require prior proof of majority before appointment. The President may lawfully appoint a person as Prime Minister first and then require them to establish Lok Sabha confidence on the floor within a reasonable timeframe. This rule underpinned the appointments of Charan Singh (1979), V.P. Singh (1989), Chandra Shekhar (1990), P.V. Narasimha Rao (1991), A.B. Vajpayee (1996, 1998), H.D. Deve Gowda (1996), and I.K. Gujral (1997).
2. **Non-MP Eligible for Six Months (*Har Sharan Verma v. Tribhuvan Narain Singh*, 1971 & *S.P. Anand v. H.D. Deve Gowda*, 1996/1997):** Under **`Article 75(5)`**, a minister who for any period of **six consecutive months** is not a member of either House of Parliament ceases to be a minister at the expiration of that period. Building on its 1971 Constitution Bench ruling in ***Har Sharan Verma*** (which upheld the appointment of non-MLA T.N. Singh as Uttar Pradesh Chief Minister under the analogous `Article 164(4)`), the Supreme Court in ***S.P. Anand v. H.D. Deve Gowda*** (decided 6 November 1996; reported AIR 1997 SC 272) held that a person who is **not a member of either House of Parliament** at the time of swearing-in can validly be appointed **Prime Minister**—provided they are qualified to become an MP and secure membership of either the Lok Sabha or the Rajya Sabha within six months. (P.V. Narasimha Rao in 1991 won a Lok Sabha by-election from Nandyal within six months; H.D. Deve Gowda in 1996 entered the Rajya Sabha from Karnataka.)
3. **Bar on Repeated Six-Month Reappointments (*S.R. Chaudhuri v. State of Punjab*, 2001):** Could an executive abuse the six-month window by resigning on day 179 and being reappointed for another six months without ever facing voters? A three-judge Supreme Court bench emphatically answered **no**, ruling that reappointing a non-legislator as minister during the remainder of the same legislature's term without election violates the basic democratic principle of representative accountability and constitutes a **"fraud on the Constitution."**
4. **Statutorily Disqualified Person Barred Completely (*B.R. Kapur v. State of Tamil Nadu*, 2001):** A five-judge Constitution Bench (concerning J. Jayalalithaa's appointment as Chief Minister while convicted under the Prevention of Corruption Act and disqualified under `Section 8` of the Representation of the People Act, 1951) carved out an essential boundary: the six-month grace period (`Article 164(4)` and identically `Article 75(5)`) applies *only* to a non-legislator who is **constitutionally and statutorily qualified** to be chosen as a legislator. A person suffering a subsisting disqualification cannot be appointed Prime Minister, Chief Minister, or Minister even for a single day.

#### Membership of Either House: India vs the United Kingdom

A sharp constitutional divergence separates India from the Westminster prototype. In **Britain**, settled constitutional convention dictates that the Prime Minister **must belong exclusively to the Lower House (House of Commons)**—the last peer to govern from the House of Lords was Lord Salisbury (1895–1902), and when Sir Alec Douglas-Home (Earl of Home) became Prime Minister in 1963, he disclaimed his hereditary peerage and won a Commons seat immediately.

In **India**, by contrast, **the Prime Minister may constitutionally belong to either the Lok Sabha or the Rajya Sabha**. Three Indian Prime Ministers held office as members of the **Rajya Sabha**:
- **Indira Gandhi** (1966–1967, upon succeeding Lal Bahadur Shastri; she entered the Lok Sabha from Rae Bareli in 1967);
- **H.D. Deve Gowda** (1996–1997, who was Karnataka Chief Minister and a non-MP at swearing-in before entering the Rajya Sabha from Karnataka); and
- **Dr. Manmohan Singh** (2004–2014, serving two full five-year terms continuously as a Rajya Sabha MP from Assam).

Additionally, **I.K. Gujral** (1997–1998) also held office as a Rajya Sabha MP (from Bihar) throughout his premiership—making **four** Prime Ministers in all who governed from the Upper House. *(Note for Prelims: While Laxmikanth highlights the classic trio of Indira Gandhi, Deve Gowda, and Manmohan Singh, I.K. Gujral was also a sitting Rajya Sabha member during 1997–98.)* Crucially, even when the Prime Minister sits in the Rajya Sabha, they remain **collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha** under `Article 75(3)` and may participate freely in Lok Sabha debates under `Article 88`, though they **cannot vote in a Lok Sabha no-confidence motion**—a vivid illustration of `Article 88`'s voting restriction!

### Oath, Term, Removal and Emoluments

#### Oaths of Office and Secrecy (`Article 75(4)` & Third Schedule)

Before entering upon office, the Prime Minister is administered **two oaths (or affirmations)** by the **President of India**, as prescribed in **Forms I and II of the Third Schedule**:

1. **Oath of Office (Form I):** The Prime Minister swears (a) to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution of India as by law established, (b) to uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India *(words added by the `16th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963`)*, (c) to faithfully and conscientiously discharge the duties of the office, and (d) to do right to all manner of people in accordance with the Constitution and the law, **without fear or favour, affection or ill-will**.
2. **Oath of Secrecy (Form II):** The Prime Minister swears not to directly or indirectly communicate or reveal to any person(s) any matter brought under consideration or known as a Union Minister, except as may be required for the due discharge of ministerial duties.

Notice a perennial UPSC Prelims trap: **the Third Schedule contains NO separate form of oath for the Prime Minister**! Both the Prime Minister and every other Union Minister subscribe to the exact same ministerial oath of office and secrecy—a point judicially underscored by the Supreme Court in ***K.M. Sharma v. Devi Lal* (1990)**.

#### Term of Office and Removal

The term of the Prime Minister is **not fixed** by the Constitution. Under **`Article 75(2)`**, ministers (including the PM) hold office **"during the pleasure of the President."** In constitutional practice, however, Presidential pleasure is neither personal nor arbitrary; it operates as the legal vessel for parliamentary confidence. So long as the Prime Minister commands the **confidence of a majority in the Lok Sabha**, the President **cannot** dismiss the Prime Minister. Conversely, the moment the Prime Minister loses Lok Sabha confidence—either through passage of a **No-Confidence Motion**, defeat on a **Confidence Motion**, rejection of a **Money Bill** or **Motion of Thanks to the President's Address**, or loss of party majority—the Prime Minister must resign; if the Prime Minister refuses to resign after demonstrably losing majority support, the President may constitutionally withdraw pleasure and **dismiss** them.

Unlike the US President (capped at two four-year terms by the 22nd Amendment), there is **no constitutional term limit** on how many times a person may be appointed Prime Minister of India.

#### Salary and Allowances (`Article 75(6)`)

Under **`Article 75(6)`**, the salaries and allowances of Ministers (including the Prime Minister) are **determined by Parliament from time to time** by law (until so determined, they were governed by the Second Schedule—a transitional provision superseded by the **Salaries and Allowances of Ministers Act, 1952**). Under current law, the Prime Minister receives the **same basic salary and allowances payable to a Member of Parliament**, supplemented by a **sumptuary allowance**, rent-free official residence (**7, Lok Kalyan Marg**, New Delhi), free travelling facilities, medical care, and Special Protection Group (SPG) security. Crucially, the Prime Minister's salary is **NOT charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**—it is voted by Parliament—and the Prime Minister is **NOT listed in the Second Schedule** (which covers the President, Governors, Presiding Officers, Judges, and CAG).

> [!prelims]
> **High-Yield Prelims Traps on the Prime Minister:**
> 1. **Selection Procedure:** The Constitution prescribes **NO specific procedure** for selecting the PM—`Art. 75(1)` only says the PM is appointed by the President.
> 2. **Minimum Age:** The Constitution does NOT mention an explicit age for PM; because the PM must be eligible for either House, the minimum age is **25 years** (if from Lok Sabha) or **30 years** (if from Rajya Sabha).
> 3. **Second Schedule Exclusion:** The Prime Minister and Union Ministers are **NOT** included in the Second Schedule (emoluments); their salaries are fixed by Parliament under `Art. 75(6)` and are **not** charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.
> 4. **Rajya Sabha PM & Voting:** A PM sitting in the Rajya Sabha **cannot vote** on a No-Confidence Motion in the Lok Sabha (`Art. 88`) and **cannot vote** in a Lok Sabha division, though they can speak and defend the government on the Lok Sabha floor.
> 5. **Sovereignty and Integrity Phrase:** The words *"uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India"* in the Third Schedule oath were inserted by the **`16th Amendment Act, 1963`** (following the National Integration Committee recommendations).

### Comprehensive Powers and Functions of the Prime Minister

As the fulcrum of the parliamentary executive, the Prime Minister exercises sweeping authority spanning four distinct institutional domains: the Council of Ministers, the President, Parliament, and apex national governance bodies.

#### 1. Powers in Relation to the Council of Ministers

The Prime Minister is the architect, coordinator, and life-force of the Union Ministry:
- **Formation of the Ministry (`Art. 75(1)`):** The President can appoint **only those persons** as Union Ministers whose names are formally recommended by the Prime Minister. In a single-party majority, the PM enjoys virtually plenary choice; in coalitions, names emerge through negotiation with alliance chiefs, though formal constitutional advice remains exclusively the PM's.
- **Allocation and Reshuffling of Portfolios:** The Prime Minister decides which minister heads which ministry or department and may reallocate or reshuffle portfolios at any time to match political needs or administrative performance.
- **Discipline and Dismissal:** The PM can ask any minister to resign or—if the minister refuses—advise the President to dismiss them under `Article 75(2)` whenever serious policy differences or integrity failures arise.
- **Presiding Over and Directing the Cabinet:** The PM summons Cabinet meetings, sets their agenda (assisted by the Cabinet Secretariat and PMO), presides over deliberations, sums up the "sense of the Cabinet," and guides, directs, controls, and coordinates the activities of all ministries.
- **Total Collapse Upon PM's Exit:** This is the ultimate proof of prime ministerial pre-eminence. **The resignation or death of the Prime Minister automatically dissolves the entire Council of Ministers**, because the PM stands at the head of the Council (`Art. 74(1)`). By contrast, the resignation or death of any other Cabinet Minister merely creates a single ministerial vacancy that the PM may fill or leave vacant.

#### 2. Powers in Relation to the President (`Article 78`)

The Prime Minister serves as the **sole official channel of communication** between the President and the Council of Ministers. Individual ministers cannot ordinarily bypass the Prime Minister to tender conflicting advice directly to Rashtrapati Bhavan. **`Article 78`** casts **three explicit constitutional duties** upon the Prime Minister (frequently tested verbatim in UPSC Prelims, e.g., **UPSC Prelims 2013**):

1. **`Article 78(a)` — Duty to Communicate:** To communicate to the President **all decisions of the Council of Ministers** relating to the administration of the affairs of the Union and **proposals for legislation**;
2. **`Article 78(b)` — Duty to Furnish Information:** To furnish such information relating to the administration of the affairs of the Union and proposals for legislation as the **President may call for**; and
3. **`Article 78(c)` — Duty to Submit Individual Decisions to Full Council:** If the President so requires, to submit for the **consideration of the Council of Ministers** any matter on which a decision has been taken by a Minister but which has **not been considered by the Council** (a vital constitutional weapon enabling the President to reinforce Cabinet collective responsibility when a minister or PM acts unilaterally).

Beyond `Article 78`, the Prime Minister effectively determines all major **constitutional and statutory appointments** made formally by the President—including the **Attorney General of India**, the **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)**, the **Chairman and members of the UPSC**, the **Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners** (through the statutory selection committee chaired by the PM under the 2023 Act), the **Chairman and members of the Finance Commission**, **Governors of States**, ambassadors, and heads of statutory regulatory bodies.

#### 3. Powers in Relation to Parliament

If the Prime Minister is a member of the Lok Sabha, they serve as **Leader of the House** (if from the Rajya Sabha, a senior Lok Sabha Cabinet Minister is designated Leader of the Lok Sabha while the PM may lead the Rajya Sabha). Across Parliament, the Prime Minister:
- Advises the President regarding **summoning and proroguing** sessions of Parliament (operationalised through the Cabinet Committee on Parliamentary Affairs);
- Can recommend the **dissolution of the Lok Sabha** to the President at any time (binding so long as the PM commands a majority; subject to Presidential situational discretion if the PM has already lost majority support);
- Announces major national and foreign policy statements on the floor of the House, intervenes in critical debates to rescue embattled ministers, and steers the government's legislative agenda.

#### 4. Other Powers and Ex-Officio Leadership of Apex National Bodies

To ensure unified national direction across federalism, development planning, science, security, and disaster response, the Prime Minister serves as **ex-officio Chairperson** of India's most pivotal constitutional, statutory, and executive national institutions:

- **NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India):** Replaced the Planning Commission on 1 January 2015 as the Union's premier policy think-tank and platform for cooperative and competitive federalism (PM chairs its Governing Council comprising all Chief Ministers and UT Lieutenant Governors).
- **National Development Council (NDC):** Extra-constitutional body created in 1952 (functionally eclipsed since NITI Aayog's Governing Council took over, though never formally dissolved by notification).
- **Inter-State Council (ISC):** Constitutional body constituted under **`Article 263`** (established in 1990 on Sarkaria Commission's recommendation) to resolve inter-state and Centre–State disputes and coordinate common-interest policy.
- **National Integration Council (NIC):** Extra-constitutional advisory forum constituted in 1961 to combat communalism, casteism, regionalism, and linguistic chauvinism.
- **National Water Resources Council (NWRC):** Apex inter-governmental body (established 1983) formulating the National Water Policy and promoting rational inter-state water development.
- **National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA):** Statutory apex body under the **Disaster Management Act, 2005**, laying down national disaster policies, plans, and guidelines.
- **Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR):** Ex-officio President of India's premier public R&D network.
- **Nuclear Command Authority (NCA) — Political Council:** Chairs the **Political Council**—the *sole* body authorised to order the use of India's nuclear weapons (assisted by the Executive Council chaired by the National Security Advisor).
- **Direct Strategic Portfolios:** By long-standing convention, the Prime Minister directly retains charge of the **Department of Atomic Energy (DAE)**, the **Department of Space (DoS)**, and the **Ministry of Personnel, Public Grievances and Pensions** (which controls the IAS, UPSC interface, CBI administrative control, and Central Staffing Scheme), alongside all portfolios not allocated to any other minister.

In addition, the Prime Minister is the **chief architect and spokesperson of India's foreign policy** (representing India at G20, BRICS, QUAD, SCO, and UN summits), **crisis manager-in-chief** during wars, pandemics, and economic shocks, **leader of the ruling party**, **political head of the civil and military services**, and the primary political face of the nation before the electorate.

### Relationship Between the Prime Minister and the President

The President–PM equation rests on the constitutional triad of **`Articles 74`, `75`, and `78`**: binding ministerial advice (`Art. 74(1)`, `42nd Amendment`), one-time Presidential reconsideration (`44th Amendment`), appointment and collective responsibility (`Art. 75`), and the PM's continuous duty to inform Rashtrapati Bhavan (`Art. 78`).

In healthy parliamentary practice, this relationship embodies **Walter Bagehot's classic three rights of a constitutional head of state**: *"the right to be consulted, the right to encourage, and the right to warn."* Yet Indian constitutional history records notable episodes of friction and principled balance:
- **Jawaharlal Nehru and Dr. Rajendra Prasad (1950–1962):** Differed over the Hindu Code Bill, the Somnath Temple inauguration (1951), and Prasad's 1960 Indian Law Institute address; Attorney General M.C. Setalvad affirmed that `Article 74` bound the President to Cabinet advice.
- **Rajiv Gandhi and Giani Zail Singh (1985–1987):** Marked the sharpest President–PM crisis. Zail Singh accused Rajiv Gandhi of breaching **`Article 78`** by withholding briefings on the Punjab/Assam accords, Bofors, and the Thakkar Commission report, and exercised India's celebrated **Pocket Veto** on the **Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986**.
- **Atal Bihari Vajpayee and K.R. Narayanan (1998–2002):** Exemplified constructive constitutional restraint. President Narayanan twice invoked the `44th Amendment` reconsideration proviso—returning Cabinet recommendations for President's Rule in Uttar Pradesh (1997, under Gujral) and Bihar (1998, under Vajpayee)—and both Cabinets respected his reasoned warning and withdrew the advice.

### Political Theorists, Chief Ministers Who Became PM and the Prime Ministerial Government Debate

#### Classical Characterisations by Constitutional Scholars

Because the Prime Minister's formal constitutional text is concise while actual political reach is immense, political scientists have coined enduring metaphors frequently cited in UPSC Prelims matching questions and Mains essays:

| Political Theorist / Jurist | Classic Characterisation of the Prime Minister | Core Constitutional Insight |
|---|---|---|
| **Lord Morley** | *Primus inter pares* ("First among equals") & **"Keystone of the Cabinet arch"** | While all ministers are legally equal colleagues, removing the PM causes the entire Cabinet arch to collapse. |
| **Sir William Vernon Harcourt** | *Inter stellas luna minores* (**"A moon among lesser stars"**) | Captures the clear asymmetry in political stature between the PM and Cabinet colleagues. |
| **Sir Ivor Jennings** | **"A sun around which planets revolve"** & **"Keystone of the Constitution"** | All roads in the Constitution lead to the PM; ministers orbit around prime ministerial authority. |
| **Harold J. Laski** | **"Pivot around which the entire governmental machinery revolves"** | Called the PM *"central to its formation, central to its life, and central to its death"* (referring to the Cabinet). |
| **Ramsay Muir** | **"The steersman of the steering wheel of the ship of the State"** | If the Cabinet is the steering wheel of the ship of State, the PM holds firm command over that wheel. |
| **William B. Munro** | **"Captain of the ship of the State"** | Emphasises unified executive command, navigation, and ultimate responsibility during national crises. |
| **H.R.G. Greaves** | **"The Government is the master of the country and the PM is the master of the Government"** | Highlights how party discipline and parliamentary majority convert Cabinet supremacy into PM supremacy. |
| **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** | **Comparable to the US President** (not India's President) | Underscores that India's real executive power lies in the Prime Minister rather than the ceremonial Head of State. |

#### Six Chief Ministers Who Became Prime Minister of India

A notable federal dimension of Indian politics—occasionally tested directly in Prelims and valuable for Mains discussions on cooperative federalism—is that **six Prime Ministers** rose to national leadership after serving as **Chief Ministers of States**:

| Prime Minister | State Where Served as Chief Minister | Transition Pathway to Prime Ministership |
|---|---|---|
| **Morarji Desai** | **Bombay State** (1952–1956) | Became first non-Congress PM (1977–1979) after long Union ministerial innings (Finance, Deputy PM). |
| **Charan Singh** | **Uttar Pradesh** (1967–1968, 1970) | Became Union Home/Finance Minister & Deputy PM before serving as PM (1979–1980). |
| **V.P. Singh** | **Uttar Pradesh** (1980–1982) | Served as Union Finance & Defence Minister before leading the National Front government (1989–1990). |
| **P.V. Narasimha Rao** | **Andhra Pradesh** (1971–1973) | Served as Union External Affairs, Home & Defence Minister before becoming PM (1991–1996); architect of 1991 economic reforms. |
| **H.D. Deve Gowda** | **Karnataka** (1994–1996) | Transitioned **directly from sitting Chief Minister to Prime Minister** (June 1996–April 1997). |
| **Narendra Modi** | **Gujarat** (2001–2014, four terms) | Transitioned **directly from sitting Chief Minister to Prime Minister** (May 2014–present; re-elected 2019 & 2024). |

*(Observe the sharp Prelims distinction: While six former Chief Ministers became Prime Minister, only **two**—**H.D. Deve Gowda** and **Narendra Modi**—stepped directly from a State Chief Minister's office into the Prime Minister's chair!)*

#### From "Cabinet Government" to "Prime Ministerial Government"

In Mains GS-II, UPSC repeatedly examines whether India has shifted from classical **Cabinet Government** (collective collegiality where the PM is genuinely *primus inter pares*) to **Prime Ministerial Government** (a term popularised in Britain by **Richard Crossman** and **John Mackintosh**). Crucially, India's constitutional text has remained unchanged since 1950—what changes is the **electoral and party arithmetic** underneath it:

- **Phase I — Nehruvian Collegial Supremacy (1947–1964):** Despite towering moral authority, Jawaharlal Nehru operated alongside formidable Cabinet equals (Sardar Patel, Maulana Azad, Rafi Ahmed Kidwai, Govind Ballabh Pant, Morarji Desai) and respected lively Cabinet debate and Chief Ministers' autonomy.
- **Phase II — Centralisation & Personalised Mandate (1969–1989):** Following the 1969 Congress split and the 1971 landslide, **Indira Gandhi** decisively shifted gravity from the Cabinet to the Prime Minister's Secretariat (under P.N. Haksar), culminating in the 1975 Emergency proclamation issued without prior Cabinet approval—directly prompting the `44th Amendment` requirement of written Cabinet recommendation (`Art. 352(3)`). Rajiv Gandhi's historic 414-seat majority (1984) continued strong prime ministerial primacy.
- **Phase III — Coalition Era & Restrained Premiership (1989–2014):** For twenty-five years, hung Lok Sabhas restored intense collegial—and sometimes fragmented—bargaining. Coalition leaders (V.P. Singh, Deve Gowda, Vajpayee, Manmohan Singh) had to accommodate assertive regional satraps who controlled key portfolios and demanded consultation via Common Minimum Programmes and Coordination Committees. During UPA I & II (2004–2014), critics noted a novel structural tension: **dual power centres**, where political authority rested largely with the Congress President / National Advisory Council (NAC) while constitutional executive responsibility sat with Prime Minister Manmohan Singh, eroding the traditional unity of the PM's office.
- **Phase IV — Single-Party Majority Revival (2014–2024) & Coalition Calibration (2024–Present):** Back-to-back single-party BJP majorities in 2014 (282 seats) and 2019 (303 seats) revived a muscular, presidentialised style of prime ministerial leadership anchored in direct voter appeal, strong PMO coordination, and whole-of-government monitoring (PRAGATI, Gati Shakti). Following the **2024 General Elections**—where the BJP won 240 seats and formed the **NDA 3.0** coalition government with allies such as TDP, JD(U), JD(S), Shiv Sena, LJP (Ram Vilas), HAM, and RLD—executive practice has exhibited calibrated coalition accommodation (visible in the July 2024 induction of key alliance ministers into major Standing Cabinet Committees like CCPA, CCEA, Investment & Growth, and Parliamentary Affairs) alongside continued strategic steering from the Prime Minister.

> [!debate]
> **Cabinet Government vs Prime Ministerial Government — Is Centralisation Harmful?**
> - **Case Against "Prime Ministerial Government":** Excessive personalisation of authority weakens Cabinet collegiality, turns line ministers into mere executors, sidelines intra-Cabinet dissent that catches policy flaws early, concentrates risk in the PMO, and erodes the spirit of collective responsibility (`Art. 75(3)`).
> - **Case for Strong Prime Ministerial Steering:** Modern governance demands rapid crisis decisions (pandemics, border conflicts, supply-chain shocks), decisive breaking of inter-ministerial silos, coherent long-term national missions (infrastructure, energy transition, digital public infrastructure), and clear electoral accountability to voters who increasingly vote for a prime ministerial leadership vision.
> - **Constitutional Synthesis:** India's Constitution works best through **"Collegial Primacy"**—a decisive Prime Minister who sets strategic direction and enforces accountability, balanced by empowered Cabinet Ministers, active Standing Cabinet Committees, a neutral Cabinet Secretariat, and vigorous parliamentary scrutiny (Question Hour, DRSCs, floor debates).

> [!mains]
> **Mains GS-II Answer-Writing Pointer:** Whenever a question asks *"Has the Indian parliamentary executive evolved into a presidentialised prime ministerial system?"*, avoid a one-sided ideological answer. Anchor your argument in **three institutional variables**: (1) **Electoral Arithmetic** (single-party majority vs coalition dependence), (2) **Institutional Checks** (`44th Amendment` written Cabinet mandate in `Art. 352(3)`, `Art. 78(c)`, Supreme Court review, Rajya Sabha federalism, State governments under rival parties), and (3) **Administrative Design** (balancing PMO strategic steering with Cabinet Secretariat neutrality and Second ARC's empowered ministries).

## 2. Union Council of Ministers: Constitutional Architecture, Responsibility and Composition

Because the Constitution vests formal executive power in the President (`Article 53`) yet intends democratic governance accountable to the elected House, the **Union Council of Ministers (CoM)** constitutes the constitutional bridge between the Executive and the Legislature. Walter Bagehot famously described the Cabinet as *"a hyphen which joins, a buckle which fastens, the legislative part of the State to the executive part of the State."* In India, this constitutional machinery is governed by five interlocking provisions—**`Articles 74`, `75`, `77`, `78`, and `88`**—whose phrasing has been refined by three landmark Constitutional Amendments (**`42nd`**, **`44th`**, and **`91st`**) and a rich body of Supreme Court jurisprudence.

### Exhaustive Article-by-Article Constitutional Framework

#### Article 74: Council of Ministers to Aid and Advise President

`Article 74` is the linchpin of India's parliamentary democracy and comprises two clauses:

- **`Article 74(1)` — Mandatory Existence & Binding Advice:** *"There shall be a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to aid and advise the President who shall, in the exercise of his functions, act in accordance with such advice."*
  - **Original 1950 Text:** Stated only that there shall be a CoM to aid and advise the President—without explicitly using the words *"shall act in accordance with such advice."* Although constitutional convention and the seven-judge bench in ***Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab* (1974)** had already established that such advice was binding, the **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`** removed all textual ambiguity by expressly writing bindingness into `Article 74(1)`.
  - **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978` Proviso:** Concerned that absolute bindingness left the President powerless against hasty or unconstitutional Cabinet actions (as witnessed during the 1975 Emergency), the Janata government added a critical **proviso** to `Article 74(1)`: *"Provided that the President may require the Council of Ministers to reconsider such advice, either generally or otherwise, and the President **shall** act in accordance with the advice tendered after such reconsideration."* Thus, the President enjoys a **one-time suspensive check**—capable of forcing sober second thought—but must yield if the Cabinet reiterates its advice.
  - **Mandatory Continuity After Dissolution (*U.N.R. Rao v. Smt. Indira Gandhi*, 1971):** After the Lok Sabha was dissolved in December 1970, a petition challenged Indira Gandhi's continued functioning as Prime Minister on the ground that collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha (`Art. 75(3)`) had vanished. A five-judge Constitution Bench rejected the challenge, ruling that **`Article 74(1)` is mandatory**: *"There shall be a Council of Ministers"* admits of **no hiatus**. Even after the Lok Sabha is dissolved, the outgoing Council of Ministers **does not cease to hold office**; it continues as a **caretaker government** until a new ministry is sworn in, because the President cannot constitutionally discharge executive functions without ministerial aid and advice. (By convention, however, a caretaker government avoids taking major new policy or financial commitments unless urgent national interest requires them.)
- **`Article 74(2)` — Bar on Judicial Inquiry into Advice:** *"The question whether any, and if so what, advice was tendered by Ministers to the President shall not be inquired into in any court."*
  - **Rationale:** Protects the confidentiality, candour, and constitutional intimacy between the President and the Council of Ministers, ensuring that the formal Head of State is never dragged into political litigation.
  - **Crucial Judicial Nuance (*S.R. Bommai v. Union of India*, 1994, 9-Judge Bench):** Does `Article 74(2)` immunise executive decisions from judicial review? The Supreme Court drew a sharp, celebrated distinction: while courts are strictly barred from probing the *content of the advice* tendered to the President, **`Article 74(2)` does NOT bar courts from examining the underlying *material* on which that advice was formulated**! If the material is irrelevant, extraneous, or *mala fide* (e.g., an arbitrary `Article 356` proclamation), the resulting Presidential order can be struck down as unconstitutional.

#### Article 75: Other Provisions as to Ministers (Including the 91st Amendment)

`Article 75` contains **eight distinct provisions** (`75(1)`, `75(1A)`, `75(1B)`, `75(2)`, `75(3)`, `75(4)`, `75(5)`, `75(6)`), every single clause of which is high-yield for Prelims:

1. **`Article 75(1)` — Appointment:** The Prime Minister is appointed by the President, and the other Ministers are appointed by the President **on the advice of the Prime Minister**.
2. **`Article 75(1A)` — 15% Ceiling on Ministry Size (`91st Amendment Act, 2003`):** *"The total number of Ministers, **including the Prime Minister**, in the Council of Ministers shall not exceed **fifteen per cent** of the total number of members of the **House of the People**."*
   - **Historical Context:** During the coalition turmoil of the 1990s and early 2000s, governments routinely inflated ministries to jumbo proportions (e.g., 90+ ministers in Uttar Pradesh) to reward defectors and buy legislative survival—draining the public exchequer and fragmenting administration. Acting on recommendations of the **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC, 2002)**, the **Law Commission (170th Report, 1999)**, and the **Dinesh Goswami Committee**, Parliament enacted the **`91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003`** (effective 1 January 2004).
   - **Exact Arithmetic & Prelims Trap:** With Lok Sabha's current strength of **543**, the Union Council of Ministers can have a maximum of **81 ministers** (15% of 543 = 81.45), **including the Prime Minister**. Crucially, the denominator is the total strength of the **Lok Sabha ONLY**—*never* the combined strength of Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha! (At the State level, parallel `Article 164(1A)` sets a 15% cap of Assembly strength with a minimum floor of 12 ministers, including the Chief Minister; for Delhi, `Article 239AA(4)` sets a stricter 10% cap, i.e., max 7 ministers.)
3. **`Article 75(1B)` — Anti-Defection Disqualification Bar (`91st Amendment Act, 2003`):** A member of either House of Parliament belonging to any political party who is **disqualified for being a member of that House under Paragraph 2 of the Tenth Schedule** (anti-defection law) shall **also be disqualified to be appointed as a Minister** under `Article 75(1)`.
   - **Duration of Bar:** The bar operates from the date of disqualification until the date on which their term as a member would expire, OR until the date on which they contest an election to either House and are **declared elected**, whichever is **earlier**.
   - **Companion Provision (`Article 361B`):** Simultaneously inserted by the `91st Amendment`, `Article 361B` closes the backdoor loophole by disqualifying a Tenth Schedule defector from holding any **"remunerative political post"** (such as chairperson of a PSU, statutory board, or corporation wholly/partially funded by the government) for the exact same period.
4. **`Article 75(2)` — Individual Pleasure:** The Ministers shall hold office **during the pleasure of the President** (source of individual ministerial responsibility).
5. **`Article 75(3)` — Collective Responsibility:** The Council of Ministers shall be **collectively responsible to the House of the People (Lok Sabha)** (not to Parliament as a whole, and never to the Rajya Sabha).
6. **`Article 75(4)` — Oaths:** Before a Minister enters upon office, the President administers the oaths of office and secrecy according to the forms set out in the **Third Schedule**.
7. **`Article 75(5)` — Six-Month Membership Rule:** A Minister who for any period of **six consecutive months** is not a member of either House of Parliament ceases to be a Minister at the expiration of that period (subject to *S.R. Chaudhuri* [2001] no-reappointment rule and *B.R. Kapur* [2001] no-disqualification rule).
8. **`Article 75(6)` — Salaries and Allowances:** Determined by Parliament from time to time by law (Salaries and Allowances of Ministers Act, 1952).

> [!news]
> **The Constitution (130th Amendment) Bill, 2025 — Mandatory Removal Upon 30-Day Detention:**
> Introduced in the Lok Sabha on **20 August 2025** (alongside companion amendments to the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act and the Government of Union Territories Act), the **Constitution (130th Amendment) Bill, 2025** proposes amending **`Articles 75`, `164`, and `239AA`** (inserting `Article 75(5A)`) to introduce a strict integrity threshold for political executives:
> - **Trigger Conditions:** Any Union Minister, State Minister, Prime Minister, or Chief Minister who is **arrested and detained in custody for 30 consecutive days** on an allegation of committing an offence punishable with **five years or more** imprisonment (conviction or even framing of charges is *not* required).
> - **Mechanism:** For a Minister, the President (or Governor) removes them on the PM's (or CM's) advice by the 31st day, failing which they **automatically cease to hold office from day 31**. A detained **PM or CM must tender resignation** by the 31st day, failing which they automatically cease to hold office from the following day. However, **reappointment is expressly permitted** upon release from custody.
> - **Current Status (November 2026):** Referred immediately to a **Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC)** chaired by **Aparajita Sarangi**; the Bill remains **pending as of November 2026** after the JPC deferred adoption of its draft report in July 2026 and received an extension until the Winter Session 2026.
> - **Mains Constitutional Debate:** Proponents argue it upholds **constitutional morality** and prevents governance from prison (echoing recent controversies over Chief Ministers continuing in office from judicial custody). Critics and constitutional analysts (including submissions before the JPC) warn of potential **Basic Structure frictions**: undermining **presumption of innocence** (removing elected leaders on mere pre-trial detention without conviction), **separation of powers** (allowing executive investigative agencies to unseat a legislature-backed PM or CM), **parliamentary democracy** (`Art. 75(3)`), and **federalism** (central agencies potentially triggering removal of Opposition State CMs, or state police triggering removal of Union ministers).

#### Article 77: Conduct of Business of the Government of India

`Article 77` establishes the formal legal machinery through which the Union Government operates:
- **`Article 77(1)`:** All executive action of the Government of India shall be expressed to be taken in the **name of the President**.
- **`Article 77(2)`:** Orders and other instruments made and executed in the name of the President shall be **authenticated** in such manner as may be specified in rules made by the President (the Authentication [Orders and Other Instruments] Rules), and the validity of an order so authenticated **shall not be called in question** on the ground that it is not an order made or executed by the President personally.
- **`Article 77(3)`:** The President shall make rules for the **more convenient transaction of the business** of the Government of India, and for the **allocation among Ministers** of the said business. Under this single clause rest the two foundational rulebooks of India's central administration: the **Government of India (Allocation of Business) Rules, 1961** and the **Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961**. As the seven-judge Constitution Bench affirmed in ***Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab* (1974)**, allocation under `Article 77(3)` is not a mere delegation of authority—the decision of a competent minister or authorised secretary under the Rules of Business *is* constitutionally the decision of the President.

#### Article 88: Rights of Ministers as Respects Houses

`Article 88` ensures smooth coordination across India's bicameral Parliament: *"Every Minister and the Attorney-General of India shall have the right to speak in, and otherwise to take part in the proceedings of, **either House**, any **joint sitting** of the Houses, and any **committee of Parliament** of which he may be named a member, but shall not by virtue of this article be entitled to **vote**."*

Three sharp Prelims consequences flow from `Article 88`:
1. A Minister who is a **Lok Sabha MP** may attend, answer questions, pilot bills, and debate in the **Rajya Sabha**, but can vote **only in the Lok Sabha**.
2. A Minister who is a **Rajya Sabha MP** (including a Rajya Sabha Prime Minister!) may pilot bills and participate in debates in the **Lok Sabha**, but can vote **only in the Rajya Sabha** (and therefore cannot vote on a Lok Sabha No-Confidence Motion).
3. A **non-MP Minister** serving during their six-month grace period under `Article 75(5)` enjoys full rights under `Article 88` to speak and participate in **both** Houses, but **cannot vote in either House** until elected or nominated!

### The Three Principles of Ministerial Responsibility

Parliamentary accountability in India rests upon three distinct doctrinal pillars—two positive constitutional duties and one deliberate omission contrasting with British law.

#### 1. Principle of Collective Responsibility (`Article 75(3)`)

**Collective responsibility** is the bedrock principle of the parliamentary system. **`Article 75(3)`** declares explicitly that *"The Council of Ministers shall be collectively responsible to the House of the People."* Notice the precision: collective responsibility runs **specifically to the Lok Sabha—NOT to Parliament as a whole and NOT to the Rajya Sabha**. Defeat of the government on an amendment or motion in the Rajya Sabha does **not** obligate the Council of Ministers to resign (though it may stall non-Money Bill legislation).

Collective responsibility operates across **two reinforcing dimensions**:

- **External Political Accountability ("Swim Together and Sink Together"):** The Council of Ministers works as a single team and owns shared responsibility before the Lok Sabha for all acts of omission and commission. When the Lok Sabha passes a **No-Confidence Motion** against the Council of Ministers (or defeats a Money Bill / Budget Demand / Motion of Thanks to the President's Address), **every single member of the Council of Ministers must resign—including those Ministers who belong to the Rajya Sabha**! Alternatively, the defeated Council of Ministers may advise the President to dissolve the Lok Sabha and call fresh elections on the ground that the House no longer reflects the electorate's will, though the President is *not* bound to accept dissolution advice from a ministry that has already lost majority support.
- **Internal Cabinet Solidarity:** Collective responsibility equally enforces discipline *within* the executive. Every Cabinet decision binds all ministers—Cabinet Ministers, Ministers of State, and Deputy Ministers alike—even if an individual minister argued fiercely against it behind closed doors in the Cabinet room. Once a decision is adopted, every minister must publicly defend and implement it both inside and outside Parliament. If a minister finds a Cabinet decision irreconcilable with their conscience or political convictions, **the sole honourable constitutional exit is resignation**. Indian parliamentary history offers memorable examples of principled adherence to this norm:
  - **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** resigned as Law Minister in **1951** over Cabinet reluctance on the Hindu Code Bill;
  - **C.D. Deshmukh** resigned as Finance Minister in **1956** over the policy on reorganisation of States and the status of Bombay;
  - **Arif Mohammad Khan** resigned as Minister of State in **1986** opposing the government's Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Bill reversing the *Shah Bano* judgment;
  - **Harsimrat Kaur Badal** resigned as Food Processing Industries Minister in **September 2020** dissenting from the three Union Farm Laws.

#### Judicial Boundaries of Collective Responsibility (*Common Cause* 1999 & *Kaushal Kishor* 2023)

Two major Supreme Court judgments have probed whether political collective responsibility translates into judicial or vicarious legal liability:

- ***Common Cause (A Registered Society) v. Union of India* (3 August 1999, 3-Judge Bench):** Dealing with arbitrary allotment of 15 retail petrol outlets out of a discretionary ministerial quota by Petroleum Minister Capt. Satish Sharma, the Supreme Court expounded the twin constitutional meanings of collective responsibility (unanimity in public defence of policies + shared political fate before the Lok Sabha). While cancelling the arbitrary allotments and affirming that ministers hold public discretion as **trustees under the Public Trust Doctrine**, the Court recalled an earlier order imposing ₹50 lakh personal exemplary damages without trial—clarifying that collective responsibility is primarily a **political accountability mechanism** and does not automatically make the whole government or a minister criminally liable absent statutory procedure, even though individual arbitrary executive acts remain fully reviewable under `Article 14`.
- ***Kaushal Kishor v. State of Uttar Pradesh* (3 January 2023, 5-Judge Constitution Bench, 4–1):** Triggered by insensitive remarks made by a State Minister dismissing the Bulandshahr gang-rape incident as a "political conspiracy," the Constitution Bench squarely addressed whether a minister's disparaging statement traceable to affairs of State can be **vicariously attributed to the Government** by invoking **collective responsibility** (`Articles 75(3)` / `164(2)`), making it actionable as a "constitutional tort."

> [!case]
> **Landmark Judgment Deep Dive: *Kaushal Kishor v. State of Uttar Pradesh* (3 January 2023, 5-Judge Constitution Bench, 4–1)**
> - **Bench:** Justices S. Abdul Nazeer, B.R. Gavai, A.S. Bopanna, V. Ramasubramanian (majority opinion), and **Justice B.V. Nagarathna** (partial dissent).
> - **Core Holding on Collective Responsibility (4–1 Majority):** Collective responsibility is an **essentially political concept** linking the Council of Ministers to the Lok Sabha / Legislative Assembly regarding formal decisions and acts of omission or commission. Crucially, **"the flow of the stream in collective responsibility is from the Council of Ministers to the individual minister—and NOT the reverse."** A stray, ill-judged oral remark made by a single minister outside the legislature—even if traceable to affairs of State or made to defend the government—**cannot automatically be attributed vicariously to the Government** as a constitutional tort. The Court also noted practically that in multi-party coalition governments, a PM or CM does not exercise absolute disciplinary whip over every ministerial utterance. (Only if a minister's statement directly results in concrete executive action depriving a citizen's rights does constitutional tort liability arise.)
> - **Justice B.V. Nagarathna's Powerful Dissent:** Argued that when a minister makes a disparaging statement **traceable to official affairs of State** (rather than purely personal remarks), it *should* be vicariously attributable to the government unless the government promptly disowns it, urging Parliament to enact a **voluntary Code of Conduct** for ministers to protect citizens' `Article 21` dignity against hate speech by high public functionaries.
> - **Additional Milestone:** The same judgment held that `Article 19(2)` free-speech restrictions are **exhaustive** (no extra judge-made curbs on ministerial free speech) and that Fundamental Rights under `Articles 19` and `21` can operate **horizontally** even against non-State actors.

#### 2. Principle of Individual Responsibility (`Article 75(2)`)

`Article 75(2)` embeds the complementary principle of **individual responsibility**: *"The Ministers shall hold office during the pleasure of the President."* Legally, this empowers the President to remove any individual minister at any time—even while the Council of Ministers as a whole enjoys full Lok Sabha confidence. Politically, however, the President exercises this pleasure **strictly on the advice of the Prime Minister**.

Individual responsibility is the enforcement engine behind collective responsibility. If an individual minister proves inefficient, faces serious corruption allegations, undermines a departmental programme, or refuses to fall in line with Cabinet consensus, the Prime Minister does not need to destabilise the entire government—they simply obtain the minister's resignation or advise the President to dismiss them. As Dr. Ambedkar remarked in the Constituent Assembly, collective responsibility can be achieved *only* through the instrumentality of the Prime Minister's power to nominate and dismiss individual colleagues.

#### 3. No Legal Responsibility (India vs United Kingdom)

In the **United Kingdom**, the maxim *"The King can do no wrong"* means the Monarch enjoys complete legal immunity and can never be sued for official acts. Consequently, **every order of the British Crown for a public act must be countersigned by a minister**, who thereby assumes **personal legal responsibility** in a court of law if the order violates any statute.

**India deliberately rejected British ministerial legal responsibility.** Two constitutional provisions establish this negative rule:
1. The Constitution of India imposes **no requirement that a Presidential order be countersigned by a minister** (authentication under `Article 77(2)` is performed by authorised civil servants—Joint Secretaries and above—not ministers as countersigners); and
2. **`Article 74(2)`** expressly forbids any court from inquiring into the nature of advice tendered by ministers to the President.

*(Mains Clarity: "No legal responsibility" does NOT mean ministers stand above the law! It refers strictly to the absence of UK-style countersignature liability for Presidential acts. An executive order issued in the President's name can still be challenged in courts by suing the **Union of India** under `Article 300` or via writ petitions under `Articles 32/226`, and individual ministers remain fully prosecutable under criminal law—such as the Prevention of Corruption Act—as well as liable for mala fide abuse of statutory discretion.)*

### Composition of the Council of Ministers: Three Tiers, Parliamentary Secretaries and Deputy PM

A classic UPSC Prelims trap (**tested directly in UPSC Prelims 2022!**) asks whether the Constitution classifies Union Ministers into ranks. The answer is an emphatic **NO**: **the Constitution of India does NOT classify ministers into Cabinet Ministers, Ministers of State, or Deputy Ministers**. The bare text refers uniformly to the Prime Minister and "other Ministers" (the sole exception being `Article 352(3)`, inserted in 1978, which mentions *"Ministers of Cabinet rank"* purely for National Emergency advice). Instead, India's three-tier ministerial hierarchy evolved through **British parliamentary convention** and is statutorily recognised in the **Salaries and Allowances of Ministers Act, 1952**.

| Rank / Category | Constitutional / Statutory Status | Portfolio & Administrative Role | Cabinet Meeting Attendance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cabinet Ministers** | Convention + `Art. 352(3)` (1978) + 1952 Act | Head major, strategic Union Ministries (Home, Defence, Finance, External Affairs, Railways, Education, etc.); top policy-makers. | **Attend Cabinet meetings as of right**; constitute the Union Cabinet alongside PM. |
| **Ministers of State (Independent Charge)** | Convention + 1952 Act | Hold independent charge of smaller ministries or departments (e.g., Statistics, Law/Science at times) with no Cabinet Minister above them; enjoy Cabinet-equivalent autonomy in their domain. | **NOT** members of Cabinet; attend **only when specially invited** for items concerning their ministry. |
| **Ministers of State (Attached)** | Convention + 1952 Act | Attached to a Cabinet Minister; given charge of a specific department within a multi-department ministry or assigned specific divisions/parliamentary work under the Cabinet Minister's overall supervision. | **Do NOT attend** Cabinet meetings (unless exceptionally invited). |
| **Deputy Ministers** | Convention + 1952 Act | Junior-most tier; **never** given independent charge; attached to Cabinet Ministers or MoS to assist with administrative, political, and parliamentary duties. Rarely appointed in contemporary Union ministries. | **Do NOT attend** Cabinet meetings. |
| **Parliamentary Secretaries** | Extra-tier political aides (appointed by PM, NOT President) | Have **no department** under their control; attached to senior ministers to help with parliamentary duties. **Not appointed at Centre since 1967** (briefly revived in 1984–85 under Rajiv Gandhi). | **Do NOT attend** Cabinet meetings. |
| **Deputy Prime Minister** | **Purely Extra-Constitutional** political title | Appointed for political or coalition balance; enjoys no special constitutional power above Cabinet Ministers (*K.M. Sharma v. Devi Lal*, 1990); typically holds a key portfolio (e.g., Home or Finance). | Attends Cabinet as a senior Cabinet Minister (may chair Cabinet in PM's absence if authorised). |

#### Closer Look at Parliamentary Secretaries and the 15% Cap

Because **Parliamentary Secretaries** are appointed by the Prime Minister (or Chief Ministers in States) rather than the President/Governor, several State governments attempted after the `91st Amendment (2003)` to bypass the 15% ministerial ceiling by appointing dozens of MLAs as "Parliamentary Secretaries" with ministerial rank, perks, and red beacons. Constitutional courts decisively dismantled this subterfuge: in ***Bimolangshu Roy v. State of Assam* (2017)**, the Supreme Court struck down the Assam Parliamentary Secretaries (Appointment, Salaries, Allowances and Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 2004 as unconstitutional for lacking legislative competence and violating the `Article 164(1A)` cap (parallel rulings invalidated similar appointments in Delhi, Punjab, West Bengal, Himachal Pradesh, and Manipur). At the Union level, no Parliamentary Secretary has been appointed since the brief 1984–85 experiment under Rajiv Gandhi.

#### The Extra-Constitutional Office of Deputy Prime Minister

Seven political leaders have held the extra-constitutional post of **Deputy Prime Minister of India**, predominantly during periods of political balancing or coalition compulsion:
1. **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** (1947–1950, under Jawaharlal Nehru);
2. **Morarji Desai** (1967–1969, under Indira Gandhi);
3. **Charan Singh** (January–July 1979, under Morarji Desai — simultaneously with Jagjivan Ram!);
4. **Jagjivan Ram** (January–July 1979, under Morarji Desai — India's only instance of **two simultaneous Deputy Prime Ministers**);
5. **Y.B. Chavan** (1979–1980, under Charan Singh);
6. **Devi Lal** (1989–1990 under V.P. Singh, and 1990–1991 under Chandra Shekhar — the only leader appointed Deputy PM **twice**);
7. **Lal Krishna Advani** (2002–2004, under Atal Bihari Vajpayee).

When Devi Lal's 1989 swearing-in was challenged on the ground that the Third Schedule prescribes no oath for a "Deputy Prime Minister," the Supreme Court in ***K.M. Sharma v. Devi Lal* (9 January 1990)** dismissed the petition. Emphasising that the Third Schedule prescribes only a single oath for a Union Minister (not even a distinct oath for the PM), the Court held that adding the words "Deputy Prime Minister" was **merely descriptive**, did not vitiate the substantial ministerial oath, and **conferred zero constitutional powers of the Prime Minister** upon the holder.

> [!remember]
> **Three Instant Memory Anchors for Prelims:**
> - **Constitution ≠ Ministerial Ranks:** The Constitution does **NOT** classify ministers into 4/3 ranks (UPSC Prelims 2022 trap!).
> - **"Cabinet" Appears Only ONCE:** Inserted solely in **`Article 352(3)`** by the **`44th Amendment Act, 1978`** (requiring written Cabinet advice to declare National Emergency).
> - **91st Amendment Twin Shield (2003):** `Art. 75(1A)` = **≤15% of Lok Sabha strength (including PM)**; `Art. 75(1B)` = **Tenth Schedule defector barred from ministership** (+ `Art. 361B` bars remunerative political posts).

### Council of Ministers vs Union Cabinet and the Kitchen Cabinet Phenomenon

Though used interchangeably in everyday media, the **Council of Ministers** and the **Union Cabinet** are sharply distinct institutions in Indian constitutional law and administrative practice. The Council of Ministers is the **wider constitutional body** (~60 to 80 ministers, capped at 81 under `Art. 75(1A)`), encompassing Cabinet Ministers, Ministers of State (Independent Charge and Attached), and Deputy Ministers. The **Union Cabinet** is the **compact inner policy-making core** (~15 to 30 members), comprising exclusively the **Prime Minister and Cabinet Ministers**.

```compare

```

#### Role of the Union Cabinet in the Indian Political System

Constitutional theorists describe the Cabinet in superlative terms: **Ramsay Muir** called it *"the steering wheel of the ship of the state,"* **Lowell** termed it *"the keystone of the political arch,"* **Sir John Marriott** named it *"the pivot around which the whole political machinery revolves,"* **Sir Ivor Jennings** called it *"the core of the British constitutional system,"* and **L.S. Amery** described it as *"the central directing instrument of government."* In India, the Union Cabinet performs **seven vital governing roles**:
1. **Highest Decision-Making Authority:** Approves all major domestic policies, national budgets, economic reforms, foreign treaties, and legislative bills before introduction in Parliament;
2. **Chief Policy-Formulating Body:** Translates electoral manifestos and constitutional mandates into coherent executive programmes;
3. **Supreme Executive Authority:** Exercises effective superintendence over the entire Central administration;
4. **Chief Coordinator:** Resolves inter-ministerial disputes and harmonises overlapping departmental initiatives;
5. **Advisory Body to the President:** Its advice binds the President (`Art. 74(1)`), and its **written recommendation** is a strict constitutional precondition for proclaiming a National Emergency under `Article 352(3)`;
6. **Chief Crisis Manager:** Directs national responses to external aggression, internal security emergencies, financial shocks, and natural disasters;
7. **Controller of Legislative & Financial Affairs:** Controls parliamentary time, major appointments (through the ACC), foreign relations, and legislative priorities.

#### The "Kitchen Cabinet" (Inner Cabinet) Phenomenon

Within—and sometimes alongside—the formal Union Cabinet often emerges an even smaller, wholly informal, **extra-constitutional** inner circle known as the **"Kitchen Cabinet"** or **"Inner Cabinet."** Typically comprising the Prime Minister plus two to four extraordinarily influential, trusted colleagues—and occasionally close non-ministerial advisors, party strategists, or confidants—this informal body debates sensitive political and strategic options before (or instead of) placing them before the full Cabinet.

Every era of Indian politics has witnessed variations of this phenomenon: Jawaharlal Nehru consulted an informal core (Patel, Azad, Kidwai, later Pant and Krishna Menon); Lal Bahadur Shastri relied on trusted advisers; **Indira Gandhi's "Kitchen Cabinet"** attained iconic (and controversial) fame during the late 1960s and 1970s; Atal Bihari Vajpayee relied on a tight inner core (Advani, Jaswant Singh, George Fernandes, Brajesh Mishra); during UPA I & II, the Congress Core Group and National Advisory Council functioned as influential inner policy circles; and strong Prime Ministers in the contemporary era continue to consult a tight circle of senior ministers (Home, Defence, Finance, External Affairs) and senior PMO advisors on strategic decisions.

- **Merits of a Kitchen Cabinet:** (1) Its compact size enables **swift, agile decision-making** far faster than a 30-member formal Cabinet; (2) It allows **candid, uninhibited deliberation** on politically sensitive or security-critical matters; (3) It safeguards **strict secrecy** where leaks could derail diplomatic negotiations, defence operations, or market-sensitive economic moves (e.g., strategic strikes or major fiscal announcements).
- **Demerits of a Kitchen Cabinet:** (1) It **erodes the status, morale, and collegiality** of the formal Union Cabinet, reducing non-inner Cabinet Ministers to rubber stamps; (2) When it includes **unelected outsiders, bureaucrats, or family confidants**, it creates **power without constitutional responsibility**, subverting the democratic chain of accountability under `Article 75(3)`; (3) Narrow groupthink increases the risk of policy blind spots that wider Cabinet scrutiny would have flagged.

## 3. Cabinet Committees, Groups of Ministers and Collegiate Decision-Making

As the functions of the modern Indian welfare and regulatory state expanded exponentially after Independence, it became physically impossible for a full 25-to-30-member Union Cabinet to examine every complex economic proposal, security acquisition, parliamentary timetable, or senior civil appointment in granular detail during weekly sittings. To preserve collegiate decision-making without succumbing to administrative paralysis, India adapted the British device of **Cabinet Committees**—specialised, compact sub-groups of ministers empowered to examine technical issues in depth and decide swiftly on behalf of the Cabinet.

### Origin, Legal Basis and Structural Features of Cabinet Committees

Every aspirant must lock in six defining constitutional and operational attributes of Cabinet Committees that repeatedly appear in UPSC Prelims statements:

1. **Extra-Constitutional Origin (Rules of Business Basis):** Cabinet Committees are **NOT mentioned anywhere in the Constitution of India**. They are **extra-constitutional** in emergence. Their formal legal sanction derives from **Rule 6 of the Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961**, promulgated by the President under **`Article 77(3)`**.
2. **Two Types — Standing and Ad Hoc:** Cabinet Committees fall into two structural categories:
   - **Standing Cabinet Committees** are permanent institutional panels set up to manage recurring, core domains of governance (political affairs, economic management, national security, top appointments, parliamentary business, accommodation, investment, and skilling).
   - **Ad Hoc Cabinet Committees** are temporary panels constituted from time to time to address specific, time-bound policy challenges or crises (e.g., drafting major reforms or resolving inter-sectoral deadlocks) and are **automatically disbanded** once their mandate is completed.
3. **Constituted and Modified by the Prime Minister:** The **Prime Minister** enjoys plenary discretion to create, reconstitute, merge, rename, or abolish Cabinet Committees according to the administrative and political exigencies of the time. Consequently, their total number, nomenclature, and membership vary across governments: there were **10** standing committees in 1994, **13** in 2013 (under UPA II), pruned to **6** in 2014 (under NDA I by abolishing panels on Prices, UIDAI, WTO Matters, and Management of Natural Calamities), expanded to **8** in **June 2019** (under NDA II), and reconstituted as **8 Standing Cabinet Committees** on **3 July 2024** (under NDA III).
4. **Size and Non-Cabinet Membership:** In classical administrative theory, a Cabinet Committee comprises **3 to 8 members**, though contemporary Indian practice spans a much wider range—from **just 2 members** (Appointments Committee of the Cabinet) to **14 members** (Cabinet Committee on Political Affairs). While they ordinarily consist of senior **Cabinet Ministers**, **non-Cabinet Ministers (Ministers of State with Independent Charge or attached MoS) are NOT constitutionally or procedurally barred** from membership; they regularly serve either as full members or as **Special Invitees** whenever their portfolios intersect with the panel's mandate.
5. **Chairmanship Convention:** Most Standing Cabinet Committees are headed by the **Prime Minister**. Crucially, **whenever the Prime Minister is a member of a Cabinet Committee, the Prime Minister invariably presides over it**. In panels where the Prime Minister does not sit, a senior Cabinet Minister—typically the **Home Minister** or the **Defence Minister**—chairs the committee.
6. **Final Decisions Subject to Full Cabinet Review:** Cabinet Committees are **NOT merely advisory bodies**. They both sort out complex inter-ministerial proposals and **take final, legally binding executive decisions** in the name of the Cabinet. Nevertheless, because collective responsibility belongs to the entire Council of Ministers, **the full Union Cabinet retains overriding authority to review, modify, or overturn** any decision taken by a Cabinet Committee.

#### Deep Dive Into the Four Core Functional Committees and the Two 2019 Additions

1. **Cabinet Committee on Political Affairs (CCPA) — The "Super-Cabinet":** Historically described by political scientists as the **"Super-Cabinet"** because it gathers the heaviest political ministers in government, the CCPA addresses policy questions carrying major political, federal, or electoral consequences—such as sensitive constitutional legislation, state reorganisation issues, major Centre–State political frictions, and overarching foreign-policy stances. In the July 2024 reconstitution, its expansion to **14 members** (including Finance, Home, Defence, Road Transport, Health, Commerce, Parliamentary Affairs, Civil Aviation [TDP], and MSME [HAM]) reflects its role as the primary political consensus-building arena of a coalition Cabinet.
2. **Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA):** Functioning as the nerve centre of India's macroeconomic management, the CCEA directs and coordinates government economic activities on a continuous basis. Its routine yet vital agenda includes approving **Minimum Support Prices (MSPs)** for *kharif* and *rabi* crops, sanctioning centrally sponsored schemes and mega-infrastructure outlays (railways, highways, ports, power, telecom), steering strategic disinvestment of CPSEs, reviewing industrial licensing and foreign investment norms, and monitoring inflation and food security.
3. **Appointments Committee of the Cabinet (ACC):** Arguably the single most powerful administrative committee inside the Indian state, the ACC controls senior personnel governance across the Union. Prior to 2014, the ACC included three members—the Prime Minister, the Home Minister, and the **Minister in charge of the concerned administrative ministry**. In **2014**, the Transaction of Business Rules were amended to streamline the ACC into a strictly **two-member panel (Prime Minister + Home Minister)**, though inputs from the concerned ministry are considered on file. The ACC approves appointments and tenure extensions of the **Cabinet Secretary**, all Union Secretaries, Additional and Joint Secretaries, Service Chiefs (Army, Navy, Air Force) and Chief of Defence Staff (CDS), heads of central intelligence and investigative agencies (R&AW, IB), RBI Governor and Deputy Governors, SEBI/TRAI heads, and Chairpersons/MDs of Maharatna/Navratna CPSEs and public sector banks. Crucially, under the Transaction of Business Rules, **any disagreement between a Union Ministry and the UPSC** regarding recruitment or disciplinary matters must be resolved by the ACC!
4. **Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS):** Bringing together the classic "Big Four" portfolios alongside the Prime Minister—**Defence, Home Affairs, Finance, and External Affairs**—the CCS is India's apex strategic command forum (assisted regularly by the **National Security Advisor** and **Cabinet Secretary**). It approves major capital defence acquisitions (high-value defence contracts), reviews border security and counter-terrorism strategy, oversees the Department of Atomic Energy's strategic aspects, guides foreign policy bearing on national security, and coordinates diplomatic-military crises.
5. **The Two Growth & Livelihood Panels Created in June 2019 (CCIG & CCSEL):** Responding to slowing private investment and demographic employment pressures in 2019, the NDA II government created two new standing committees—the **Cabinet Committee on Investment and Growth (CCIG)** and the **Cabinet Committee on Employment and Skill Development**. On **3 July 2024**, the latter was deliberately broadened and renamed the **Cabinet Committee on Skill, Employment and Livelihood (CCSEL)** to integrate rural/urban livelihood missions, MSME job creation, and workforce transition alongside formal skilling.
6. **The Two Non-PM Chaired Committees (Accommodation & Parliamentary Affairs):** Neither the **Cabinet Committee on Accommodation (CCA)** nor the **Cabinet Committee on Parliamentary Affairs** includes the Prime Minister. Accommodation is chaired by the **Home Minister** (with Road Transport, Finance, Housing & Urban Affairs, and Commerce Ministers, plus MoS PMO as Special Invitee). Parliamentary Affairs—historically chaired by the Home Minister in earlier governments—has been chaired since 2019 (and reaffirmed on 3 July 2024) by the **Defence Minister (Rajnath Singh)**, coordinating legislative business across Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha alongside the Home Minister, Finance Minister, Health Minister (Leader of the House in Rajya Sabha), Parliamentary Affairs Minister, and Law Ministers (as Special Invitees).

### Groups of Ministers (GoMs) and Empowered Groups of Ministers (EGoMs)

Alongside formal Standing Cabinet Committees, Indian governments have extensively used **ad hoc inter-ministerial ministerial groups** to resolve contentious cross-cutting issues that cut across multiple ministries:

- **Groups of Ministers (GoMs):** Ad hoc advisory committees of select ministers constituted by the Prime Minister (or Cabinet) to examine a complex policy subject, reconcile conflicting departmental viewpoints (for example, Environment vs Power or Finance vs Agriculture), and **submit consensus recommendations** to the Union Cabinet for final decision.
- **Empowered Groups of Ministers (EGoMs):** Unlike ordinary GoMs, EGoMs were explicitly **delegated the Cabinet's authority to take final binding decisions** on specified subjects *without* needing to return to the full Cabinet. During the UPA I and UPA II coalition eras (2004–2014), EGoMs proliferated dramatically (governing telecom spectrum auctions, natural gas pricing, drought relief, food security, and Telangana bifurcation) because coalition compromises often required delicate multi-party negotiation outside formal Cabinet votes.
- **The May 2014 Abolition and Functional Reset:** Critics—including the **Second Administrative Reforms Commission (13th Report)**—warned that excessive proliferation of GoMs and EGoMs diluted the authority of line ministries, undermined Cabinet accountability, and created chronic **"policy paralysis"** as ministries routinely punted tough decisions upward to slow-moving ministerial groups. Immediately upon assuming office in **May 2014**, the Narendra Modi government **abolished all 9 existing EGoMs and 21 GoMs**, instructing line ministries and departments to decide issues within their own mandate and escalate unresolved inter-ministerial deadlocks directly to the Cabinet Secretariat, PMO, or the relevant Standing Cabinet Committee. Since 2014, while EGoMs remain discontinued, compact, strictly time-bound ad hoc GoMs continue to be deployed selectively for targeted legislative or inter-ministerial tasks (alongside statutory GoMs functioning within the federal **GST Council**).

> **Mains Analytical Significance of Cabinet Committees:**
> Cabinet Committees are an indispensable institutional device for **division of labour, specialised policy scrutiny, and effective delegation** inside a sprawling parliamentary executive. ## 4. Central Administration: PMO, Cabinet Secretariat, Ministries and Reform

Political leadership by the Prime Minister and Union Council of Ministers can translate into law, budgets, diplomatic strategy, and public service delivery *only* through an elaborate permanent administrative machinery. At the apex of the Government of India sits a **tri-layered institutional architecture**: (1) the **Prime Minister's Office (PMO)**, serving as the Prime Minister's personal strategic staff agency; (2) the **Cabinet Secretariat**, acting as the neutral institutional nerve centre of the entire Cabinet and civil services; and (3) the **Central Secretariat**—comprising over fifty Ministries and roughly ninety Departments—backed by **Attached Offices**, **Subordinate Offices**, and **Autonomous Agencies** executing policy across India's 28 States and 8 Union Territories.

### The Prime Minister's Office (PMO): Evolution, Structure and Role

#### Constitutional and Organisational Status

Like Cabinet Committees, the **Prime Minister's Office (PMO)** is an **extra-constitutional** body—it finds **no mention anywhere in the Constitution of India**. Administratively, however, it was accorded the formal status of a **"Department of the Government of India"** under the **`Government of India (Allocation of Business) Rules, 1961`**. Unlike line ministries, the PMO is a pure **staff agency**—it has **no attached offices and no subordinate offices** under its direct administrative hierarchy. Its singular institutional mandate is to provide secretarial, analytical, strategic, and coordinating assistance directly to the Prime Minister.

#### Historical Evolution: From Modest PMS to Strategic Nerve Centre (1947–2026)

1. **Nehru Era (1947–1964) — Modest Secretarial Support:** Created in **August 1947** as the **Prime Minister's Secretariat (PMS)** to replace the office of the Secretary to the Governor-General (Personal), it functioned under Jawaharlal Nehru as a lean, unobtrusive secretarial desk. Nehru relied predominantly on senior Cabinet Ministers, the Cabinet Secretariat, and the Ministry of External Affairs (which he held personally) rather than building a rival bureaucratic empire inside the PMS.
2. **Shastri & Indira Gandhi Eras (1964–1977) — Emergence of the Policy Powerhouse:** **Lal Bahadur Shastri** appointed senior ICS officer **L.K. Jha** as Secretary to the PM in 1964, expanding the PMS's economic and policy-analysis role. Under **Indira Gandhi**, the PMS attained unprecedented institutional dominance: in **1971**, **P.N. Haksar** became the first officer designated **Principal Secretary to the Prime Minister**, turning the PMS into a formidable "mini-cabinet" driving bank nationalisation, foreign policy (1971 Bangladesh Liberation War), and strategic appointments.
3. **Janata Reset (June 1977) — Renaming to PMO:** Viewing the Indira-era PMS as an unconstitutional parallel power centre that had eclipsed Cabinet collegiality, Prime Minister **Morarji Desai** renamed the agency the **Prime Minister's Office (PMO)** in **June 1977**, trimmed its staff strength, and curtailed its intervention in line ministries.
4. **Post-1980 Resurgence & Modern Strategic Architecture (1980–2026):** Despite Desai's rollback, subsequent governments steadily rebuilt the PMO's coordinating clout: Rajiv Gandhi infused technology advisers and mission teams; Atal Bihari Vajpayee appointed **Brajesh Mishra** in **1998** in the dual role of Principal Secretary and India's first **National Security Advisor (NSA)**; during UPA I & II (2004–2014), the PMO balanced coalition ministries alongside the National Advisory Council; and since 2014, the PMO has operated as a high-velocity, data-driven command centre for flagship missions, infrastructure monitoring (**PRAGATI**), and foreign-policy summits. In **2019**, the post of **Principal Secretary to the PM** was elevated to the rank of a **Union Cabinet Minister**; in **February 2025**, former RBI Governor **Shaktikanta Das** was appointed **Principal Secretary-2 to the PM** alongside **Dr. P.K. Mishra** (serving alongside NSA **Ajit Doval** and MoS PMO **Dr. Jitendra Singh** as of November 2026). On **13 February 2026**, closing nearly eight decades in colonial-era South Block, the PMO formally shifted into its modern Central Vista headquarters at **Seva Teerth** (with North and South Blocks designated for the planned *Yuge Yugeen Bharat National Museum*).

#### Composition and Core Functions of the PMO

Politically, the PMO is headed by the **Prime Minister** (assisted by a **Minister of State in the PMO** who answers Parliament questions on its behalf). Administratively, it is headed by the **Principal Secretary to the Prime Minister** (aided as of 2026 by Principal Secretary-2, the National Security Advisor, Advisors to the PM, Additional Secretaries, Joint Secretaries, Directors, Deputy Secretaries, Officers on Special Duty, and Private Secretaries—drawn predominantly from the IAS, IFS, IPS, IRS, and domain experts).

The PMO discharges **seven core functions**:
- **Executive & Policy Support:** Assists the PM in discharging overall executive responsibility as Head of Government, functioning as the PM's **strategic think-tank** across economic, diplomatic, and security domains;
- **Inter-Ministerial & Federal Liaison:** Maintains continuous contact with Union Ministers, Secretaries, State Chief Ministers, and Governors to align execution and break cross-departmental deadlocks;
- **Processing Rules-of-Business References:** Processes all files and Cabinet notes requiring the Prime Minister's personal approval under the Transaction of Business Rules (including sensitive appointments prior to ACC clearance);
- **Mission & Grievance Monitoring (PRAGATI):** Operates **PRAGATI** (*Pro-Active Governance and Timely Implementation*, launched **March 2015**)—a three-tier ICT and video-conferencing platform integrating the PMO, Union Secretaries, and State Chief Secretaries to personally review stalled mega-projects and citizen grievances registered on CPGRAMS;
- **Unassigned Portfolios:** Handles all administrative matters relating to ministries/departments retained directly by the PM (such as Atomic Energy, Space, and Personnel) or subjects not specifically allocated to any ministry;
- **Public Relations, Parliament & Foreign Engagements:** Manages the PM's official correspondence, speeches, press/media outreach, parliamentary interventions, and high-level bilateral/multilateral diplomacy;
- **Relief Funds:** Administers the **Prime Minister's National Relief Fund (PMNRF)** and provides administrative interface for national relief initiatives.

> [!debate]
> **Mains GS-II Debate: "PMO-isation" of Governance — Over-Centralisation vs Whole-of-Government Agility**
> - **The Critique (Over-Centralisation & Erosion of Collegiality):** Administrative scholars and former Cabinet Secretaries warn that an overly dominant PMO acts as a **"super-cabinet"** or **"parallel government."** When Union Secretaries report directly to PMO Joint Secretaries over the heads of their own Cabinet Ministers, it (a) undermines ministerial initiative and `Article 75(3)` collective responsibility, (b) eclipses the neutral mediating role of the **Cabinet Secretariat**, (c) creates upward decision bottlenecks where routine files stall awaiting PMO clearance, and (d) narrows policy inputs to an unaccountable bureaucratic coterie.
> - **The Defence (Whole-of-Government Coordination):** Proponents contend that 21st-century governance problems—climate transition, semiconductor supply chains, digital public infrastructure, pandemic management, and multi-modal logistics—cut across a dozen siloed ministries that historically resisted coordination. A strong, technically equipped PMO provides **unified strategic direction**, enforces real-time accountability via tools like **PRAGATI**, accelerates clearances that otherwise stagnate in inter-ministerial turf wars, and reflects the electorate's direct mandate to the Prime Minister.
> - **Balanced Way Forward:** Reserve the PMO strictly for **high-level strategic vision, national security, crisis intervention, and cross-sectoral bottleneck resolution**, while restoring routine inter-ministerial coordination to the **Cabinet Secretariat** and operational autonomy to line Ministers and Secretaries.

### The Cabinet Secretariat and the Office of the Cabinet Secretary

If the PMO represents the **personal and political strategic staff** of the Prime Minister, the **Cabinet Secretariat** represents the **permanent, non-partisan institutional memory and administrative nerve centre** of the Union Cabinet and the entire Indian civil service.

#### Origin, Legal Basis and Organisational Structure

The Cabinet Secretariat evolved directly from the colonial **Secretariat of the Executive Council of the Governor-General** (headed originally by a Private Secretary, with Lord Willingdon's reforms and Sir Eric Coates strengthening secretarial coordination). Upon formation of the Interim Government on **5 September 1946**, it was formally redesignated the **Cabinet Secretariat**. Placed directly under the **Prime Minister** (NOT under any other Minister), it derives its legal authority from the **`Government of India (Allocation of Business) Rules, 1961`** framed under `Article 77(3)`. After functioning from Rashtrapati Bhavan on Raisina Hill for nearly eight decades, the Cabinet Secretariat relocated in **September 2025** to **Seva Teerth-2** in the Executive Enclave of the Central Vista.

Administratively, the Cabinet Secretariat is structured into specialised operational verticals:
1. **Civil Wing:** The primary core providing secretarial assistance to the Union Cabinet and all Standing and Ad hoc Cabinet Committees—vetting Cabinet notes for compliance with inter-ministerial consultation rules, circulating agendas, recording official minutes, and monitoring post-Cabinet implementation across ministries.
2. **Military Wing:** Historically responsible for providing secretarial support to the Defence Committee of the Cabinet, Military Affairs Committee, and inter-services coordination (operating today alongside the Chief of Defence Staff, Department of Military Affairs, and National Security Council Secretariat).
3. **Intelligence, Security & Strategic Agencies:** Crucially for Prelims, the Cabinet Secretariat serves as the **parent administrative umbrella** for India's premier external intelligence and elite security agencies—including the **Research and Analysis Wing (R&AW)** (headed by Secretary [R]), the **Special Protection Group (SPG)** (protecting the Prime Minister), the **Special Frontier Force (SFF)**, and the **National Authority for Chemical Weapons Convention (NACWC)**. *(Classic Prelims trap: While the Intelligence Bureau [IB] sits under the Ministry of Home Affairs, **R&AW sits directly under the Cabinet Secretariat**!)*
4. **Coordination, Public Grievances & Delivery Verticals:** Headed by **Secretary (Coordination & PG)**, this wing oversees inter-ministerial consensus-building, coordinates the **Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) Mission** (originally housed in the Planning Commission, shifted to Department of Expenditure in July 2013, and placed directly inside the **Cabinet Secretariat on 14 September 2015** to accelerate JAM-trinity welfare delivery across 50+ central ministries and 36 State/UT DBT cells), oversees legacy performance-management frameworks, and anchors secretarial coordination for **PRAGATI**.

#### The Office of the Cabinet Secretary

The administrative head of the Cabinet Secretariat is the **Cabinet Secretary**—the **senior-most civil servant of the Government of India** (first incumbent: **N.R. Pillai**, 1950; current incumbent as of November 2026: **Dr. T.V. Somanathan**, IAS, appointed 30 August 2024 and granted a one-year service extension by the ACC on **5 August 2026**). Ranking **11th in the Indian Order of Precedence** (bracketed with the Attorney General of India and Lieutenant Governors within their respective UTs), the Cabinet Secretary occupies a pivotal institutional position:

- **Ex-Officio Head of the Civil Services:** Serves as the **ex-officio Chairman of the Civil Services Board (CSB)** (which recommends senior empanelment and postings under the Central Staffing Scheme) and is recognised under the Rules of Business as the administrative head of the Indian Administrative Service (IAS) and all central civil services;
- **Chief Coordinator via Committees of Secretaries (CoS):** Presides over the **Committee of Secretaries (CoS)**—the apex inter-ministerial bureaucratic forum that resolves departmental deadlocks *before* proposals reach the Union Cabinet—and presides over the **Conference of Chief Secretaries**, acting as the primary administrative bridge between the Union and State bureaucracies;
- **Custodian of the Rules of Business:** Administers both the **`Allocation of Business Rules, 1961`** and the **`Transaction of Business Rules, 1961`**, ensuring that ministries follow strict inter-ministerial consultation (especially with Finance and Law Ministries) before placing items before the Cabinet;
- **Apex Crisis Manager (NCMC):** Chairs the **National Crisis Management Committee (NCMC)**, coordinating immediate national civil-military-state administrative responses during mega-disasters (cyclones, earthquakes, pandemics, hijackings, supply-chain disruptions);
- **Institutional Neutrality & Continuity:** Unlike political ministers who change with electoral cycles, the Cabinet Secretary preserves administrative continuity, institutional memory, and strictly non-partisan counsel—as **Gopalaswami Ayyangar** (1949) and the **First and Second ARCs** emphasised, the Cabinet Secretary must function as the "conscience-keeper" and elder counsellor of the entire civil service.

### Central Secretariat, Ministries, Departments and Executive Agencies

#### Constitutional Foundation and the Twin 1961 Rulebooks (`Article 77(3)`)

The vast operational engine of the Union Government is collectively known as the **Central Secretariat**—comprising all **Ministries and Departments** of the Government of India. Ministries and Departments are **NOT fixed by the Constitution**; they are created, bifurcated, merged, or reorganised by the **President on the advice of the Prime Minister** under **`Article 77(3)`**, operationalised through two foundational legal instruments administered by the Cabinet Secretariat:

1. **The Government of India (Allocation of Business) Rules, 1961:** Specifies **what** subjects belong to which Ministry or Department (the *substantive division of work*). From just **18 ministries on 15 August 1947**, the Union structure has expanded—driven by welfare expansion, technical specialisation, and coalition portfolio creation—to **over 50 Ministries and nearly 90 Departments** today (for instance, new ministries like the Ministry of Jal Shakti [2019] and the Ministry of Cooperation [2021] were created by amending these Rules). Importantly, some strategic **Independent Departments**—notably the **Department of Atomic Energy (DAE)** and the **Department of Space (DoS)**—sit **directly under the Prime Minister** without being grouped under any Ministry.
2. **The Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961:** Specifies **how** official business is conducted inside and between ministries (the *procedural manual*)—including which decisions an individual minister can clear independently, which proposals require mandatory prior consultation with the Ministry of Finance or Ministry of Law & Justice, which major matters must go to the full Cabinet or a Standing Cabinet Committee (Rule 6), and which files require prior submission to the Prime Minister and President.

To physically break departmental silos fostered by ministries scattered across dozens of buildings in New Delhi, the Central Vista Redevelopment Project began co-locating ministries into the new **Common Central Secretariat (CCS)**—marked by the inauguration of its first integrated complex, **Kartavya Bhawan**, in **August 2025**.

#### The Structural Hierarchy of a Union Ministry and Department

A **Ministry** is the top political-administrative unit placed under a Cabinet Minister (or MoS with Independent Charge). A large ministry may encompass several **Departments**—each functioning as a self-contained administrative unit headed by a **Secretary to the Government of India**. Inside every Department, the Central Secretariat operates through a classic **six-tier pyramidal hierarchy** (from Secretary down to Section Officer):

| Organisational Unit | Presiding Officer / Designation | Core Administrative & Policy Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Ministry** (Political Apex) | **Cabinet Minister** / MoS (Independent Charge) | Political head; sets strategic policy, defends ministry in Parliament, accountable to Lok Sabha (`Art. 75(3)`). |
| **Department** (Administrative Apex) | **Secretary to the Government of India** | Senior-most administrative head & principal policy advisor to Minister; **Chief Accounting Authority** before PAC; appears before Parliamentary Committees. |
| **Wing** | **Special Secretary / Additional Secretary / Joint Secretary** | Holds independent administrative and policy charge of a major functional Wing; Joint Secretary is often the critical file-disposing authority for routine government orders. |
| **Division** | **Director / Deputy Secretary** | Supervises a Division consisting of 2–3 Branches; scrutinises policy notes, drafts legislation, and clears mid-level proposals. |
| **Branch** | **Under Secretary** | Heads a Branch (typically 2 Sections); first rung of gazetted decision-making authority handling initial disposal of cases and parliamentary questions. |
| **Section** (Base Supervisory Unit) | **Section Officer** (aided by Assistant Section Officers / Assistants) | Lowest operational office unit; maintains institutional records, examines precedents, compiles factual notes, and tracks file movement (now digitised via **e-Office**). |

*(The Split / Tenure System: Unlike countries with a static headquarters bureaucracy, India's Central Secretariat—inheriting Lord Curzon's 1905 reforms—follows the **Tenure System** under the **Central Staffing Scheme [CSS]**. Officers from the All India Services [IAS, IPS, IFoS] and Central Civil Services [IRS, IA&AS, IRTS, etc.] come on fixed-term **central deputation** at Deputy Secretary, Director, and Joint Secretary levels and rotate back to State/field postings. This "split system" injects real-world district experience into Union policy-making while exposing field officers to national macroeconomic perspectives—though critics argue it perpetuates generalist dominance over deep sectoral expertise.)*

### Second ARC (13th Report) and Contemporary Civil Service & Organisational Reforms

Despite its resilience, India's Central Secretariat has long been critiqued for ministerial proliferation, multi-layered file delays, blurring of policy and execution, frequent transfers, and shortage of technical specialists. The most authoritative reform blueprint remains the **Second Administrative Reforms Commission's (Second ARC) 13th Report**, ***Organisational Structure of Government of India*** (**April 2009**, chaired by **M. Veerappa Moily**), building on the First ARC (1966–70).

#### Four Structural Pillars of the Second ARC (13th Report, 2009)

1. **Applying the Principle of Subsidiarity & Right-Sizing to 20–25 Ministries:** The Second ARC emphasised that under **cooperative federalism**, the Union Government should restrict itself strictly to **core national functions**—national defence, foreign affairs, macroeconomic and fiscal management, national infrastructure networks, inter-state coordination, and strategic regulation—while devolving implementation of state/local subjects back to States and PRIs/ULBs. On this basis, it recommended consolidating 50+ fragmented ministries into **20 to 25 cohesive, logically grouped ministries** (for example, merging energy ministries or transport modes), each headed by a senior Coordinating Cabinet Minister assisted by specialised Ministers of State.
2. **Strict Separation of Policy from Execution (Executive Agencies):** Observing that Union Secretaries spend excessive time micro-managing routine delivery rather than strategic policy analysis, the Commission recommended **recasting the Allocation of Business Rules into outcome-oriented mission statements**. Ministries should focus exclusively on **policy formulation, legislative drafting, budgeting, parliamentary oversight, and independent evaluation**, while operational delivery should be hived off to semi-autonomous **Executive Agencies** (modelled on the UK's *Next Steps* agencies) governed by transparent **Performance Agreements / Memoranda of Understanding (MoUs)** specifying measurable outputs and operational autonomy.
3. **Flattening the Secretariat Hierarchy & Modernising Workflows:** To eradicate bureaucratic red tape where a single file routinely climbs six to seven desks (Assistant → Section Officer → Under Secretary → Deputy Secretary → Joint Secretary → Additional Secretary → Secretary → Minister), the Second ARC recommended that **no file should travel through more than three administrative levels** before final decision. It advocated expanding the **Desk-Officer System** (where a single middle-level officer disposes of cases independently with minimal clerical noting), strengthening delegation of financial powers, shifting to **integrated e-Office** digital workflows, and transitioning from input-oriented expenditure tracking to **outcome-based budgeting**.
4. **Domain Specialisation, Fixed Tenures & Competitive Induction:** To overcome the limitations of pure generalist rotation, the Second ARC recommended (a) assigning civil servants to broad **functional domains** (e.g., finance, infrastructure, social sector, internal security) after 14–16 years of service; (b) guaranteeing **fixed minimum tenures** (at least 2 years) for Union Secretaries and senior posts to ensure accountability; (c) establishing a statutory **Central Civil Services Authority** to insulate senior postings from political arbitrariness; and (d) opening senior Secretariat positions (Joint Secretary and above) to **open, transparent competition** combining career civil servants and external domain specialists (**lateral entry**).

#### Contemporary Reforms and Debates: Lateral Entry and Mission Karmayogi (2018–2026)

Over the past decade, the Union Government has partially operationalised the Second ARC's human-resource vision through two landmark initiatives—each revealing both the promise and political complexity of administrative reform:

1. **Lateral Entry into the Central Secretariat (2018–2026):** Acting on the **NITI Aayog Three-Year Action Agenda (2017)** and the **Sectoral Group of Secretaries on Governance (2017)** (echoing Second ARC), the government launched formal **lateral entry** in **2018** to induct domain experts from the private sector, academia, and public enterprises on 3-year contracts (extendable to 5 years) at **Joint Secretary, Director, and Deputy Secretary** levels via UPSC selection. Between 2018 and 2023, **63 specialists** were recruited across critical technical domains (fintech, aviation, shipping, agriculture, cybersecurity, climate finance), of whom roughly two-thirds remained in service as of June 2026.
   - **The August 2024 Withdrawal & Reservation Debate:** In **August 2024**, the Union Government directed UPSC to **withdraw a major advertisement for 45 lateral entry posts** following intense nationwide debate over the absence of **SC, ST, and OBC reservation** (since individual specialised posts had been treated as "single-cadre standalone posts" exempt from roster quotas). Addressing a DoPT briefing in **June 2026**, MoS Personnel Dr. Jitendra Singh clarified that while **no order suspends lateral entry**, no fresh recruitment had occurred since 2023 because a legally sustainable model for applying reservation to standalone specialised posts had not yet been finalised.
   - **Balanced Mains Assessment:** Lateral entry injects **cutting-edge sectoral expertise**, introduces **healthy performance competition**, and bridges the mid-level IAS central-deputation deficit. Yet it demands strict institutional safeguards against **regulatory capture and private-sector conflicts of interest**, clear alignment with constitutional **social justice (`Article 16(4)`)** through grouped cadre-pooling or reservation rosters, and transparent cooling-off rules post-tenure.
2. **Mission Karmayogi — National Programme for Civil Services Capacity Building (NPCSCB):** Launched in **September 2020**, Mission Karmayogi represents India's most ambitious shift from **"rule-based"** to **"role-based"** civil service capacity building—replacing episodic, supply-driven classroom training with continuous, competency-mapped lifelong learning under the **Framework of Roles, Activities and Competencies (FRAC)**. Its four-tier institutional backbone comprises the **Prime Minister's Public Human Resources Council (PMHRC)** (apex strategic body), the independent **Capacity Building Commission (CBC)**, the non-profit **Karmayogi Bharat** Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV), and the **iGOT Karmayogi** digital learning platform—which by **June 2026** had scaled to **over 1.6 crore registered civil servants**, **5,000+ courses**, and **nearly 13 crore course completions** integrated with AI-assisted competency mapping.

## 1. The Prime Minister: Appointment, Tenure, Powers and Constitutional Role

- **Real (*de facto*) Executive (`Arts. 74, 75, 78`):** President = *de jure* Head of State; **PM** = *de facto* Head of Government. **Ambedkar** (4 Nov 1948) compared India's **PM**—not President—to the US President.
- **Appointment (`Art. 75(1)`):** **NO constitutional selection procedure**; President appoints PM, and other ministers on PM's advice. Convention: leader of **Lok Sabha majority** party/pre-poll coalition.
- **Situational Discretion:** Only in (a) **hung Lok Sabha**—first used **1979** (Neelam Sanjiva Reddy → **Charan Singh**, resigned before facing House); later **1989** (V.P. Singh), **1990** (Chandra Shekhar), **1991** (Narasimha Rao), **1996** (Vajpayee [13 days], Deve Gowda), **1997** (I.K. Gujral), **1998** (K.R. Narayanan mandated **written support letters** + 10-day floor test for Vajpayee); or (b) **sudden PM death** without elected successor (1964/1966 Gulzarilal Nanda acting vs 1984 Rajiv Gandhi sworn directly).
- **Appointment Case Law:** **Delhi HC (1980, *Dinesh Chandra Pande*):** no pre-appointment proof of majority required; appoint first, floor test within reasonable time. ***Har Sharan Verma* (1971)** & ***S.P. Anand v. Deve Gowda* (1996/1997):** **non-MP** may be appointed PM/Minister for **6 months** (`Art. 75(5)`). ***S.R. Chaudhuri* (2001):** bars repeated 6-month reappointment without election ("fraud on the Constitution"). ***B.R. Kapur* (2001, 5-Judge):** statutorily disqualified person (RPA s. 8) barred even for 6 months.
- **Either House vs UK:** India's PM may sit in **LS OR RS** (**Indira Gandhi** 1966, **Deve Gowda** 1996, **I.K. Gujral** 1997–98, **Manmohan Singh** 2004/2009 sat in RS); UK requires House of Commons strictly. Min age: **25** (LS) / **30** (RS). RS PM **cannot vote** on LS No-Confidence Motion (`Art. 88`).
- **Oath, Term & Salary:** President administers **Oaths of Office & Secrecy** (**Third Schedule Forms I–II**; **NO separate PM oath**; *"sovereignty and integrity"* via `16th Amend, 1963`). Term **not fixed**; President's pleasure (`Art. 75(2)`) = LS confidence; no term limits. Salary fixed by Parliament (`Art. 75(6)` — MP salary + sumptuary allowance + 7, Lok Kalyan Marg); **NOT** in Second Schedule, **NOT** charged on CFI.
- **Powers, `Art. 78` & Ex-Officio Chairs:** Recommends/reshuffles/dismisses ministers; **PM's death/resignation dissolves ENTIRE CoM**. **`Art. 78`** three duties: `(a)` communicate CoM decisions & legislative proposals; `(b)` furnish info President seeks; `(c)` place individual minister's decision before full CoM if President requires. Chairs **NITI Aayog**, NDC, **ISC (`Art. 263`)**, NIC, NWRC, **NDMA**, CSIR, **NCA Political Council**; retains **DAE, DoS & Personnel** by convention.
- **Scholars & 6 CM-to-PMs:** **Morley** (*primus inter pares*, "keystone of Cabinet arch"); **Harcourt** (*inter stellas luna minores*); **Jennings** ("sun around which planets revolve"); **Laski** ("pivot"; formation/life/death of Cabinet); **Ramsay Muir** ("steersman of steering wheel"); **Munro** ("captain of ship"). **6 former CMs:** Morarji Desai (Bombay), Charan Singh (UP), V.P. Singh (UP), P.V. Narasimha Rao (AP), **H.D. Deve Gowda** (Karnataka — direct), **Narendra Modi** (Gujarat — direct).

## 2. Union Council of Ministers: Constitutional Architecture, Responsibility and Composition

- **`Article 74`:** `74(1)` Mandatory CoM headed by PM; advice made binding by **`42nd Amend (1976)`**; one-time reconsideration via **`44th Amend (1978)`**. ***U.N.R. Rao* (1971, 5-Judge):** caretaker CoM continues even after LS dissolution. `74(2)` Courts barred from probing *advice tendered*, **but CAN** examine underlying *material* (***S.R. Bommai*, 1994**).
- **`Article 75` & `91st Amendment (2003)`:** **`Art. 75(1A)`** caps CoM (**including PM**) at **≤15% of Lok Sabha strength** (max **81** of 543 — LS ONLY, UPSC 2022). **`Art. 75(1B)`** bars Tenth Schedule defectors from ministership until term ends or re-elected (earlier); **`Art. 361B`** bars remunerative political posts.
- **`Art. 77` & `Art. 88`:** `Art. 77` — actions in President's name; authenticated orders immune from personal-execution challenge; Rules of Business (`Art. 77(3)`, *Shamsher Singh*, 1974). **`Art. 88`** — Ministers & AG may speak/participate in **either House**, joint sittings & committees named to, but **vote ONLY in their own House** (non-MP votes in neither).
- **Three Principles of Responsibility:**
  1. **Collective (`Art. 75(3)`):** Specifically to **Lok Sabha ONLY** (NOT Parliament/RS — UPSC 2013!). "Swim or sink together" (RS ministers also resign on LS No-Confidence); Cabinet solidarity (resignations: Ambedkar 1951, C.D. Deshmukh 1956, Arif Mohd. Khan 1986, Harsimrat Kaur Badal 2020). ***Common Cause* (1999):** Public Trust Doctrine. ***Kaushal Kishor* (3 Jan 2023, 5-Judge 4–1):** flows from CoM to minister, NOT vice versa; stray ministerial remarks outside House create **no automatic vicarious government tort** (Justice Nagarathna dissented).
  2. **Individual (`Art. 75(2)`):** President's pleasure (on PM's advice) allows targeted dismissal.
  3. **No Legal Responsibility:** No UK countersignature; `Art. 74(2)` bars probing advice (orders still reviewable via Union of India, `Art. 300`).
- **130th Amendment Bill, 2025:** Introduced **20 Aug 2025** (`Arts. 75, 164, 239AA`); mandates removal/resignation of PM/CM/Minister detained **30 consecutive days** for **5+ yr** offences (automatic cessation day 31; reappointment allowed on release). Pending before **JPC (Aparajita Sarangi)** as of Nov 2026.
- **Ranks, CoM vs Cabinet & Kitchen Cabinet:** Constitution does **NOT** classify ministerial ranks (UPSC 2022); convention + 1952 Act define **Cabinet Ministers**, **MoS (Independent Charge / Attached)**, **Deputy Ministers**. **Parliamentary Secretaries** (appointed by PM; unused at Centre since 1967 except 1984–85; State quota evasion struck down in *Bimolangshu Roy*, 2017). **Deputy PM** extra-constitutional (7 holders: Patel, Morarji, Charan Singh & Jagjivan Ram [simultaneous 1979], Y.B. Chavan, Devi Lal [twice], L.K. Advani; *K.M. Sharma*, 1990 — descriptive only). Word **"Cabinet"** appears **ONLY ONCE** in Constitution — **`Art. 352(3)` (`44th Amend, 1978`)**. **Kitchen Cabinet** = informal inner circle (speed/secrecy vs collegiality erosion).

## 3. Cabinet Committees, Groups of Ministers and Collegiate Decision-Making

- **Status & Rules:** **Extra-constitutional** — **Rule 6, Transaction of Business Rules, 1961** (`Art. 77(3)`). **Standing** vs **Ad hoc**; created/reconstituted by **PM**. Classical size 3–8 (actual 2–14); **non-Cabinet Ministers (MoS) allowed** as members/Special Invitees. When PM sits, **PM invariably chairs**. Decisions are **final**, subject to **full Cabinet review**.
- **All 8 Standing Cabinet Committees (Reconstituted 3 July 2024; active Nov 2026):**
  - **PM Chairs 6:** (1) **Political Affairs [CCPA]** ("Super-Cabinet", 14 members); (2) **Economic Affairs [CCEA]** (11 members — MSPs, investments, disinvestment); (3) **Appointments [ACC]** (**2 members ONLY since 2014: PM + Home Minister**; top posts & Ministry–UPSC disputes); (4) **Security [CCS]** (5 members: PM + Defence, Home, Finance, EAM); (5) **Investment & Growth [CCIG]** (2019; 12 + 2 invitees); (6) **Skill, Employment & Livelihood [CCSEL]** (2019 *Employment & Skill Development*, **renamed 3 July 2024**; 11 + 1 invitee).
  - **2 Non-PM Exceptions (PM NOT a member):** (7) **Accommodation** — **Home Minister** (Amit Shah, 5 + 1 invitee); (8) **Parliamentary Affairs** — **Defence Minister** (Rajnath Singh, 10 + 2 invitees; session dates & legislative business).
  - **Note:** **Home Minister Amit Shah** sits on **all 8** panels; NDA allies inducted across major panels in July 2024.
- **GoMs & EGoMs:** GoMs advise; EGoMs took binding decisions. **May 2014** abolished all **9 EGoMs & 21 GoMs** to empower line ministries and end policy paralysis.

## 4. Central Administration: PMO, Cabinet Secretariat, Ministries and Reform

- **PMO:** **Extra-constitutional** staff agency; **Department** status under `Allocation of Business Rules, 1961` (**NO attached/subordinate offices**). 1947 **PMS** → **P.N. Haksar** first Principal Secretary (1971) → renamed **PMO (June 1977)** by Morarji Desai → PS given **Cabinet Minister rank (2019)** → **Dr. P.K. Mishra** (PS), **Shaktikanta Das** (PS-2, Feb 2025), NSA **Ajit Doval** → shifted South Block → **Seva Teerth (13 Feb 2026)**. Think-tank, **PRAGATI** (March 2015), CPGRAMS, PMNRF, unassigned portfolios. Mains tension: **"PMO-isation"** vs whole-of-government coordination.
- **Cabinet Secretariat:** Renamed **5 Sept 1946**; directly under PM (`Allocation & Transaction Rules, 1961`, `Art. 77(3)`); shifted Rashtrapati Bhavan → **Seva Teerth-2 (Sept 2025)**. Headed by **Cabinet Secretary** (senior-most civil servant; first **N.R. Pillai** 1950; **Dr. T.V. Somanathan** since Aug 2024, extended 1 yr on **5 Aug 2026**; **11th** in Precedence; ex-officio Chair of **Civil Services Board**, **Committee of Secretaries [CoS]**, and **NCMC**). Wings: Civil, Military, Intelligence/Security (**R&AW, SPG, SFF, NACWC** — IB is under MHA!), Coordination & PG (**DBT Mission** shifted **14 Sept 2015**).
- **Central Secretariat, Ministries & Offices:** Created by President on PM's advice under **Allocation of Business Rules, 1961** (*what*) & **Transaction of Business Rules, 1961** (*how*), both administered by **Cabinet Secretariat**. Grew from 18 ministries (1947) to **50+ ministries / ~90 departments** (plus independent DAE/DoS under PM; **Kartavya Bhawan** CCS opened Aug 2025). Hierarchy: **Minister → Secretary (Dept) → Special/Addl/Joint Secy (Wing) → Director/Deputy Secy (Division) → Under Secy (Branch) → Section Officer (Section)**. **Secretariat** (policy staff) vs **Attached Offices** (executive direction/technical advice: CPWD, DGCA, DGFT, CWC, DGHS, ED) vs **Subordinate Offices** (field execution: Passport Offices, IT/Customs Commissionerates).
- **Second ARC (13th Report, April 2009):** (1) Subsidiarity → **20–25 cohesive ministries**; (2) Separate policy from execution via MoU-bound **Executive Agencies**; (3) Max **3 file levels** + Desk-Officer System + e-Office (+ **PM Gati Shakti**); (4) Empower Cabinet Secretariat, fixed 2-yr Secretary tenures, domain specialisation, Central Civil Services Authority & **lateral entry**.
- **Lateral Entry & Mission Karmayogi (2018–2026):** **Lateral entry** (2018; 63 specialists at JS/Director/DS); **Aug 2024** UPSC withdrew 45-post ad over lack of SC/ST/OBC reservation in standalone posts (paused awaiting reservation model as of June 2026). **Mission Karmayogi** (Sept 2020; rule-based → role-based; PMHRC, CBC, Karmayogi Bharat; **iGOT Karmayogi** crossed **1.6 crore users & ~13 crore completions** by June 2026).
