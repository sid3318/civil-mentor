## 1. High Courts: Structure, Judges and Independence

Unlike the United States—where federal courts and state courts operate as two parallel, watertight systems enforcing federal and state laws separately—the Constitution of India constructs a **single, integrated judicial system**. At the apex sits the Supreme Court of India (`Part V`), immediately below it stand the **High Courts** at the head of each State's judicial administration (`Part VI, Chapter V, Articles 214 to 231`), and at the base lies the sprawling network of **District and Subordinate Courts** (`Part VI, Chapter VI, Articles 233 to 237`). Both Union laws and State laws are interpreted and enforced through this unified pyramid, supplemented since 1976 by specialised **Tribunals** (`Part XIV-A, Articles 323A–323B`) and a statutory ecosystem of **Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR)** mechanisms anchored in `Article 39A`.

Within this constitutional design, the High Court is far more than a regional court of appeal. It is a **constitutional court** of first resort for citizens' rights, the custodian of the rule of law across the State, the institutional supervisor of every subordinate court and tribunal within its territorial jurisdiction, and the primary feeder pool from which Supreme Court judges are elevated. Because the vast majority of constitutional, administrative, commercial, and criminal litigation ends at the High Court level—only a fraction ever reaches New Delhi—the independence, capacity, and integrity of India's 25 High Courts determine the everyday health of Indian constitutionalism.

### Historical Evolution and Territorial Architecture

The institution of the High Court predates the Republic of India by nearly nine decades and constitutes one of the oldest continuous institutional pillars of modern Indian governance. Before 1861, colonial justice in British India suffered from an awkward institutional bifurcation: the Crown's **Supreme Courts** established at Calcutta (1774), Madras (1800), and Bombay (1823) administered English common law inside the Presidency towns, while the East India Company's **Sadar Diwani Adalats** (civil) and **Sadar Nizamat Adalats** (criminal) administered personal and customary laws across the *mofussil* (interior districts). Friction between these rival hierarchies routinely paralysed administration.

To fuse both streams into unified superior courts of record, the British Parliament enacted the **Indian High Courts Act, 1861**. Acting under this statute, Queen Victoria issued Letters Patent establishing India's first three High Courts in **1862**—at **Calcutta** (1 July 1862, the oldest High Court in India), **Bombay** (14 August 1862), and **Madras** (15 August 1862). Four years later, a fourth High Court was established for the North-Western Provinces at Agra in **1866** and shifted to **Allahabad** in 1869. Over the ensuing decades, every British Indian province gradually acquired its own High Court—including Patna (1916), Lahore (1919), Nagpur (1936), Orissa and Gauhati (1948). Under the **Government of India Act, 1935**, these provincial High Courts were woven into a single judicial architecture beneath the newly established Federal Court of India (1937). When the Constitution commenced on **26 January 1950**, `Article 214` declared that *"there shall be a High Court for each State,"* seamlessly converting existing provincial High Courts into constitutional High Courts of the corresponding States (`Article 225`).

#### Common High Courts and Union Territory Jurisdictions (Articles 230, 231 and 241)

Although `Article 214` sets out the default rule of one High Court for each State, India's federal reorganisation quickly demonstrated the inefficiency of mandating separate High Courts for tiny States and island territories. The **`7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956`** therefore substituted **`Article 231`**, empowering **Parliament by law** (by simple majority, NOT an `Article 368` amendment) to establish a **common High Court** for two or more States, or for two or more States and a Union Territory. Simultaneously, **`Article 230`** empowers Parliament by law to **extend the jurisdiction** of a High Court to, or **exclude the jurisdiction** of a High Court from, any Union Territory—while explicitly clarifying (`Article 230(2)`) that where a State High Court exercises jurisdiction over a UT, the State Legislature cannot increase, restrict, or abolish that jurisdiction. Complementing both provisions, **`Article 241`** empowers Parliament to constitute a separate High Court for a Union Territory (the constitutional route used for the **Delhi High Court Act, 1966**).

As of **November 2026**, India has **25 High Courts** serving 28 States and 8 Union Territories. The latest expansion occurred on **1 January 2019**, when the common High Court of Judicature at Hyderabad was bifurcated into the **High Court of Andhra Pradesh** (principal seat at Amaravati) and the **High Court for the State of Telangana** (at Hyderabad), raising the total from 24 to 25. Earlier, in March 2013, the creation of separate High Courts for **Manipur (Imphal)**, **Meghalaya (Shillong)**, and **Tripura (Agartala)** had trimmed the jurisdiction of the Gauhati High Court from seven north-eastern States down to four.

A perennial UPSC Prelims favourite concerns which High Courts exercise **multi-State or State-plus-UT jurisdiction** and which Union Territories possess **their own High Courts**. Out of India's 25 High Courts, **seven High Courts** exercise multi-unit jurisdiction, while **only two Union Territories—NCT of Delhi (1966) and the common High Court of Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh (reconstituted in October 2019)**—have their own High Courts (with Chandigarh, Puducherry, Andaman & Nicobar Islands, Lakshadweep, and Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu attached to neighbouring State High Courts).

Notice two subtle constitutional details regarding territorial seats and benches. First, **Parliament** (not the State Legislature) possesses exclusive legislative power under `Entry 78 of List I` (Union List) to regulate the constitution and organisation of High Courts, including establishing permanent benches (typically on the recommendation of the High Court Chief Justice, the State Government, and the CJI, guided by the **Jaswant Singh Commission, 1985** criteria). Second, the territorial jurisdiction of a High Court is ordinarily co-terminus with the territory of the State(s) or UT(s) it serves, subject to Parliament's power under `Articles 230` and `231`.

### Composition, Strength and Appointment of Judges

#### Composition and Numerical Strength (Article 216)

Under **`Article 216`**, *"Every High Court shall consist of a Chief Justice and such other Judges as the President may from time to time deem it necessary to appoint."* This single sentence conceals one of the most heavily tested UPSC Prelims traps in Indian Polity. Whereas the sanctioned numerical strength of the **Supreme Court** is prescribed by **Parliament by ordinary law** under `Article 124(1)` (raised from 34 to **38** in August 2026), the Constitution neither fixes the numerical strength of a High Court nor vests that power in Parliament or the State Legislature. Instead, it leaves the strength of every High Court entirely to the **discretion of the President** (acting on Union executive advice in consultation with the CJI and State stakeholders), allowing flexible periodic upward revision as institutional workload grows.

As of **1 November 2026** (Department of Justice official statement), India's 25 High Courts collectively carry a **sanctioned strength of 1,122 judges**—comprising **847 permanent judges** and **275 additional judges**. Against this sanctioned complement, only **827 judges** are actually working (697 permanent and 130 additional), leaving **295 vacancies (26.3%)**. Across the country, High Court sizes mirror demographic and docket realities: the **Allahabad High Court** is the largest with **160 sanctioned posts** (104 working, 56 vacant), followed by **Bombay** (94), **Punjab & Haryana** (85), **Madras** (75), **Calcutta** (72), **Karnataka** (62), and **Delhi** (60); at the smallest end stand **Sikkim** (3 sanctioned, 2 working), **Meghalaya** (4 sanctioned, 4 working—the sole High Court with zero vacancies as of November 2026), **Manipur** (5), and **Tripura** (5).

#### Appointment Process and the High Court Collegium (Article 217(1))

Under **`Article 217(1)`**, every Judge of a High Court is appointed by the **President by warrant under his hand and seal** after consultation with:
- the **Chief Justice of India (CJI)**;
- the **Governor of the State** (and in the case of a common High Court for two or more States under `Article 231`, the Governors of all the concerned States); and
- in the case of appointment of a Judge **other than the Chief Justice**, the **Chief Justice of the High Court** concerned.

As with the Supreme Court, the word *"consultation"* in `Article 217(1)` underwent a transformative judicial reinterpretation across the **Judges Cases**:
1. **First Judges Case (*S.P. Gupta v. Union of India*, 1981):** A seven-judge bench held that "consultation" does not mean "concurrence," giving the Union Executive decisive primacy in appointing and transferring High Court judges.
2. **Second Judges Case (*Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India*, 1993):** A nine-judge bench overruled *S.P. Gupta*, holding that the CJI's opinion—formed collectively after consulting senior colleagues—enjoys **primacy** and binds the President unless cogent objections are returned, and that reiteration by the judiciary is binding.
3. **Third Judges Case (*Special Reference No. 1 of 1998*):** A nine-judge advisory bench codified the exact architecture of the **Collegium System** and drew a crucial numerical distinction between Supreme Court appointments and High Court appointments:
   - **High Court Collegium (Initiation):** Proposals originate at the State level with the **Chief Justice of the High Court** consulting the **two senior-most puisne judges** of that High Court (a three-member HC Collegium). The names pass through the **Chief Minister** and the **Governor**, who forward their inputs to the Union Ministry of Law and Justice (which obtains Intelligence Bureau background checks).
   - **Supreme Court Collegium for High Courts (Final Judicial Vetting):** Unlike Supreme Court appointments and High Court transfers—which require **CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges**—the Supreme Court Collegium recommending **High Court Judge appointments** consists of the **CJI + 2 senior-most Supreme Court judges**! In addition, this three-member SC Collegium must consult in writing the **Supreme Court judge(s) conversant with the affairs of that particular High Court** (typically judges elevated from that parent HC). The sole opinion of the CJI without consulting these colleagues carries zero constitutional validity.
4. **Fourth Judges Case (*SCAORA v. Union of India*, 2015):** A five-judge Constitution Bench (4:1 majority) struck down the **`99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014`** and the **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act, 2014**, restoring the Collegium for both Supreme Court and High Court appointments while directing revision of the **Memorandum of Procedure (MoP)**—a revision that remains deadlocked between the Centre and the judiciary even in 2026.

By long-standing institutional policy—endorsed by the Law Commission and adopted since the 1980s to curb parochialism and bar-bench familiarity—the **Chief Justice of a High Court is ordinarily appointed from outside that State** (elevating or transferring a senior puisne judge from another High Court), and roughly one-third of all High Court judges are encouraged to be drawn from outside the State, though the one-third quota has been unevenly applied in practice.

```flow
# Constitutional Workflow: High Court Judge Appointment (Art. 217) vs Transfer (Art. 222)
Appointment Initiation | HC CJ + 2 senior-most HC Judges initiate names || Transfer Initiation | Initiated exclusively by CJI in public interest / better administration of justice
Executive & IB Vetting | Chief Minister & Governor forward views → Union Law Ministry adds IB report || Dual-HC Consultation | CJI obtains views of CJs of BOTH transferring & receiving High Courts
Appointment Collegium | **CJI + 2 senior-most SC Judges** (+ SC judges conversant with that HC) || Transfer Collegium | **CJI + 4 senior-most SC Judges** (5-member expanded Collegium!)
Presidential Warrant | President issues warrant (`Art. 217` / `Art. 222`); Collegium reiteration binds Union Executive (though "pocket delays" remain a major 2020s friction point)
```

### Qualifications, Oath, Tenure, Removal and Transfer

#### Qualifications (Article 217(2))

To be appointed as a Judge of a High Court, a person must satisfy two cumulative requirements under **`Article 217(2)`**:
1. They must be a **citizen of India**; AND
2. They must possess **either** of the following professional qualifications:
   - **(a) Judicial Office Route:** Have held a **judicial office** in the territory of India for at least **ten years** (including judicial offices in subordinate courts and, under `Explanation (aa)` inserted by the `42nd Amendment, 1976`, any post held after becoming an advocate that requires special knowledge of law—such as a member of a tribunal or judicial authority); **OR**
   - **(b) Bar Route:** Have been an **advocate of a High Court** (or of two or more such Courts in succession) for at least **ten years**.

Two omissions in `Article 217(2)` are absolute Prelims goldmines:
- **NO minimum age is prescribed** by the Constitution for appointment as a High Court Judge (just as no minimum age exists for a Supreme Court Judge—by convention, however, the Collegium ordinarily considers advocates aged 45 and above).
- **NO "distinguished jurist" category exists** for High Court Judges! Whereas `Article 124(3)(c)` allows the President to appoint a "distinguished jurist" directly to the Supreme Court, the Constitution deliberately omits this route for High Courts. In the 1976 `42nd Amendment`, Parliament did briefly insert a "distinguished jurist" clause into `Article 217(2)(c)`, but the **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`** swiftly deleted it!

#### Oath or Affirmation (Article 219)

Before entering upon office, every person appointed as a Judge of a High Court must make and subscribe an oath or affirmation according to the form prescribed in the **Third Schedule** before the **Governor of the State**, or some person appointed in that behalf by the Governor (`Article 219`). In that oath, the Judge swears to:
- bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution of India;
- uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India (added by the `16th Amendment Act, 1963`);
- duly and faithfully and to the best of their ability, knowledge, and judgment perform the duties of office without fear or favour, affection or ill-will; and
- uphold the Constitution and the laws.

Note the critical asymmetry that trips up candidates every year: **the Governor administers the oath (`Article 219`), yet the Judge is appointed by the President (`Article 217`), submits their resignation ONLY to the President (`Article 217(1)(a)`), and can be removed ONLY by the President (`Article 217(1)(b)`)!**

#### Tenure, Retirement Age and Age Disputes (Article 217(1) & (3))

The Constitution does not fix a fixed-year term for High Court Judges; instead, it guarantees security of tenure through four concrete rules under `Article 217`:
- **Retirement Age of 62 Years:** Originally fixed at 60 years in 1950, the retirement age of a High Court Judge was raised to **62 years** by the **`15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963`**. (The *Constitution (114th Amendment) Bill, 2010*, which proposed harmonising HC retirement age with the Supreme Court's 65 years to curb attrition and post-retirement tribunal hankering, lapsed with the 15th Lok Sabha; despite recurring recommendations from the Venkatachaliah Commission and Parliamentary Standing Committees, the 62-year cap remains unchanged in 2026.)
- **Resolution of Age Disputes (`Article 217(3)`):** Also inserted by the `15th Amendment, 1963` (following the controversial *J.P. Mitter* dispute in the Calcutta High Court), `Article 217(3)` specifies that if any question arises as to the age of a High Court Judge, it shall be decided by the **President after consultation with the Chief Justice of India**, and **the decision of the President shall be final**. Contrast this sharply with the Supreme Court (`Article 124(2A)`), where age disputes are determined by such authority and in such manner as **Parliament may by law provide**!
- **Resignation:** A Judge may resign at any time by writing under their hand addressed to the **President** (NOT the Governor, NOT the HC Chief Justice).
- **Vacancy on Elevation or Transfer:** The office is automatically vacated if the Judge is appointed by the President to be a Judge of the Supreme Court or is transferred by the President to any other High Court (`Article 217(1)(c)`).

#### Removal of a High Court Judge (Article 217(1)(b) read with Article 124(4))

A High Court Judge can be removed from office by an order of the **President** on the **exact same grounds and through the exact same parliamentary procedure** prescribed for removing a Supreme Court Judge under `Article 124(4)` and regulated by the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968**. Crucially, **neither the State Legislature nor the State Governor plays any role whatsoever** in removing a High Court Judge—insulating High Court judges completely from provincial political vendettas.

The removal process proceeds across five rigorous constitutional and statutory stages:
1. **Only Two Constitutional Grounds:** A judge may be removed strictly on the ground of **(i) proved misbehaviour** or **(ii) incapacity**. Notably, the Constitution does not use the word "impeachment" for judges (reserving that term strictly for the President under `Article 61`), though public discourse casually calls it judicial impeachment.
2. **Initiation of Removal Motion:** A motion signed by at least **100 members of the Lok Sabha** (if introduced in the Lower House) OR at least **50 members of the Rajya Sabha** (if introduced in the Upper House) is submitted to the **Speaker** or **Chairman**, who has statutory discretion either to **admit** or to **refuse to admit** the motion.
3. **Three-Member Statutory Inquiry Committee:** If admitted, the Speaker or Chairman constitutes a three-member inquiry committee comprising: **(a)** the Chief Justice or a Judge of the **Supreme Court**, **(b)** a **Chief Justice of a High Court**, and **(c)** a **distinguished jurist**. The committee frames charges, affords full hearing to the judge, and submits a factual report. If the committee absolves the judge, the process terminates immediately.
4. **Special Majority in Both Houses in the Same Session:** If the committee finds the judge guilty of misbehaviour or suffering from incapacity, the House takes up the motion. To succeed, an address must be passed by **each House of Parliament** by a **special majority**—that is, **(i) a majority of the total membership of that House** AND **(ii) a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting**—and both Houses must pass it in the **same parliamentary session**.
5. **Presidential Removal Order:** Only after receiving this joint address does the **President** issue the formal order removing the judge.

To date, **no High Court (or Supreme Court) judge has ever completed the full removal process to a Presidential order**. Two landmark High Court proceedings illustrate its formidable threshold: in **2011**, the Rajya Sabha passed a removal motion against **Justice Soumitra Sen** of the Calcutta High Court (for financial misappropriation prior to elevation), prompting him to resign days before the Lok Sabha vote; earlier the same year, **Justice P.D. Dinakaran** (Chief Justice of the Sikkim High Court) resigned after a Rajya Sabha inquiry committee framed charges against him.

#### Transfer of High Court Judges (Article 222)

Under **`Article 222(1)`**, the **President** may, **after consultation with the Chief Justice of India**, transfer a Judge from one High Court to any other High Court. Upon transfer, the judge is entitled to receive a compensatory allowance in addition to regular salary, as determined by Parliament (`Article 222(2)`).

Because arbitrary executive transfers were infamously weaponised during the **Emergency (1975–77)**—when 16 High Court judges who had ruled against the government were transferred across the country without their consent—the Supreme Court erected strict constitutional guardrails across three landmark cases:
- ***Union of India v. Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth* (1977):** Held that while prior consent of the judge is not constitutionally mandatory, transfer under `Article 222` can be effected **only in public interest** and never as a **punishment**, and requires "full and effective consultation" with the CJI.
- ***Second Judges Case* (1993):** Held that judicial review of a transfer is strictly narrow—necessary only to check **lack of proper consultation**—and **only the transferred judge** (not third-party PIL litigants) has *locus standi* to challenge it.
- ***Third Judges Case* (1998):** Raised the collegium threshold for transfers above ordinary HC appointments! Before recommending a transfer, the **CJI must consult the four senior-most judges of the Supreme Court** (`CJI + 4`) **PLUS the Chief Justice of the High Court from which the judge is being transferred AND the Chief Justice of the High Court to which the judge is proposed to be transferred**.

> [!debate]
> **Transfers Under Article 222: National Integration Tool or Opaque Sanction?**
> - **Constitutional Rationale:** Transfers promote national judicial integration, dismantle entrenched local Bar-Bench cliques, supply experienced judges to smaller High Courts, and underpin the policy of appointing Chief Justices from outside the State.
> - **Institutional Critique:** Even under the Collegium era, transfers executed "for better administration of justice" without publishing reasons have repeatedly sparked Bar protests—critics argue that transferring an independent judge who delivered inconvenient rulings against the executive, or transferring a tainted judge instead of disciplining them, converts `Article 222` into either a **chilling punitive weapon** or a **"passing-the-parcel" cover-up** that merely shifts an integrity problem to another State.

### Acting, Additional and Retired Judges (Articles 223, 224 and 224A)

To prevent judicial bottlenecks caused by temporary absences, surging dockets, or chronic vacancies, the Constitution establishes **four temporary judicial mechanisms** for High Courts—each with distinct appointing authorities and statutory limits frequently juxtaposed against Supreme Court equivalents (`Articles 126–128`) in UPSC Prelims:

| Constitutional Office & Article | Appointing / Requesting Authority | Prerequisite Consent / Consultation | Duration, Conditions & Constitutional Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acting Chief Justice** (`Art. 223`) | **President** appoints any Judge of that High Court | None constitutionally specified (convention: senior-most judge of that HC) | Triggered when HC Chief Justice office is vacant, or CJ is absent or unable to perform duties. |
| **Additional Judge** (`Art. 224(1)`) | **President** appoints duly qualified person | Collegium consultation (same as `Art. 217`) | Triggered by **temporary increase in business** or **arrears of work**; fixed period **not exceeding 2 years**; cannot hold office beyond **age 62**. *(In practice, most Bar appointees enter as Additional Judges for 2 years before permanent confirmation.)* |
| **Acting Judge** (`Art. 224(2)`) | **President** appoints duly qualified person | Collegium consultation | Triggered when a permanent judge (other than CJ) is temporarily absent, unable to act, or appointed Acting CJ; holds office until the permanent judge resumes duties; cannot serve past **age 62**. |
| **Retired Judge** (`Art. 224A`, *15th Amend, 1963*) | **Chief Justice of the High Court** requests a retired judge of that or any other HC | **Previous consent of the President** AND **consent of the retired judge** | Receives allowances fixed by the **President**; enjoys all jurisdiction, powers, and privileges of an HC Judge, **BUT is NOT deemed to be a Judge of that High Court**. *(Contrast: NO age-62 cap!)* |

> [!news]
> **Article 224A Ad-Hoc Judges Guidelines Relaxed (*Lok Prahari*, 30 January 2025)**
> Dormant for nearly six decades, **`Article 224A`** was first operationalised by the Supreme Court in ***Lok Prahari v. Union of India* (April 2021)** to combat crippling High Court backlogs—subject to strict triggers, including a threshold requiring vacancies to exceed **20%** of sanctioned strength (or 10% of cases pending >5 years) and barring ad-hoc judges from replacing regular appointments.
>
> Observing that High Court pendency had crossed 60 lakh (reaching **65.58 lakh by November 2026**), including nearly 20 lakh criminal appeals, a three-judge Special Bench led by **CJI Sanjiv Khanna** (with Justices **B.R. Gavai** and **Surya Kant**) relaxed those curbs on **30 January 2025**. The Bench kept the **>20% vacancy floor in abeyance**, authorising every High Court to recommend **2 to 5 retired judges** as ad-hoc judges—capped at **10% of sanctioned strength**—to sit exclusively on **Division Benches presided over by a sitting High Court judge** to clear pending **criminal appeals**.

> [!remember]
> **Collegium Sizes & Temporary Judges: Zero-Confusion Memory Anchor**
> - **Collegium Sizes (`Third Judges Case`, 1998):**
>   - **CJI + 2 senior-most SC Judges** = **High Court Judge Appointments** (`Art. 217`).
>   - **CJI + 4 senior-most SC Judges** = **Supreme Court Appointments** (`Art. 124`) AND **High Court Judge Transfers** (`Art. 222`, plus CJs of both HCs!).
> - **Who Appoints vs Who Requests Temporary Judges:**
>   - **President appoints:** Acting CJ (`Art. 223`), Additional Judge (`Art. 224(1)`), Acting Judge (`Art. 224(2)`).
>   - **HC Chief Justice requests (with President's prior consent):** Retired Judge (`Art. 224A`) — exact State mirror of CJI requesting retired SC judges under `Art. 128`!

### Independence of High Courts: Ten Constitutional Safeguards

Because High Courts routinely adjudicate disputes between citizens and State Governments—striking down State statutes, quashing arbitrary detentions, and policing police excesses—the framers embedded **ten interlocking constitutional guarantees** to insulate High Court judges from executive intimidation and legislative pressure:

1. **Insulated Mode of Appointment (`Article 217`):** Judges are appointed by the President after binding Collegium consultation with the judiciary, curbing unilateral political patronage by either the Union or State Executive.
2. **Security of Tenure (`Article 217(1)(b)`):** Judges do not hold office during the pleasure of the President or Governor. They serve until age **62** and can be removed **only** through the near-impossible special-majority parliamentary process under `Article 124(4)` on proved misbehaviour or incapacity.
3. **Fixed and Non-Diminishable Service Conditions (`Article 221`):** Salaries, allowances, leave, and pension rights are fixed by **Parliament by law** (under the *High Court Judges (Salaries and Conditions of Service) Act, 1954*, last revised effective 2016 by the 2018 Amendment Act: **₹2,50,000/month** for a High Court Chief Justice—equal to a Supreme Court puisne judge—and **₹2,25,000/month** for a High Court Judge, plus official residence, car, medical, and staff facilities). Crucially, these privileges **cannot be varied to a judge's disadvantage after appointment**, except during a proclaimed **Financial Emergency (`Article 360(4)(b)`)**.
4. **Expenses Charged on Consolidated Funds—The Classic Salary vs Pension Split:** To shield High Courts from annual legislative arm-twisting, salaries, allowances, and administrative expenses of a High Court (including staff salaries under `Article 229(3)`) are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State (`Article 202(3)(d)`)**—meaning the State Legislature may discuss them but **cannot vote** on them! **HOWEVER, the PENSIONS of High Court Judges are charged on the Consolidated Fund of INDIA (`Article 112(3)(d)(iii)`)**, NOT the Consolidated Fund of the State! Why did the framers engineer this subtle split? Because High Court judges are frequently **transferred across multiple States** under `Article 222` during their careers; charging pensions to the Consolidated Fund of India guarantees uniform, uninterrupted retirement security without forcing a retired judge to chase pro-rata pension shares from three or four separate State treasuries.
5. **Conduct Cannot Be Discussed in Legislatures (`Articles 121 and 211`):** Neither Parliament (`Article 121`) nor any State Legislature (`Article 211`) may discuss the judicial conduct of a High Court Judge in the discharge of duties—except in Parliament when considering a formal substantive motion for removal.
6. **Qualified Ban on Post-Retirement Practice (`Article 220`):** Originally banning retired permanent High Court judges from pleading before *any* court in India, `Article 220` was relaxed by the **`7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956`**. Today, a person who has held office as a **permanent Judge** of a High Court is barred from pleading or acting in any court or before any authority in India **EXCEPT the Supreme Court and the OTHER High Courts**. Thus, a retired permanent Judge of the Bombay High Court cannot appear before the Bombay High Court or any subordinate court or tribunal in India, yet may practise freely before the Supreme Court and the Delhi or Madras High Courts. *(Crucial subtlety: this bar applies to **permanent judges**; an Additional Judge who retired without being made permanent is not barred by `Art. 220`, though Bar ethics restrict appearances.)*
7. **Power to Punish for Its Own Contempt (`Article 215`):** As a Court of Record, a High Court possesses inherent constitutional power to punish civil or criminal contempt against itself or subordinate courts, safeguarding its authority and public confidence.
8. **Freedom to Appoint Its Own Staff (`Article 229`):** The Chief Justice of the High Court appoints officers and servants of the High Court and prescribes their service conditions without executive interference (though rules involving salaries, allowances, leave, or pensions require the **Governor's approval** since they burden the State Consolidated Fund).
9. **Jurisdiction Cannot Be Curtailed:** Neither Parliament nor a State Legislature can strip away the constitutional jurisdiction conferred on High Courts by the Constitution itself (particularly `Articles 226` and `227`, entrenched as Basic Structure in *L. Chandra Kumar*, 1997), though legislatures may expand ordinary statutory jurisdiction.
10. **Separation of Judiciary from Executive (`Article 50`) and Exclusive Subordinate Control (`Article 235`):** High Courts operate completely outside the State bureaucracy and exercise exclusive administrative control over the entire district judiciary.

> [!prelims]
> **Five Classic UPSC Prelims Traps on High Courts (Verified Against PYQs)**
> 1. **Strength Fixing (`Art. 216` vs `Art. 124`):** **President** fixes High Court judge strength; **Parliament** fixes Supreme Court judge strength (38 in 2026). Neither requires an `Article 368` amendment!
> 2. **Qualifications (`Art. 217(2)`):** Citizen + 10 yrs judicial office OR 10 yrs HC advocate. **NO "distinguished jurist" route** (deleted by 44th Amend!) and **NO minimum age**!
> 3. **Governor vs President Split:** **Governor** administers the **oath (`Art. 219`)** and is consulted on appointment (`Art. 217`), **BUT** the Judge **resigns to the President**, age disputes are settled **finally by the President (`Art. 217(3)`)**, and removal is solely by **Parliament + President**—the **Governor and State Legislature have ZERO role in removal**!
> 4. **The Salary–Pension Funding Trap:** High Court Judge **salaries & administrative costs** → charged on **Consolidated Fund of the STATE (`Art. 202(3)(d)`)**; High Court Judge **PENSIONS** → charged on **Consolidated Fund of INDIA (`Art. 112(3)(d)(iii)`)**!
> 5. **Post-Retirement Practice Bar (`Art. 220` vs `Art. 124(7)`):** Retired SC judges are barred from pleading in **ALL** courts/authorities in India; retired **permanent HC judges** may practise ONLY in the **Supreme Court and OTHER High Courts**!

## 2. High Court Jurisdiction and Comparison with the Supreme Court

At present, a High Court exercises a remarkably sweeping suite of **seven distinct jurisdictions and powers**: Original Jurisdiction, Writ Jurisdiction (`Article 226`), Appellate Jurisdiction, Supervisory Jurisdiction (`Article 227`), Control over Subordinate Courts (`Article 235`), Court of Record (`Article 215`), and Power of Judicial Review. Crucially, the Constitution does not spell out every detail of a High Court's civil and criminal powers in a single exhaustive schedule; instead, **`Article 225`** preserves all pre-Constitution jurisdiction and powers exercised by existing High Courts immediately before 26 January 1950 (subject to the Constitution and appropriate legislation), while **explicitly abolishing the pre-1950 colonial proviso** that had barred High Courts from exercising original jurisdiction in **revenue matters**.

### Original Jurisdiction of High Courts

**Original jurisdiction** denotes the power of a High Court to hear and decide disputes **in the first instance**, directly, rather than on appeal from a lower court. A High Court's original jurisdiction spans seven distinct domains:
1. **Admiralty, Will, Marriage, Divorce, Company Law and Contempt of Court:** High Courts exercise statutory and inherent original jurisdiction over maritime/admiralty claims (e.g., under the *Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017*), probate of wills, matrimonial/divorce petitions where statutorily vested, specified company matters (partially shifted to NCLT since 2016), and contempt proceedings.
2. **Election Disputes of MPs and MLAs:** Under **Section 80A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951** (inserted in 1966 on the Election Commission's recommendation to replace Election Tribunals), the **High Court** is the **sole court of original jurisdiction** to try election petitions challenging elections to **Parliament** (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha) and **State Legislatures** (Assemblies and Councils) within the State, with a statutory appeal lying directly to the Supreme Court. *(Contrast: disputes relating to the election of the **President** and **Vice-President** lie exclusively with the **Supreme Court** under `Article 71`!)*
3. **Revenue Matters:** Following `Article 225`'s deletion of the colonial GoI Act 1935 bar, High Courts exercise original jurisdiction over revenue assessments and acts ordered or done in revenue collection.
4. **Enforcement of Fundamental Rights (`Article 226`):** Citizens may directly approach a High Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights (and ordinary legal rights) without first exhausting ordinary civil court remedies where efficacious alternative remedies do not exist.
5. **Cases Transferred Involving Constitutional Interpretation (`Article 228`):** Where a High Court is satisfied that a case pending in a subordinate court involves a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution**, it **must withdraw** the case to itself and either dispose of the whole case or determine the constitutional question and return the case to the subordinate court for judgment in conformity with its ruling.
6. **Original Civil Jurisdiction of Four Presidency/Chartered & Delhi High Courts:** The High Courts of **Calcutta, Bombay, Madras** (inheriting Letters Patent Presidency powers), and **Delhi** (conferred by the *Delhi High Court Act, 1966*, plus **Himachal Pradesh** and **J&K** in specified monetary brackets) possess **ordinary original civil jurisdiction** to try civil suits of higher pecuniary value directly (for instance, above ₹2 crore in Delhi High Court and above ₹10 crore in Bombay High Court for specified suits following statutory revisions).
7. **Abolition of Original Criminal Jurisdiction:** Before 1973, the three Presidency High Courts of Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras also possessed original criminal jurisdiction for serious crimes committed within Presidency town limits. The **Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973** abolished this relic uniformly across India (a position retained under the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita [BNSS], 2023**), vesting original criminal trial jurisdiction cleanly in Sessions Courts and Magistrates.

> [!example]
> **Original Jurisdiction in Action: Election Petitions & Commercial Suits**
> When the validity of a sitting Member of Parliament's election—such as Indira Gandhi's 1971 Rae Bareli election tried by **Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha** of the **Allahabad High Court** in *State of Uttar Pradesh v. Raj Narain* (12 June 1975)—is challenged for corrupt electoral practices, the petition is filed **originally** before the High Court under Section 80A of the RPA, 1951, never before a District Court or the Supreme Court. Likewise, high-stakes intellectual property or commercial disputes arising in Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata, or New Delhi are routinely filed directly on the **Original Side** of those High Courts rather than in District Courts.

### Writ Jurisdiction (Article 226) and Comparison with Article 32

**`Article 226`** is the jewel in the crown of High Court authority. It empowers every High Court to issue to any person or authority—including, in appropriate cases, any Government—directions, orders, or writs, including writs in the nature of ***Habeas Corpus*** ("to have the body of" — checking unlawful detention), ***Mandamus*** ("we command" — compelling public duty), ***Prohibition*** (forbidding lower court/tribunal from exceeding jurisdiction), ***Certiorari*** ("to be certified" — quashing illegal/ultra vires quasi-judicial orders), and ***Quo Warranto*** ("by what authority" — testing usurpation of public office).

Four constitutional features define the unique power—and subtle boundaries—of `Article 226`:
- **Substantively WIDER Than Article 32:** While the Supreme Court under `Article 32` can issue writs **ONLY** for the enforcement of **Fundamental Rights (Part III)**, a High Court under `Article 226` can issue writs for **(a)** the enforcement of any of the **Fundamental Rights** AND **(b) "for any other purpose"**—meaning the enforcement of any **ordinary statutory, constitutional (non-FR), or common-law legal right** (such as `Article 300A` right to property, service benefits, contractual arbitrariness, or municipal licensing disputes).
- **Discretionary vs Guaranteed Remedy:** Because `Article 32` is itself a **Fundamental Right** inside Part III, the Supreme Court cannot refuse to entertain a genuine Fundamental Rights writ petition merely because an alternative remedy exists. Conversely, `Article 226` sits in **Part VI** and is **NOT itself a Fundamental Right**; exercising `Article 226` is **discretionary**, and a High Court may decline intervention if an equally efficacious statutory alternative remedy exists, if the petitioner approaches with "unclean hands" or inordinate delay (*laches*), or if disputed questions of fact require trial evidence.
- **Territorial Reach & the Cause-of-Action Expansion (`Article 226(2)`):** Originally, a High Court could issue writs only against persons or authorities located **within its territorial jurisdiction** (creating the absurdity that Central Ministries headquartered in New Delhi could only be sued in the Punjab/Delhi High Court). The **`15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963`** inserted **`Article 226(2)`**, empowering a High Court to issue writs even against a Government, authority, or person **located OUTSIDE its territory** if the **cause of action arises, wholly or in part**, within the High Court's territorial jurisdiction!
- **Interim-Stay Discipline (`Article 226(3)`) & Non-Derogation (`Article 226(4)`):** Inserted by the **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`**, `Article 226(3)` provides that where an *ex parte* interim order (stay/injunction) is passed against a party without hearing them and that party applies to vacate it, the High Court **must dispose of the vacation application within two weeks (14 days)**—failing which the interim order stands **automatically vacated**! And `Article 226(4)` confirms that High Court writ power is **concurrent** with, and never in derogation of, the Supreme Court's `Article 32` power.

```compare
# Writ Jurisdiction: Supreme Court (Article 32) vs High Court (Article 226)
Supreme Court (Article 32 — Part III) | High Court (Article 226 — Part VI)
```

> [!case]
> ***L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997) — 7-Judge Constitution Bench**
> - **Constitutional Question:** Could Parliament, exercising powers under `Articles 323A(2)(d)` and `323B(3)(d)` (added by the `42nd Amendment, 1976`), permanently exclude the writ jurisdiction of High Courts (`Article 226`) and supervisory jurisdiction (`Article 227`) over tribunals?
> - **Ratio Decidendi:** Unanimously held that the power of **judicial review** vested in the **High Courts under `Articles 226 and 227`** and in the **Supreme Court under `Article 32`** is an **inviolable part of the Basic Structure** of the Constitution and can never be ousted even by a constitutional amendment!
> - **Operational Impact:** High Courts remain the mandatory **constitutional sentinel** sitting directly above every statutory tribunal; challenges to tribunal orders must lie first before a **Division Bench of the jurisdictional High Court** before reaching the Supreme Court.

### Appellate, Supervisory, Administrative and Record Powers

#### Appellate Jurisdiction (Civil and Criminal)

The bulk of a High Court's daily docket consists of appeals against judgments of District, Sessions, and Subordinate Courts:
- **Civil Appeals:**
  - *First Appeals* lie on both **questions of fact and questions of law** from decrees and orders of District Judges, Additional District Judges, and subordinate courts where the pecuniary value exceeds specified statutory limits.
  - *Second Appeals* lie from appellate decrees of District Courts strictly on a **substantial question of law** (Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908—not mere errors of fact).
  - *Intra-Court Appeals (Letters Patent Appeals):* In High Courts possessing Letters Patent / statutory intra-court appeal jurisdiction (such as Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, Delhi, Allahabad), an appeal lies from a **Single Judge** of the High Court to a **Division Bench** (two judges) of the same High Court.
  - *Tribunal Appeals:* Appeals/writ challenges from Administrative Tribunals and various specialised statutory tribunals lie to a Division Bench of the High Court.
- **Criminal Appeals and Mandatory Death-Sentence Confirmation:**
  - Appeals lie to the High Court against judgments of a **Sessions Judge** or **Additional Sessions Judge** where a sentence of imprisonment for **more than seven years** has been passed, as well as against convictions by specified courts under the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023** (replacing the CrPC, 1973).
  - **Mandatory Death-Sentence Confirmation (Classic Prelims Fact!):** A **death sentence (capital punishment)** awarded by a Sessions Court **CANNOT be executed unless it is confirmed by the High Court** (`Section 407, BNSS 2023`, formerly `Section 366 CrPC`)—**even if the convicted prisoner chooses NOT to file an appeal**! Every death reference must be heard and signed by a Division Bench of at least **two High Court judges**.
  - Appeals against **acquittals** by subordinate courts also lie to the High Court (by the State Government or by victims/complainants with High Court leave).

#### Supervisory Jurisdiction (Article 227)

Under **`Article 227(1)`**, every High Court exercises the **power of superintendence over ALL courts and tribunals** throughout the territories in relation to which it exercises jurisdiction—subject to **one explicit constitutional exception**: **`Article 227(4)` expressly EXCLUDES any court or tribunal constituted by or under any law relating to the Armed Forces** (such as Courts-Martial and the Armed Forces Tribunal, though even military tribunals remain subject to narrow `Article 226` writ review for jurisdictional illegality or Fundamental Rights violations per *Union of India v. Parashotam Dass*, 2023!).

Understanding the five distinctive features of `Article 227` separates top-scoring aspirants from average ones:
1. **Both Judicial AND Administrative Superintendence:** Unlike `Article 226` (which is purely judicial), `Article 227` confers **both** judicial superintendence (correcting jurisdictional errors, patent perversity, or grave miscarriage of justice by lower courts/tribunals) **and** administrative superintendence.
2. **Concrete Administrative Powers (`Article 227(2)`):** A High Court may **(a)** call for returns from subordinate courts, **(b)** make and issue general rules and prescribe forms for regulating practice and proceedings, **(c)** prescribe forms in which books, entries, and accounts shall be kept by court officers, and **(d)** settle tables of fees allowed to sheriffs, clerks, advocates, and attorneys (subject to **previous approval of the Governor**, and consistent with existing law under `Article 227(3)`).
3. **Exercisable Suo Motu:** Unlike appellate or ordinary writ proceedings that require a litigant's petition, the High Court can invoke `Article 227` ***suo motu*** (on its own motion) to pull up a malfunctioning subordinate court or tribunal.
4. **Extraordinary Corrective—Not Appellate—Power:** As emphasised by the Supreme Court in *Surya Dev Rai* (2003) and *Shalini Shyam Shetty* (2010), `Article 227` is not a substitute for regular appeals; it is exercised sparingly to keep subordinate courts and tribunals strictly within the bounds of their legal authority and to correct flagrant violations of natural justice.
5. **Distinction from Article 226 Certiorari:** Under `Article 226`, a High Court may only **quash** an unlawful order (*certiorari*) and remit the case; under `Article 227`, the High Court may not only set aside the perverse order but also **substitute its own appropriate direction** or guide the subordinate forum on how to proceed. Moreover, `Article 226` reaches *administrative* authorities as well as courts, whereas `Article 227` reaches **only courts and tribunals** (not purely executive officers).

#### Control Over Subordinate Courts (Article 235) and Withdrawal of Cases (Article 228)

Beyond `Article 227`'s general superintendence, **`Article 235`** vests the entire **administrative and disciplinary control** over district courts and courts subordinate thereto exclusively in the **High Court**—covering postings, promotions, transfers, grant of leave, confirmation, suspension, and disciplinary recommendations (including dismissal/removal recommendations, which bind the Governor). Simultaneously, **`Article 228`** safeguards uniformity of constitutional interpretation across the State by requiring the High Court to withdraw any subordinate-court case involving a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution**.

#### Court of Record (Article 215)

Mirroring `Article 129` for the Supreme Court, **`Article 215`** declares every High Court to be a **Court of Record**, conferring **two classical legal powers** plus one crucial implied power:
1. **Perpetual Memory and Evidentiary Value:** Its judgments, proceedings, and acts are permanently recorded and admitted as authoritative legal evidence across all subordinate courts within the State; their authenticity cannot be questioned when produced before any lower court, and its legal precedents bind all subordinate courts and tribunals inside that State.
2. **Power to Punish for Contempt of Court:** It possesses inherent constitutional power to punish for **civil contempt** (wilful disobedience of court judgments, writs, or undertakings) and **criminal contempt** (scandalising or lowering the authority of the court, prejudicing judicial proceedings, or obstructing the administration of justice), regulated procedurally by the **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** (simple imprisonment up to six months, fine up to ₹2,000, or both—subject to statutory defences of **truth in public interest** inserted by the **2006 Amendment**, innocent publication, and fair/accurate reporting).
3. **Power to Review Its Own Judgment:** Unlike the Supreme Court—which holds an explicit constitutional review clause under `Article 137`—Part VI contains **no express Article** granting High Courts review power over their own writ orders. Nevertheless, in ***Shivdeo Singh v. State of Punjab* (1963)**, the Supreme Court held that as a plenary **Court of Record (`Article 215`)**, every High Court possesses inherent power to **review and correct its own judgments** to prevent miscarriage of justice!

#### Power of Judicial Review (Articles 13 and 226)

Every High Court wields robust **judicial review** to examine the constitutionality of **BOTH Central laws and State laws**, as well as executive orders of both Union and State Governments, striking them down as *ultra vires* on three classical grounds: **(a)** violation of **Fundamental Rights (Part III)**, **(b)** lack of **legislative competence** under the Seventh Schedule, or **(c)** contravention of any other constitutional provision (or manifest arbitrariness).

Here lies an essential historical amendment trap frequently tested in UPSC Prelims: during the Emergency, the **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`** deliberately crippled High Court judicial review by inserting **`Article 226A`** (barring High Courts from considering the constitutional validity of **any Central law**, reserving that solely to the Supreme Court) and imposing a two-thirds bench-majority requirement (`Article 228A`) for invalidating State laws. Immediately after the Emergency ended, the Janata Government enacted the **`43rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1977`**, which **repealed `Articles 226A` and `228A`** and **fully restored High Courts' power to test and strike down Central laws as well as State laws**!

> [!prelims]
> **High Court Jurisdiction Traps — Do Not Fall For These!**
> 1. **`42nd` vs `43rd` Amendment Judicial Review Trap:** Can a State High Court strike down a **Central Act passed by Parliament**? **YES!** The `42nd Amendment (1976)` stripped that power (`Art. 226A`), but the **`43rd Amendment (1977)`** restored it in full (confirming *Kusum Ingots* & uniform constitutional review).
> 2. **Armed Forces Exclusion (`Art. 227(4)`):** High Court superintendence under **`Art. 227`** covers **all courts and tribunals EXCEPT Armed Forces courts/tribunals** (identical to the military exclusion from Supreme Court SLP under `Art. 136(2)`!).
> 3. **Death Sentence Confirmation:** Every Sessions Court death sentence **must be confirmed by the High Court** even if the convict does NOT appeal (`Sec. 407 BNSS, 2023`).
> 4. **Review Power Without an Express Article:** Unlike SC (`Art. 137`), HCs have no separate Review Article—their review power flows inherently from **Court of Record (`Art. 215`)** (*Shivdeo Singh*, 1963).
> 5. **No Advisory Jurisdiction:** Unlike the Supreme Court (`Article 143`), **High Courts have NO constitutional advisory jurisdiction** to answer references from the Governor!

### Exhaustive Comparison: Supreme Court vs High Court

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar famously underscored the structural unity—and yet deliberate institutional differentiation—between India's Supreme Court and High Courts when introducing the Draft Constitution:

> "The Indian Federation though a Dual Polity has no Dual Judiciary at all. The High Courts and the Supreme Court form one single integrated Judiciary having jurisdiction and providing remedies in all cases arising under the constitutional law, the civil law or the criminal law." — B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 4 November 1948

Crucially, however, within this single pyramid, **a High Court is NOT an administrative subordinate of the Supreme Court**! As the Supreme Court itself reaffirmed in ***Tirupati Balaji Developers v. State of Bihar* (2004)** and ***S.P. Gupta* (1981)**, High Courts are independent constitutional courts—the Supreme Court is **appellate superior** (its law binds all courts under `Article 141`), **NOT** an administrative boss exercising `Article 227`-style superintendence over High Courts. ## 3. Subordinate Courts and Judicial Reforms

While constitutional textbooks spend pages dissecting the Supreme Court and High Courts, the everyday reality of Indian justice unfolds in the **district and taluka courtrooms**. Over **88% of all pending cases in India** are filed, argued, and decided in the **subordinate (district) judiciary**—the primary interface where ordinary citizens seek bail, enforce property rights, contest matrimonial disputes, or obtain redress for crime. Recognising that an independent apex court cannot deliver rule of law if trial courts remain under executive thumbs, the framers inserted a dedicated constitutional shield—**`Part VI, Chapter VI (Articles 233 to 237)`**—to insulate the district judiciary from State executive interference and anchor it directly beneath the High Court.

### Constitutional Framework for Subordinate Courts (Articles 233 to 237)

Six compact constitutional provisions govern the appointment, qualifications, promotion, and administrative discipline of subordinate judicial officers across every Indian State:

1. **Appointment of District Judges (`Article 233`):**
   - **`Article 233(1)`:** Appointments of persons to be, and the posting and promotion of, **District Judges** in any State shall be made by the **Governor of the State in consultation with the High Court** exercising jurisdiction in relation to such State. In the landmark judgment of ***Chandra Mohan v. State of Uttar Pradesh* (1966)**, the Supreme Court declared that consultation with the High Court is **mandatory and substantive**—not an empty ritual—and struck down Uttar Pradesh rules allowing an executive-dominated selection committee to bypass the High Court. (To protect thousands of past judgments from collapse after *Chandra Mohan*, Parliament enacted the **`20th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1966`**, inserting **`Article 233A`** solely to validate irregular pre-1966 District Judge appointments!)
   - **`Article 233(2)` (Direct Recruitment from the Bar and In-Service Officers):** A person not already in the service of the Union or of the State shall only be eligible to be appointed a District Judge if they have been for **not less than seven years an advocate or a pleader** and are **recommended by the High Court** for appointment. *(Crucial 2025 update: see the five-judge Constitution Bench ruling in ***Rejanish K.V. v. K. Deepa* (9 October 2025)** below, which significantly reinterpreted eligibility for in-service judicial officers!)*
2. **Recruitment of Other Judicial Officers (`Article 234`):** Appointments of persons **other than District Judges** to the judicial service of a State (such as Civil Judges and Judicial Magistrates) shall be made by the **Governor of the State** in accordance with rules made by the Governor **after consultation with the State Public Service Commission (SPSC) and with the High Court** exercising jurisdiction in relation to such State. Note the subtle institutional contrast: the **SPSC** is consulted for **entry-level subordinate judges (`Article 234`)**, **NEVER** for **District Judges (`Article 233`)**!
3. **Exclusive High Court Control Over Subordinate Courts (`Article 235`):** The control over district courts and courts subordinate thereto—including the **posting and promotion of, and the grant of leave to**, persons belonging to the judicial service of a State and holding any post inferior to the post of District Judge—**vests exclusively in the High Court**. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held (notably in *State of West Bengal v. Nripendra Nath Bagchi*, 1966, and *Shamsher Singh*, 1974) that "control" under `Article 235` is comprehensive: it encompasses disciplinary inquiries, suspensions, transfers, confidential reports, compulsory retirement, and recommendations for dismissal or removal (which the Governor is constitutionally bound to accept). `Article 235` is thus the operational heart of **`Article 50`** (Directive Principle mandating separation of the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State).
4. **Broad Constitutional Definition of District Judge and Judicial Service (`Article 236`):**
   - **"District Judge"** includes a Judge of a City Civil Court, Additional District Judge, Joint District Judge, Assistant District Judge, Chief Judge of a Small Cause Court, Chief Presidency Magistrate, Additional Chief Presidency Magistrate, Sessions Judge, Additional Sessions Judge, and Assistant Sessions Judge.
   - **"Judicial Service"** means a service consisting **exclusively** of persons intended to fill the post of District Judge and other civil judicial posts inferior to the post of District Judge.
5. **Extension to Specified Magistrates (`Article 237`):** The Governor may, by public notification, direct that the foregoing provisions of Chapter VI (and rules made thereunder) shall apply in relation to any class or classes of **magistrates** in the State as they apply to persons appointed to the judicial service of the State—the mechanism States used post-1950 to transfer criminal magistracy from District Collectors (Executive Magistrates) to Judicial Magistrates under High Court control.

> [!news]
> **Two Landmark 2025 Supreme Court Verdicts Reshaping Subordinate Judicial Recruitment**
> 1. ***Rejanish K.V. v. K. Deepa* (9 October 2025, 5-Judge Constitution Bench led by CJI B.R. Gavai, `2025 INSC 1208`):** Unanimously overruling *Dheeraj Mor v. High Court of Delhi* (2020), the Constitution Bench held under **`Article 233`** that serving judicial officers who possess **7 years' combined experience** as an advocate and/or as a judicial officer (assessed continuously as on the **date of application**, subject to a **35-year minimum age** floor for both Bar and in-service candidates, operating prospectively) are eligible to compete for **direct recruitment** as District/Additional District Judges! The Court rejected the contention that the 25% direct recruitment quota under `Article 233(2)` is an exclusive Bar monopoly.
> 2. ***All India Judges Association v. Union of India* (20 May 2025, 3-Judge Bench led by CJI B.R. Gavai):** Reversing its 2002 modification (which had allowed fresh law graduates to enter directly), the Supreme Court restored a mandatory **minimum 3 years of active legal practice at the Bar** (reckoned from provisional Bar Council enrolment, with law clerkship to judges counting toward experience) to apply for **Civil Judge (Junior Division)** examinations prospectively. Simultaneously, to reward meritorious young judicial officers, the Bench restored the **Limited Departmental Competitive Examination (LDCE)** promotion quota into the Higher Judicial Service (District Judge cadre) from 10% back to **25%** and introduced a **10% LDCE fast-track quota** for promotion to Civil Judge (Senior Division).

### Three-Tier Civil and Criminal Hierarchy of Subordinate Courts

The organisational structure, pecuniary limits, and exact local designations of subordinate courts are laid down by each **State Government** (through State Civil Courts Acts) in consultation with the High Court, causing slight terminological differences across States—yet the architectural core is uniform across India. At the apex of every district sits the **same judicial officer** wearing two distinct hats: when adjudicating civil suits, they sit as the **District Judge**; when conducting criminal trials, they sit as the **Sessions Judge**.

```flow
# Twin Three-Tier Hierarchy of District and Subordinate Courts (Updated to BNSS 2023)
High Court of the State | Constitutional head exercising `Art. 227` superintendence & `Art. 235` exclusive administrative control
CIVIL SIDE — Tier 1: District Judge | Highest civil court in the district; unlimited original pecuniary jurisdiction + hears civil appeals + administrative head of district courts || CRIMINAL SIDE — Tier 1: Sessions Judge | Highest criminal court in the district; can impose ANY sentence including life imprisonment & death sentence (**death needs mandatory HC confirmation**, `Sec. 407 BNSS`)
CIVIL SIDE — Tier 2: Subordinate Judge / Civil Judge (Sr. Div.) | Unlimited original civil pecuniary jurisdiction in most States; hears appeals from Junior Division where empowered || CRIMINAL SIDE — Tier 2: Chief Judicial Magistrate (CJM) | Tries serious non-sessions offences; imposes imprisonment **up to 7 years** (cannot award death or life; `Sec. 23(1) BNSS, 2023`)
CIVIL SIDE — Tier 3: Munsiff / Civil Judge (Jr. Div.) | Lowest civil court; tries civil suits of small/limited pecuniary value (varies by State statute) || CRIMINAL SIDE — Tier 3: Judicial Magistrate 1st Class (JMFC) & 2nd Class (JMSC) | **JMFC:** up to **3 yrs**, fine up to **₹50,000**, or **community service**; **JMSC:** up to **1 yr**, fine up to **₹10,000**, or **community service** (`Sec. 23(2)-(3) BNSS`)
```

Three important statutory nuances—especially after India replaced the colonial Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 with the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023** (effective **1 July 2024**)—must be mastered for Prelims:
- **Updated Sentencing Ceilings Under `Section 23, BNSS 2023` (Replacing `Section 29 CrPC`):** While a **CJM** continues to pass sentences up to **7 years**, the BNSS dramatically updated Magistrate powers by raising the fine ceiling of a **Judicial Magistrate of the First Class (JMFC)** fivefold from ₹10,000 to **₹50,000** (alongside imprisonment up to **3 years**) and raising a **Judicial Magistrate of the Second Class (JMSC)** from ₹5,000 to **₹10,000** (imprisonment up to **1 year**)—and crucially introduced **community service** as a brand-new non-custodial sentence for petty offences!
- **Abolition of Metropolitan Magistrates and Assistant Sessions Judges (`BNSS, 2023`):** Under the old CrPC, cities with populations exceeding one million (such as Mumbai, Delhi, Kolkata, and Chennai) operated parallel cadres of "Chief Metropolitan Magistrates" and "Metropolitan Magistrates," alongside "Assistant Sessions Judges" (who could sentence up to 10 years). To simplify and homogenise criminal courts nationwide, the **BNSS, 2023 abolished the separate designations of Metropolitan Magistrates and Assistant Sessions Judges**, bringing all urban and district criminal courts under the unified Sessions Judge → CJM → JMFC/JMSC ladder!
- **Specialised District-Level and Revenue Courts:** On the civil side, metropolitan cities also maintain **City Civil Courts** and **Presidency Small Cause Courts** (for swift recovery of small monetary claims). Separately, every State operates a parallel hierarchy of **Revenue Courts**—running from **Tehsildar / Naib Tehsildar** → **Sub-Divisional Officer (SDO)** → **District Collector** → **Divisional Commissioner** → **Board of Revenue** (highest State revenue appellate tribunal)—to adjudicate land-revenue assessment, mutation, and tenancy matters outside ordinary civil courts.

> [!prelims]
> **Subordinate Courts & BNSS 2023 Prelims Checkpoints**
> 1. **SPSC Consultation Trap (`Art. 233` vs `Art. 234`):** The **SPSC** is consulted ONLY for appointing **subordinate judicial officers below District Judge (`Art. 234`)**—**NOT** for appointing **District Judges (`Art. 233`)**, where the Governor consults ONLY the **High Court**!
> 2. **20th Constitutional Amendment (1966):** Inserted **`Article 233A`** to retrospectively validate District Judge appointments made prior to the *Chandra Mohan* (1966) ruling.
> 3. **Dual Hat Rule:** The District Judge and the Sessions Judge are the **same person** exercising civil and criminal jurisdictions respectively.
> 4. **BNSS, 2023 (`Sec. 23`):** JMFC fine limit raised to **₹50,000** (up to 3 yrs); JMSC fine raised to **₹10,000** (up to 1 yr); **community service** added; **Metropolitan Magistrate** and **Assistant Sessions Judge** cadres abolished!
> 5. **Executive Magistrates vs Judicial Magistrates:** Under `Article 50` and BNSS, **Judicial Magistrates** (under HC control) conduct criminal trials and impose sentences; **Executive Magistrates** (District Magistrate/SDM under State Government) handle preventive law-and-order powers (e.g., unlawful assembly dispersal, `Sec. 163 BNSS` prohibitory orders).

Empirical data from the **National Judicial Data Grid (NJDG)**, the **Department of Justice**, the **National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB)**, and the **India Justice Report (2025)** lays bare the scale of the bottleneck as of **November 2026**:

#### Five Structural Root Causes Behind India's Backlog

For UPSC Mains (GS Paper II), listing numbers alone is insufficient—answers must diagnose the **five interlocking structural causes** sustaining pendency:

1. **Chronic Judicial and Support-Staff Vacancies:** Nearly a quarter of sanctioned judicial seats remain empty at any given moment. In the High Courts, friction between the Supreme Court Collegium and the Union Executive over the stalled **Memorandum of Procedure (MoP)**—manifested in selective clearances ("cherry-picking") and prolonged executive "pocket vetoes" on reiterated names—keeps vacancies above 26%. In the district judiciary, States routinely miss the strict annual recruitment timetable mandated by the Supreme Court in ***Malik Mazhar Sultan v. U.P. Public Service Commission* (2007)** because of erratic SPSC–High Court coordination, litigation over exam keys, and delayed physical infrastructure.
2. **The Government as the Largest Litigant (~46–50%):** Union Ministries, State Governments, and Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) have historically accounted for **46% to 50% of all pending cases** in Indian courts! Driven by risk-averse bureaucracy—where civil servants fear audit or vigilance scrutiny (*CAG/CVC*) if they accept an adverse trial ruling—departments file mechanical, meritless appeals right up to the Supreme Court (even in petty service, pension, or land-acquisition disputes already settled by binding precedent). The Union's **LIMBS** (*Legal Information Management & Briefing System*) portal alone tracked **7.27 lakh Central Government cases** as of March 2025, excluding massive State-level dockets.
3. **Adjournment Culture and Procedural Abuse:** Although Order XVII Rule 1 of the CPC caps adjournments at **three** per suit, trial courts routinely grant dozens of adjournments due to Bar strikes, absent police witnesses, unserved summons, and frequent interlocutory appeals against interim orders—stretching straightforward property or criminal trials across 8 to 15 years.
4. **Fiscal Starvation and Infrastructure Deficit:** Combined Union and State budgetary outlay for the judiciary hovers around a dismal **0.1% of GDP** (compared to ~0.4%–0.8% in comparable democracies). The **India Justice Report (2025)** underscored that States allocate nearly 80% of their justice-sector spending to policing and barely 15%–20% to courts, leaving acute shortages of courtrooms, residential quarters, stenographers, record rooms, and forensic science laboratories (whose DNA/ballistics delays stall criminal trials for years).
5. **Docket Flooding by Special Regulatory Statutes and Low-Value Disputes:** A handful of statutory categories choke district dockets—above all **cheque-bounce prosecutions under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881** (accounting for roughly 10%–15% of criminal magistrate backlogs nationwide), motor-accident insurance claims, minor traffic challans, and revenue-recovery suits—matters ideally suited for automated Online Dispute Resolution (ODR) or mediation rather than formal courtroom adjudication.

> [!mains]
> **Rethinking the "50 Judges Per Million" Metric (Mains Value-Addition)**
> Most aspirants mechanically cite the **120th Law Commission Report (1987)** recommendation of **50 judges per million population**. Elevate your Mains answer by adding the crucial corrective from the **245th Law Commission Report (2014)** and the **National Court Management Systems (NCMS) Committee (2016)**: raw population ratios distort reality because litigation filing rates vary hugely across Indian States (for example, per-capita litigation in Kerala or Delhi is many times higher than in Bihar or Assam). Both bodies therefore urged adopting a scientific **"weighted rate-of-disposal / judge-to-caseload" methodology** that calculates required judge strength dynamically from annual case filings, case complexity weights, and backlog clearance targets!

### Major Judicial Reforms and Institutional Debates

Over the past decade—accelerating markedly between 2023 and 2026—the judiciary and executive have pursued five major reform tracks, each carrying sharp Prelims details and rich Mains tensions.

#### 1. The All-India Judicial Service (AIJS) Debate (Article 312)

Envisaged since the **1st Law Commission (14th Report, 1958)** and strongly endorsed by the **116th Law Commission (1986)**, the **First National Judicial Pay Commission (Shetty Commission)**, the **Supreme Court in *All India Judges Association* (1992)**, and **NITI Aayog's *Strategy for New India @ 75***, the **All-India Judicial Service (AIJS)** proposes a centralised, UPSC-style national recruitment service for District Judges—mirroring the IAS and IPS.

Its constitutional pathway was explicitly inserted into **`Article 312`** by the **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`**:
- **Step 1 — Rajya Sabha Special Resolution (`Article 312(1)`):** Because creating an All-India Service curtails State autonomy over district administration, the process can begin **ONLY** if the **Rajya Sabha** (Council of States) passes a resolution declaring it *"necessary or expedient in the national interest"* supported by **not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting**.
- **Step 2 — Ordinary Parliamentary Legislation (`Article 312(1)` & `(4)`):** Armed with that resolution, Parliament may enact a law by **simple majority** establishing the AIJS and regulating its recruitment and conditions of service. **`Article 312(4)`** explicitly clarifies that such a law may make consequential changes to Chapter VI of Part VI (`Articles 233–237`) and **shall NOT be deemed a constitutional amendment under `Article 368`**!
- **Crucial Constitutional Floor (`Article 312(3)`):** **`Article 312(3)` expressly prohibits the AIJS from including ANY post inferior to that of a District Judge** (as defined in `Article 236`). Thus, Civil Judges (Junior/Senior Division) and Judicial Magistrates **cannot** be subsumed into the AIJS; they remain purely State judicial services!

Despite this constitutional provision standing on the statute book for fifty years—and repeated Union attempts to revive it (including fresh consultations placed before the Chief Justices' Conference from 2021 through 2025–2026)—**AIJS has never been created** due to intense opposition from a majority of **High Courts and State Governments**.

```compare
# All-India Judicial Service (Article 312): Case For vs Federal & Institutional Objections
Arguments FOR Creating AIJS | Federal & Operational Objections AGAINST AIJS
Merit & Uniform Talent Pool :: National UPSC-calibre exam attracts top law graduates nationwide, raising trial-court judgment quality and creating a stronger future High Court bench pipeline | **Local Language & Custom Barrier:** District trials rely heavily on witnesses, FIRs, land records, and cross-examinations in regional languages (Tamil, Marathi, Assamese, Malayalam); all-India cadres risk linguistic alienation
Eliminating Chronic Vacancies :: Predictable annual centralised recruitment ends erratic State-level exam cancellations, SPSC delays, and the 23.7% district vacancy crisis | **Erosion of Federalism & `Art. 235` Control:** Transfers control over District Judge selection from High Courts and States toward the Union, weakening `Article 235` High Court superintendence and judicial independence
Social Inclusion & Diversity :: Uniform national reservation norms systematically widen representation of **SC, ST, OBC, and women** judges at the District Judge level, feeding diversity into High Courts | **Promotion Bottleneck for State Civil Judges:** Filling District Judge posts via centralised direct entry shrinks upward promotion prospects for serving State Civil Judges—demoralising the lower judiciary
Cost & Uniform Standards :: Harmonises training (via a National Judicial Academy track), pay parity, and ethical accountability across rich and poorer States alike | **Existing Constitutional Alternative:** Critics argue enforcing *Malik Mazhar Sultan* (2007) annual calendars, **Second National Judicial Pay Commission** parity, and *Rejanish K.V.* (2025) merit rules solves vacancies without undermining federalism
```

#### 2. Digital Justice: e-Courts Mission Mode Project Phase III and Judicial AI

India's most tangible modern judicial reform is the **e-Courts Mission Mode Project**, jointly implemented by the **Department of Justice** and the **e-Committee of the Supreme Court** under the National e-Governance Plan. Following **Phase I (2011–2015)** (basic court computerisation and initial jail-court video-conferencing) and **Phase II (2015–2023)** (rollout of Case Information Software [CIS], **National Judicial Data Grid [NJDG]** public tracking, e-filing, and nationwide VC equipment across 3,240 court complexes and 1,272 jails), the Union Cabinet approved **e-Courts Phase III on 13 September 2023** with a landmark budgetary outlay of **₹7,210 crore for 2023–2027**.

Phase III transitions Indian courts from *digitised record-keeping* to **native paperless, cloud-hosted digital justice**, encompassing:
- **Complete Legacy & Fresh Digitisation:** Targeting over 310 crore legacy pages originally—massively exceeded in execution! According to Union Law Ministry **Lok Sabha replies in March 2026**, Indian courts had already digitised **660.36 crore pages**, conducted **3.97+ crore hearings via video-conferencing**, and operationalised **2,444 e-Sewa Kendras** (one-stop citizen helpdesks bridging the digital divide for rural litigants).
- **Virtual Courts & Automated Traffic/Petty Adjudication:** Eliminating physical court appearances for minor traffic challans and statutory offences via round-the-clock online guilty pleas and digital fine settlement (resolving crores of challans without clogging magistrate benches).
- **Live-Streaming of Constitutional Proceedings:** Anchored in ***Swapnil Tripathi v. Supreme Court of India* (2018)** (recognising open-court access as integral to `Article 21`), the Supreme Court Constitution Benches and multiple High Courts (Gujarat, Karnataka, Orissa, Patna, Madhya Pradesh, Jharkhand, Gauhati, Calcutta) now live-stream courtrooms publicly, boosting institutional transparency.
- **The Judicial AI & Electronic Ecosystem:** Three Supreme Court digital platforms amplify Phase III's reach:
  - **FASTER** (*Fast and Secured Transmission of Electronic Records*): Instant, cryptographically authenticated transmission of Supreme Court bail orders, interim stays, and release warrants directly to prison superintendents—ending the tragedy of prisoners languishing in jail for days waiting for physical postal copies.
  - **SUVAS** (*Supreme Court Vidhik Anuvaad Software*): AI-assisted neural translation engine converting English Supreme Court and High Court judgments into Indian vernacular languages (over 16–18 scheduled languages), operationalising true linguistic access to justice.
  - **SUPACE** (*Supreme Court Portal for Assistance in Courts' Efficiency*): An AI-powered research assistant that reads voluminous case files, extracts relevant facts/precedents, and organises dossiers for judges while strictly leaving final legal decision-making to human judicial discretion.
- **Digital Access Initiatives (DISHA Scheme):** Complementing e-Courts are **Tele-Law** (connecting rural citizens at Common Service Centres [CSCs] to panel lawyers via video/tele-conferencing before litigation begins) and **Nyaya Bandhu** (India's national *pro bono* legal services network matching verified volunteer advocates with marginalised litigants).

#### 3. Fast-Track Special Courts, Commercial Courts and Infrastructure Reform

- **Fast-Track Special Courts (FTSCs):** Launched in **October 2019** as a Centrally Sponsored Scheme funded largely from the **Nirbhaya Fund** (subsequently extended up to **September 2026**) pursuant to the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2018, FTSCs expedite trials of rape and **Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act** cases. As of **30 June 2026**, **779 functional FTSCs**—including **399 exclusive e-POCSO courts**—operating across 29 States and UTs had disposed of **3.97 lakh cases**. However, critics warn that FTSCs must not rely on temporary ad-hoc judges borrowed from regular dockets without creating permanent additional benches and forensic capacity.
- **Commercial Courts Act, 2015 (Amended 2018):** Established dedicated **Commercial Courts** at the district level and **Commercial Divisions/Appellate Divisions** inside High Courts with original jurisdiction, lowered the "specified value" threshold from ₹1 crore to **₹3 lakh** (2018), mandated strict Case Management Hearings, and introduced **compulsory pre-institution mediation (`Section 12A`)** for non-urgent commercial suits—improving contract enforcement rankings and easing business friction.
- **Evening Courts, Morning Courts and Shift Systems:** Pioneered successfully in **Gujarat** (and recommended by the **Malimath Committee** and Law Commission), utilising existing courthouse buildings during off-peak hours (6:00 PM–8:30 PM) with separate judicial staff to dispose of petty criminal, traffic, and cheque-bounce cases without waiting for new physical buildings.
- **National Judicial Infrastructure Authority of India (NJIAI):** Proposed by former CJI N.V. Ramana in 2021 to replace fragmented, under-utilised Centrally Sponsored Schemes with a dedicated statutory umbrella body (modeled on NALSA, with State counterparts) to plan, fund, and standardise courtrooms, digital infrastructure, toilets, waiting areas, and witness-protection rooms across India's district courts.

#### 4. Curbing Government Litigation: From National Litigation Policy to the April 2025 Directive

For over fifteen years—since the draft **National Litigation Policy (NLP), 2010** sought to transform the State into an "efficient and responsible litigant"—successive governments struggled to enact a binding cabinet-level NLP. In a major administrative shift on **4 April 2025**, the **Department of Legal Affairs (Union Ministry of Law & Justice)** dropped the standalone Cabinet-level NLP draft and issued the **"Directive for the Efficient and Effective Management of Litigation by the Government of India,"** binding all Union Ministries, Departments, and CPSEs. Key operational pillars include mandatory pre-litigation internal vetting, discouraging routine appeals below monetary/policy thresholds, enforcing inter-departmental dispute resolution (AMRCD — *Administrative Mechanism for Resolution of CPSEs Disputes*) instead of suing fellow public entities in court, and real-time case tracking via **LIMBS**. Notably, the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice nonetheless selected a comprehensive review of a statutory **National Litigation Policy** for its **2026–27** work agenda, underscoring that executive circulars alone rarely restrain State-level litigation.

#### 5. Regional Benches of the Supreme Court and the National Court of Appeal Debate

Under **`Article 130`**, the Supreme Court sits in Delhi *"or in such other place or places, as the Chief Justice of India may, with the approval of the President, from time to time, appoint."* Because New Delhi is geographically distant from southern, eastern, and north-eastern States—making Supreme Court access disproportionately expensive for litigants outside North India—the **229th Law Commission Report (2009)** proposed transforming the Delhi bench into a dedicated **Constitutional Court** while creating **four regional Cassation Benches** (North at Delhi, South at Chennai/Hyderabad, East at Kolkata, West at Mumbai) to hear non-constitutional appellate matters. Similarly, the **Attorney General** and Parliamentary Committees have periodically backed establishing a **National Court of Appeal** between the High Courts and the Supreme Court.

> [!debate]
> **Regional Benches of the Supreme Court / National Court of Appeal: Access vs Institutional Unity**
> - **Case For Regional Benches:** Eliminates extreme geographic skew (empirical studies show Delhi, Punjab & Haryana, and neighbouring States generate a vastly higher share of SC SLPs than Tamil Nadu, Kerala, or Assam); cuts litigant travel and elite-Bar fee barriers; frees the Delhi Supreme Court to sit regularly in five- and seven-judge **Constitution Benches** rather than acting as an overgrown municipal court of appeal (a backlog concern that pushed Parliament to raise SC strength from 34 to **38** in August 2026).
> - **Case Against (Supreme Court Full Court Position):** Repeatedly rejecting `Article 130` regional benches (in 1999, 2001, 2004, and 2010), the Supreme Court Full Court warns that splitting the apex court would erode its **institutional unity, collegial coherence, and uniform national precedent (`Article 141`)**, and that hybrid/video-conferencing hearings under **e-Courts Phase III** can deliver nationwide access without fragmenting the Court. A 2016 Constitution Bench reference (*V. Vasanthakumar v. H.C. Bhatia*) on creating a National Court of Appeal remains pending.

## 4. Tribunals and Quasi-Judicial Bodies

As India transformed from a minimalist post-colonial state into a complex welfare and regulatory republic, ordinary civil and criminal courts struggled to keep pace with highly technical disputes involving public-service rules, customs valuation, corporate insolvency, telecom spectrum tariffs, electricity pricing, and ecological damage. Generalist judges bound by the strict technicalities of the Code of Civil Procedure (CPC), 1908 and the Evidence Act often lacked specialised domain training, while clogged High Court dockets delayed economic and administrative certainty for decades. To bridge this institutional gap, modern administrative law turned to **tribunals and quasi-judicial bodies**.

Crucially, **the original Constitution of 1950 contained NO separate Part on tribunals** (though `Articles 136` and `227` casually acknowledged their statutory existence—India's oldest tribunal, the **Income Tax Appellate Tribunal [ITAT]**, had already operated since **1941**!). Acting on the recommendations of the **Swaran Singh Committee (1976)**, Parliament enacted the **`42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`**, inserting an entirely new **`Part XIV-A`** titled **"Tribunals,"** consisting of **only two Articles**: **`Article 323A`** (Administrative Tribunals) and **`Article 323B`** (Tribunals for Other Matters).

### Quasi-Judicial Nature, Rationale and Institutional Characteristics

A **quasi-judicial body** is a non-court statutory or constitutional institution vested with adjudicatory powers to determine legal rights, impose penalties, or resolve disputes between parties—standing midway between a regular **court of law** and a purely **executive/administrative authority**. While every tribunal is a quasi-judicial body, the broader quasi-judicial universe also encompasses statutory rights commissions (such as the **NHRC**, **CIC**, and **NCW**), market regulators exercising adjudicatory functions (such as **SEBI**, **CCI**, and **TRAI**), three-tier **Consumer Commissions**, and the **Election Commission** when deciding party-symbol disputes or `Article 103/192` disqualification references.

Four functional features distinguish tribunals from ordinary courts:
1. **Principles of Natural Justice Over Rigid Technical Procedure:** Tribunals are ordinarily **not bound by the strict procedural rules of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908** or the strict rules of evidence under the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023** (formerly Indian Evidence Act, 1872). Instead, they are constitutionally bound to adhere to the three bedrock **principles of natural justice**:
   - ***Audi alteram partem*** (no person shall be condemned unheard—fair notice and opportunity of hearing);
   - ***Nemo judex in causa sua*** (no person shall be a judge in their own cause—freedom from personal, pecuniary, or institutional bias); and
   - **Reasoned ("speaking") orders** showing transparent application of mind so appellate/writ courts can test legality.
2. **Blended Judicial and Technical Composition:** Most major tribunals pair **Judicial Members** (retired or sitting judges/senior advocates) with **Technical or Expert Members** (chartered accountants, ecologists, engineers, economists, or senior civil servants), combining legal rigour with sector-specific expertise.
3. **Speed, Accessibility and Lower Cost:** Simplified pleadings, nominal filing fees, and self-representation (allowing litigants to appear without costly counsel in fora like CAT or consumer commissions) aim to deliver affordable, rapid redress.
4. **Docket Decongestion for High Courts:** By siphoning off high-volume specialised disputes—such as lakhs of government-employee service grievances or GST tax appeals—tribunals free High Courts to focus on core constitutional and appellate adjudication.

### Constitutional Architecture: Article 323A vs Article 323B

Although both provisions entered the Constitution together via the `42nd Amendment (1976)`, **`Article 323A`** and **`Article 323B`** differ fundamentally across **three exact dimensions** repeatedly tested in UPSC Prelims—**subject matter**, **establishing legislature**, and **institutional hierarchy**:

```compare
# Constitutional Comparison: Article 323A vs Article 323B (Part XIV-A)
Article 323A — Administrative Tribunals | Article 323B — Tribunals for Other Matters
Subject Matter :: **Public service matters ONLY** — disputes & complaints regarding recruitment and conditions of service of Union, State, local/public-authority, or PSU employees | **9 Enumerated Matters:** (1) Taxation; (2) Foreign exchange, import & export; (3) Industrial & labour disputes; (4) Land reforms; (5) Urban property ceiling; (6) **Elections to Parliament & State Legislatures**; (7) Foodstuffs/essential goods supply & prices; (8) **Rent & tenancy** (`75th Amend, 1994`); (9) Offences & fees connected to the above
Who Can Establish :: **Parliament ONLY** — State Legislatures have **ZERO power** to enact a law creating an `Art. 323A` tribunal! | **BOTH Parliament AND State Legislatures** with respect to matters falling within their respective Seventh Schedule legislative competence
Hierarchy Permitted? :: **NO hierarchy permitted** — only ONE tribunal for the Centre (CAT), and ONE for each State (SAT) or TWO or more States (JAT) | **Hierarchy IS permitted** — legislatures may create multi-tier original AND appellate tribunals (e.g., DRT → DRAT; NCLT → NCLAT)
Statutory Examples :: Central Administrative Tribunal (**CAT**), State Administrative Tribunals (**SATs**), Joint Administrative Tribunals (**JATs**) under *Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985* | Industrial Tribunals, Rent Control Tribunals, Land Reforms Tribunals, Tax Tribunals *(note: Parliament can also create tribunals like NCLT/NGT directly under Seventh Schedule entries outside Art. 323B — R. Gandhi, 2010!)*
```

#### Central, State and Joint Administrative Tribunals (Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985)

Exercising its exclusive authority under `Article 323A`, Parliament enacted the **Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985**, establishing a dedicated service-jurisprudence tier across India:
- **Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT):** Operational since November 1985, CAT sits with its **Principal Bench in New Delhi** alongside 18+ additional regular and circuit benches across State capitals. It exercises **original jurisdiction** over recruitment and service matters concerning **All-India Services (IAS, IPS, IFoS)**, **Central civil services**, **civil posts under the Union**, and **civilian employees of the defence services** (plus notified central PSUs/autonomous bodies). CAT is a multi-member body headed by a Chairperson (serving or retired High Court Judge) supported by Judicial and Administrative Members. Crucially, UPSC Prelims routinely tests the **three exact constitutional/statutory categories EXCLUDED from CAT's jurisdiction**:
  1. **Members of the defence forces** (Army, Navy, Air Force — separately covered since 2009 by the **Armed Forces Tribunal [AFT]** under the *AFT Act, 2007*);
  2. **Officers and servants of the Supreme Court** (and High Court staff); and
  3. **Secretarial staff of Parliament** (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha Secretariats).
- **State Administrative Tribunals (SATs) and Joint Administrative Tribunals (JATs):** Under the 1985 Act, the **Central Government** (NOT the State Legislature!) establishes a **SAT** strictly on the **specific request of the concerned State Government**, or a **JAT** for two or more States on their joint request. The Chairperson and Members of a SAT/JAT are appointed by the **President after consultation with the Governor(s)** of the concerned State(s). Notably, dissatisfaction over mounting SAT delays and vacancies prompted several States—including **Madhya Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Himachal Pradesh, Odisha, and Andhra Pradesh**—to request the Centre to abolish their SATs, transferring State-employee service cases back to High Courts.

> [!prelims]
> **Four High-Yield UPSC Prelims Traps on Part XIV-A Tribunals**
> 1. **Not in Original 1950 Constitution:** `Part XIV-A (Arts. 323A–323B)` was inserted by the **`42nd Amendment (1976)`** (Swaran Singh Committee)—NOT original!
> 2. **Elections & Rent Are Under `Article 323B`:** Disputes relating to **elections to Parliament and State Legislatures** sit under **`Article 323B`** (NOT `323A`), and **rent & tenancy** was added to `Art. 323B` by the **`75th Amendment Act, 1994`**.
> 3. **Who Creates SATs?** Even **State Administrative Tribunals (SATs)** are established by the **Central Government** (under Parliament's 1985 Act) upon a State's request—State Legislatures CANNOT establish `Art. 323A` tribunals!
> 4. **CAT's Three Exclusions:** CAT covers *civilian defence staff*, **BUT EXCLUDES** (a) **armed forces personnel**, (b) **Supreme Court officers/staff**, and (c) **Parliamentary secretarial staff**!

### The Tribunalisation Tug-of-War: Landmark Jurisprudence and the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026

No institutional battlefield in modern Indian constitutional law has generated sharper Executive–Judiciary friction over the past four decades than **"tribunalisation of justice."** Originally, `Articles 323A(2)(d)` and `323B(3)(d)` authorised legislatures to **completely exclude the jurisdiction of ALL courts—including High Courts under `Articles 226/227`—except the Supreme Court under `Article 136`**. Simultaneously, ministries began staffing tribunals with retiring bureaucrats, controlling their budgets and secretariats, shortening tenures to 3–4 years, and routing appeals straight to the Supreme Court. Across seven landmark rulings between 1987 and November 2025, the Supreme Court systematically defended judicial independence and separation of powers—culminating in Parliament's sweeping **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026**.

#### The Constitutional Appeal Pathway Post-L. Chandra Kumar (1997)

A subtle yet recurring confusion among aspirants concerns **where appeals against tribunal orders lie**. Under the constitutional rule laid down by the seven-judge bench in ***L. Chandra Kumar* (1997)**, every tribunal created under `Articles 323A` or `323B` remains subject to the writ and supervisory scrutiny of the jurisdictional **High Court (`Articles 226/227`)**—specifically before a **Division Bench (two judges)**—so that aggrieved litigants cannot leapfrog directly to the Supreme Court under `Article 136`. Simultaneously, however, Parliament has enacted numerous **sectoral statutes** outside `Part XIV-A` (such as the NGT Act 2010, Companies Act/IBC for NCLAT, SEBI Act for SAT, Consumer Protection Act for NCDRC, and AFT Act) providing a **direct statutory first appeal to the Supreme Court**! Both the Supreme Court (in *Rojer Mathew*, 2019) and the **272nd Law Commission Report (2017)** have criticised this direct-SC bypass for choking the apex court with routine fact-heavy appeals and pricing out regional litigants.

#### Madras Bar Association (19 November 2025) and the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026

When Parliament responded to *Madras Bar Association-5* (July 2021) by enacting the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021**—abolishing nine appellate tribunals (including the **Intellectual Property Appellate Board [IPAB]** and **Film Certification Appellate Tribunal [FCAT]**) while brazenly re-enacting the exact **50-year minimum age bar**, **four-year tenure**, and **two-name SCSC panel** that the Supreme Court had just declared unconstitutional weeks earlier—it triggered an acute constitutional confrontation over separation of powers.

On **19 November 2025**, in ***Madras Bar Association v. Union of India* (`2025 INSC 1330`)**, a Division Bench of **Chief Justice B.R. Gavai and Justice K. Vinod Chandran** struck down those re-enacted provisions of the 2021 Act as an impermissible **"legislative override."** While Parliament may alter the underlying legal basis of a judgment, it cannot merely re-enact invalidated clauses verbatim without removing their constitutional defects. Ordering the Union Government to establish an independent **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)** within **four months** (and an interim Tribunals Wing inside the Ministry of Finance in the meantime), Chief Justice Gavai delivered a ringing defence of institutional autonomy:

> "When Parliament designs or alters the tribunal system, it must do so in a manner consistent with the constitutional requirements of independence, impartiality, and effective adjudication. A law that undermines these foundational values, such as by enabling executive control over appointments, curtailing tenure arbitrarily, or weakening institutional autonomy, does not merely offend an 'abstract principle', it strikes at the core of the constitutional arrangement." — Chief Justice B.R. Gavai, *Madras Bar Association v. Union of India*, 19 November 2025

> [!case]
> ***Madras Bar Association v. Union of India* (19 November 2025, `2025 INSC 1330`) — "MBA-6"**
> - **Bench:** Chief Justice B.R. Gavai and Justice K. Vinod Chandran.
> - **Core Holding:** Invalidated the re-enacted tenure (4-year), minimum age (50-year), and two-name SCSC recommendation clauses of the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** as an unconstitutional **legislative override** violating separation of powers and judicial independence; confirmed that *MBA-4* (2020) and *MBA-5* (2021) standards govern appointments and service conditions.
> - **Mandamus:** Directed the Centre to constitute an independent **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)** within four months to sever tribunals' financial and administrative dependence on parent ministries (which are routinely the largest litigants before those very tribunals!).

> [!news]
> **The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026 (`Act No. 17 of 2026` — Enforced 25 August 2026)**
> Responding to *MBA-6* (Nov 2025), Parliament passed the **Tribunals Reforms Bill, 2026** (Lok Sabha 10 Aug; Rajya Sabha 11 Aug), receiving Presidential assent on **13 August 2026** and entering into force on **25 August 2026** (`Notification S.O. 4713(E)`). Repealing the 2021 Act, its landmark features include:
> 1. **Judiciary-Led National Tribunals Commission (NTC):** Establishes a **five-member NTC** headquartered in **New Delhi**—a **Chairperson** (former SC Judge or HC Chief Justice), **two Judicial Members** (former HC CJ or HC Judge), and **two Technical Members** (25+ years' specialised experience)—serving **5 years or until age 70** to supervise selections, administration, infrastructure, and discipline across **16 tribunals** listed in the First Schedule (including CAT, NGT, NCLAT, ITAT, CESTAT, NCDRC, AFT, SAT, TDSAT, and DRTs/DRATs).
> 2. **Restored Tenure and Abolished 50-Year Bar:** Restores a **5-year tenure** (with upper age caps of **70 years** for Chairpersons and **67 years** for Members) and **eliminates the controversial 50-year minimum age bar**, reopening tribunal benches to accomplished advocates with 10+ years' standing.
> 3. **Single-Name SCSC Recommendations & 3-Month Cap:** Replaces executive-tilted two-name panels with a **single-name recommendation** (plus **one waitlisted candidate**) from the NTC-led Search-cum-Selection Committee, with a mandatory **three-month timeline** for Union appointment decisions.
> 4. **National Tribunals Data Grid:** Mandates a unified public digital case-repository mirroring the NJDG for all First Schedule tribunals.

> [!mains]
> **Critical Mains Evaluation: Does the 2026 Act Fully Resolve the Tribunal Independence Crisis?**
> While the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026** marks a historic statutory milestone by creating the **NTC**, constitutional scholars and practitioners (echoing the **272nd Law Commission Report, 2017**) flag **four lingering structural vulnerabilities** as of **November 2026**:
> 1. **Executive Leverage Inside the NTC:** The Union Government appoints NTC Judicial Members after mere *"consultation"* (NOT binding concurrence) with the CJI, appoints Technical Members unilaterally, and retains significant complaint-screening and removal powers over NTC members—inviting the *"old wine in a new bottle"* critique.
> 2. **Glaring Exclusions from the First Schedule:** Crucially, **BOTH the GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT) AND the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) sit OUTSIDE the 2026 Act's First Schedule!** GSTAT thus remains governed by `Section 110 of the CGST Act`, which still retains the very **50-year age bar, 4-year term, and 2-name panel** invalidated in *MBA-6*—exposing GST appeals to fresh constitutional challenges!
> 3. **Deterrents to Young Legal Talent:** A five-year term without assured renewal, paired with post-tenure appearance restrictions before the tribunal, continues to deter top mid-career advocates from leaving lucrative Bar practices.
> 4. **Direct Supreme Court Appeals & High Court Bypass:** The 2026 Act leaves untouched numerous sectoral statutes routing appeals straight from tribunals to the Supreme Court, perpetuating SC docket congestion.

#### Deep Dive: National Green Tribunal (NGT Act, 2010)

Enacted pursuant to India's international commitments at the **1992 Rio Earth Summit** and **2002 Johannesburg Summit**, and operationalising the **Right to a Healthy Environment under `Article 21`** (*Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar*, 1991), the **National Green Tribunal Act, 2010** made India only the **third country in the world** (after **Australia** and **New Zealand**)—and the **first developing nation**—to establish a specialised statutory environmental tribunal.

- **Structure, Benches and Composition:** Headquartered at its **Principal Bench in New Delhi**, NGT operates through **four Zonal Benches** at **Bhopal** (Central), **Pune** (Western), **Kolkata** (Eastern), and **Chennai** (Southern), supplemented by circuit benches (Shimla, Shillong, Jodhpur, Kochi). It comprises a **Chairperson** (retired Supreme Court Judge or High Court Chief Justice), **10 to 20 full-time Judicial Members**, and **10 to 20 full-time Expert Members** (environmental scientists, technologists, and forestry/ecological administrators), appointed for a **five-year non-renewable term** (brought under the 2026 NTC framework).
- **Guiding Principles (`Section 20`), Procedure and Timeline:** Unbound by the CPC or Evidence Act, NGT is governed by **principles of natural justice** and is statutorily mandated under **Section 20** to apply three foundational environmental doctrines in every order:
  1. **Principle of Sustainable Development** (balancing economic progress with intergenerational ecological equity);
  2. **Precautionary Principle** (where threats of serious or irreversible ecological harm exist, lack of full scientific certainty cannot postpone preventive action); and
  3. **Polluter Pays Principle** (imposing absolute liability on polluters to compensate victims AND restore degraded ecosystems).
  NGT exercises **civil jurisdiction** over substantial questions relating to the environment, must endeavour to dispose of applications within **six months of filing**, awards compensation/restitution, imposes penalties for non-compliance (imprisonment up to 3 years and/or fine up to ₹10 crore for individuals, ₹25 crore for companies), and its orders are appealable to the **Supreme Court within 90 days**.

#### Deep Dive: The Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT, 2025–2026)

For eight years after the GST rollout in July 2017, the absence of a functional **GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT)** forced lakhs of businesses to flood High Courts with `Article 226` writ petitions against first-appellate tax orders. Following amendments to `Sections 109–110 of the CGST Act, 2017`, the Union formally **launched GSTAT on 24 September 2025** under its inaugural President, **Justice Sanjaya Kumar Mishra** (former Chief Justice of the Jharkhand High Court), and **commenced adjudicatory hearings on 16 February 2026**. Operating via its **Principal Bench in New Delhi** (which also assumed the statutory role of **National Appellate Authority for Advance Rulings [NAAAR]** in **April 2026**) alongside **31 State Benches across 45 locations**, each full GSTAT bench embodies fiscal cooperative federalism by pairing **two Judicial Members** with **two Technical Members (one Centre, one State)**. Orders of State Benches on substantial questions of law are appealable to the jurisdictional **High Court**, while Principal Bench orders (deciding inter-State "place of supply" disputes) are appealable directly to the **Supreme Court**.

## 5. Alternative Dispute Resolution and Legal Aid

No democracy can clear a backlog of **5.85 crore pending cases** solely by building more adversarial courtrooms. Formal litigation in India is frequently prohibitively expensive, linguistically alienating, emotionally destructive to family and business relationships, and structurally slow. Shifting the justice system from a **litigation-first** reflex to a **consensual, multi-door dispute resolution culture**—combining **free legal aid**, grassroots **Lok Adalats**, **Family Courts**, **Gram Nyayalayas**, commercial **Arbitration**, and standalone **Mediation**—is therefore both a constitutional obligation and an economic imperative.

### Constitutional Anchor and Statutory Foundations of Legal Aid

The bedrock of India's Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) and legal-aid architecture rests on three constitutional provisions reinforced by a pivotal civil-procedure statute:

- **`Article 39A` (Directive Principle of State Policy — Inserted by `42nd Amendment Act, 1976`):** Directs the State to secure that *"the operation of the legal system promotes justice, on a basis of equal opportunity, and shall, in particular, provide free legal aid, by suitable legislation or schemes or in any other way, to ensure that opportunities for securing justice are not denied to any citizen by reason of economic or other disabilities."*
- **Fundamental Rights Synthesis (`Articles 14 and 21`):** Transforming `Article 39A` from an unenforceable directive into a live constitutional guarantee, the Supreme Court held in ***M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra* (1978)** and ***Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar* (1979)** that free legal representation for an indigent accused is an indispensable ingredient of *"reasonable, fair and just procedure"* under **`Article 21`**—without which a criminal trial stands constitutionally vitiated!
- **`Section 89` of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (Inserted by 1999 Amendment, Effective 1 July 2002):** Empowered every civil court, wherever elements of an acceptable settlement appear to exist, to formulate settlement terms and refer pending disputes outside the courtroom to **four statutory ADR tracks**: **(1) Arbitration**, **(2) Conciliation**, **(3) Judicial Settlement (including Lok Adalat)**, and **(4) Mediation**. In ***Afcons Infrastructure Ltd. v. Cherian Varkey Construction Co.* (2010)**, the Supreme Court rationalised `Section 89` by clarifying which disputes are suitable for ADR (commercial, family, tort, consumer, contractual) and which are non-arbitrable/unsuitable (serious crimes, sovereign election disputes, guardianship requiring judicial determination, serious fraud).

### NALSA and the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987

To translate `Article 39A` into a uniform nationwide statutory grid, Parliament enacted the **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987**, which came into force on **9 November 1995** (commemorated annually across India as **National Legal Services Day**). The Act constructs a **four-tier institutional pyramid** dedicated to free legal aid, legal literacy, and Lok Adalats:
1. **National Legal Services Authority (NALSA):** The apex policy-making, monitoring, and fund-allocating body. The **Chief Justice of India** serves as **Patron-in-Chief**, while the **second senior-most Judge of the Supreme Court** is nominated by the President (in consultation with the CJI) as **Executive Chairman**. *(Complemented by the Supreme Court Legal Services Committee headed by a sitting SC Judge.)*
2. **State Legal Services Authority (SLSA):** Established in every State, with the **Chief Justice of the High Court** as Patron-in-Chief and a serving or retired **High Court Judge** as Executive Chairman. *(Complemented by High Court Legal Services Committees.)*
3. **District Legal Services Authority (DLSA):** Established in every district, chaired *ex officio* by the **District Judge**.
4. **Taluk Legal Services Committee (TLSC):** Operational at the sub-divisional/taluk level, chaired *ex officio* by the senior-most judicial officer in the taluk.

#### All Eight Eligible Categories for Free Legal Aid (Section 12)

A staple UPSC Prelims trap tests **who qualifies for free legal services under `Section 12` of the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987**. Every person who has to file or defend a case is entitled to free legal aid (covering court fees, counsel fees, certified copies, and paper-book expenses) if they belong to **any one of the eight statutory categories**:
1. **`Section 12(a)`:** A member of a **Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe**;
2. **`Section 12(b)`:** A **victim of trafficking in human beings or *begar*** (as referred to in `Article 23` of the Constitution);
3. **`Section 12(c)`:** A **woman or a child** *(crucial Prelims point: EVERY woman and EVERY child qualifies regardless of personal or family income!)*;
4. **`Section 12(d)`:** A **person with disability** (under statutory disability law);
5. **`Section 12(e)`:** A person under circumstances of **undeserved want**, such as being a **victim of a mass disaster, ethnic violence, caste atrocity, flood, drought, earthquake, or industrial disaster**;
6. **`Section 12(f)`:** An **industrial workman**;
7. **`Section 12(g)`:** A person **in custody**—including custody in a protective home under the *Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956*, a juvenile home under the Juvenile Justice framework, or a psychiatric hospital/nursing home under mental healthcare law; and
8. **`Section 12(h)`:** A person in receipt of **annual income below the prescribed ceiling**—while the 1987 bare text retains legacy baselines (₹9,000/₹12,000 *"or such other higher amount"*), notified Central and State rules currently fix the means ceiling at **₹5 lakh per annum** for cases before the **Supreme Court**, and between **₹1 lakh and ₹3 lakh per annum** across most States and UTs for High Courts and subordinate courts! Crucially, categories `(a)` through `(g)` are **completely independent of income**!

To elevate the quality of indigent criminal defence—historically hampered by underpaid part-time panel lawyers—NALSA launched the **Legal Aid Defense Counsel System (LADCS)** (deploying full-time salaried public defenders in district courts across India), supported by community **Para-Legal Volunteers (PLVs)**, the toll-free national legal helpline **15100**, and **Legal Aid Clinics** inside prisons and law colleges.

### Lok Adalats vs Permanent Lok Adalats

Rooted in Gandhian ideals of village conciliation and participatory harmony, **Lok Adalats** literally mean **"People's Courts."** The first post-independence Lok Adalat camp was organised informally at **Una in Gujarat in March 1982**. Recognising their extraordinary potential to settle thousands of disputes swiftly and amicably, Parliament conferred full **statutory status** on Lok Adalats through **Chapter VI (`Sections 19–22`) of the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987**.

#### Regular Lok Adalats (Sections 19 to 22)

- **Organisation and Composition:** Convened periodically by NALSA, SLSAs, DLSAs, or TLSCs (including nationwide quarterly **National Lok Adalats**). Each bench ordinarily comprises a **Chairman** (a serving or retired judicial officer) alongside two other members—typically a **lawyer** and a **social worker**.
- **Jurisdiction:** Covers both **cases already pending in any court** AND **pre-litigation disputes** not yet filed in court—spanning civil suits, motor-accident compensation claims, land-acquisition references, bank recovery, matrimonial/family disputes (excluding divorce decrees requiring formal court satisfaction), utility bills, and **compoundable criminal offences ONLY**. **Crucial Prohibition:** A Lok Adalat has **ZERO jurisdiction over any non-compoundable criminal offence** (such as murder, rape, dacoity, or non-compoundable corruption offences)!
- **Zero Court Fee and Fee Refund:** Filing a pre-litigation dispute before a Lok Adalat carries **no court fee**. Even more attractively, when a suit already pending in a civil court is referred to and settled in a Lok Adalat, the **court fee originally paid by the plaintiff is refunded in full**!
- **Powers, Compromise-Only Mandate and Finality:** A Lok Adalat wields the procedural powers of a **civil court** under the CPC (summoning witnesses, examining witnesses on oath, compelling document production) and is guided strictly by justice, equity, fair play, and legal principles. Its cardinal operational rule is **pure compromise**: it persuades and facilitates mutual settlement, and **CANNOT adjudicate on merits if parties fail to reach consensus** (simply returning pending cases to the originating court). Once settled, however, every Lok Adalat award is deemed a **decree of a civil court**, is **final and binding on all parties**, and **NO APPEAL lies against it to ANY court**! As the Supreme Court clarified in ***State of Punjab v. Jalour Singh* (2008)**, a Lok Adalat award can be questioned solely through a very narrow High Court writ petition under `Articles 226/227` on extraordinary grounds such as **fraud, coercion, or total lack of consent**.

#### Permanent Lok Adalats (Chapter VI-A, Sections 22A to 22E — 2002 Amendment)

Because Regular Lok Adalats could not pass an order whenever a stubborn public-sector insurer, power utility, or telecom company walked out of negotiations—forcing ordinary citizens back into years of costly civil trials—Parliament enacted the **Legal Services Authorities (Amendment) Act, 2002**, inserting **Chapter VI-A (`Sections 22A–22E`)** to establish **Permanent Lok Adalats (PLAs)** exclusively for **pre-litigation conciliation AND adjudication of Public Utility Service disputes**.

Under `Section 22A`, **Public Utility Services (PUS)** statutorily encompass **six core sectors**: **(1)** transport services (air, road, or water), **(2)** postal, telegraph, or telephone services, **(3)** supply of power, light, or water to the public, **(4)** public conservancy or sanitation, **(5)** hospital or dispensary services, and **(6)** insurance services—plus any additional service notified by the Central or State Government in public interest (several jurisdictions have additionally notified housing/real estate, education, and financial/banking services). Each PLA consists of a **Chairman** (serving or retired District Judge or judicial officer of higher rank) and **two other persons** having adequate experience in public utility services. Its pecuniary ceiling—originally ₹10 lakh—was increased tenfold by Central Government notification on **20 March 2015** to **₹1 crore**!

```compare
# Regular Lok Adalat vs Permanent Lok Adalat (Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987)
Regular Lok Adalat (Chapter VI, Secs. 19–22) | Permanent Lok Adalat (Chapter VI-A, Secs. 22A–22E, 2002 Amend)
Nature & Continuity :: Organised periodically (periodic benches & quarterly National Lok Adalats) | **Permanent, continuous statutory tribunal** specifically dedicated to Public Utility Services
Stage & Subject Jurisdiction :: **Both pending court cases AND pre-litigation** civil matters + **compoundable criminal offences** (NO non-compoundable offences) | **Pre-litigation ONLY** (before any party approaches a court) strictly for **Public Utility Services (PUS)**; **NO criminal offence jurisdiction** whatsoever
Pecuniary Limit :: **No fixed upper pecuniary limit** | Up to **₹1 crore** (raised from ₹10 lakh on **20 March 2015**; Central Govt may revise)
If Conciliation Fails :: **CANNOT adjudicate on merits** — closes proceedings and returns pending matter to regular court | **CAN ADJUDICATE ON MERITS** by majority vote (`Section 22C(8)`), provided dispute does not relate to an offence!
Bar on Parallel Suits :: Parties remain free to litigate if compromise fails | Once ANY party invokes the PLA, **neither party can approach any regular court** for that dispute (`Sec. 22C(2)`)
Finality & Appeal :: Award = **final, binding civil court decree**; **NO APPEAL** (only narrow `Art. 226/227` writ for fraud/coercion) | Award (whether by settlement or merits adjudication) = **final, binding civil court decree**; **NO regular appeal** (only narrow `Art. 226/227` writ)
```

### Family Courts and Gram Nyayalayas

Beyond Lok Adalats, Parliament established **two specialised statutory grassroots fora** designed to remove socially sensitive matrimonial disputes and everyday rural disputes from formal, adversarial courtrooms.

#### Family Courts (Family Courts Act, 1984)

Acting on the **59th Law Commission Report (1974)** and sustained advocacy by the Committee on the Status of Women in India (*Towards Equality*, 1974), Parliament enacted the **Family Courts Act, 1984** to promote conciliation and secure speedy settlement of marriage and family affairs without the acrimony of ordinary civil procedure. Its defining statutory features include:
- **Mandatory Population Threshold:** Every State Government, in consultation with the High Court, **SHALL establish** a Family Court in every city or town whose population **exceeds one million (10 lakh)**, and **MAY** establish them in smaller towns and districts (over 800+ Family Courts now operate across India).
- **Exclusive Matrimonial & Family Jurisdiction:** Strips ordinary district/civil courts of jurisdiction over suits for nullity of marriage, judicial separation, divorce, restitution of conjugal rights, validity of marriage, matrimonial property disputes between spouses, legitimacy of children, maintenance (`Sec. 144 BNSS` / erstwhile `Sec. 125 CrPC`), and guardianship/custody of minors.
- **Conciliation-First Mandate & Support Ecosystem:** Imposes an explicit legal duty on the Family Court Judge to make every effort first to assist and persuade parties toward reconciliation—supported by **marriage counsellors**, medical/welfare experts, and recognised social welfare organisations.
- **Informal Procedure and Restricted Lawyer Entry (`Section 13`):** Family Courts may hold proceedings ***in camera*** (behind closed doors) to protect family privacy, are not strictly bound by technical rules of the Evidence Act/BSA (recording only a memorandum of substance of oral evidence), and crucially under **`Section 13`**, **NO party to a Family Court proceeding is entitled as of right to be represented by a legal practitioner** (though the court may permit counsel or appoint a legal expert as *amicus curiae* where justice requires!).
- **Single Appeal Rule:** Provides **only ONE right of appeal**—to a **two-judge Bench of the High Court within 30 days**—with **NO appeal whatsoever** permitted against an interlocutory order or a **consent decree**!

#### Gram Nyayalayas (Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008)

Conceived in the **114th Law Commission Report (1986)** to fulfil `Article 39A` for rural India, the **Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008** (assented 7 January 2009; enforced **2 October 2009**, Gandhi Jayanti) created statutory **mobile village courts** aimed at taking affordable justice literally to the doorstep of rural citizens.

- **Institutional Placement and Mobile Character:** Established by **State Governments in consultation with the High Court** for every **Panchayat at the intermediate level** (block/mandal panchayat) or a group of contiguous intermediate panchayats. Though headquartered at the intermediate panchayat, the Gram Nyayalaya is statutorily designed to function as a **mobile court**, travelling periodically to surrounding villages to conduct hearings in the local language.
- **The Presiding Officer — Nyayadhikari (Classic Prelims Trap!):** Unlike traditional informal *Nyaya Panchayats* run by elected village elders, every Gram Nyayalaya is presided over by a **Nyayadhikari**—a **regular, qualified judicial officer of the rank of Judicial Magistrate of the First Class (JMFC)** appointed by the State Government in consultation with the High Court, enjoying the exact same salary, perks, and statutory protections as a JMFC!
- **Dual Civil and Criminal Jurisdiction:** Exercises **BOTH civil and criminal jurisdiction** over offences and disputes enumerated in the First and Second Schedules (such as petty criminal offences punishable up to 2 years, theft of modest value, property/pasture/water-channel disputes, minimum wages, payment of wages, protection of women from domestic violence, and rural tenancy matters). Notably, Parliament AND State Governments may amend the Schedules (within their legislative competence).
- **Summary Procedure, Plea Bargaining and Natural Justice:** Criminal trials follow **summary procedure** and actively encourage **plea bargaining**; civil disputes prioritise mandatory conciliation through a panel of **Conciliators** (local social workers). Guided by **principles of natural justice** (NOT strictly bound by rules of evidence under the BSA/Evidence Act), subject to any rule made by the High Court!
- **Swift Single-Tier Appeals:** Criminal appeals lie to the **Sessions Court** and civil appeals lie to the **District Court** within **30 days**, with a statutory mandate that the appellate court dispose of the appeal within **six months** (and no further second appeal lies!).

> [!example]
> **Two Faces of Grassroots ADR in 2026: National Lok Adalat Scale vs Gram Nyayalaya Stagnation**
> - **The Success Story (National Lok Adalats):** NALSA's quarterly National Lok Adalats achieve unmatched dispute-resolution volume—disposing of **2.84 crore cases** (March 2026), **2.07 crore** (May 2026), and **2.10 crore cases** settling claims worth **₹13,589 crore** in a single day on **12 September 2026** (predominantly pre-litigation bank, utility, traffic, and motor-accident matters).
> - **The Implementation Deficit (Gram Nyayalayas):** Against the 2008 Act's original target of **5,000+ rural courts**, official Department of Justice data (**2025–2026**) reveals a sobering gap: only **489 Gram Nyayalayas have been notified** across 15 States/UTs and merely **338 are actually operational** (333 across 11 States as of October 2025, concentrated overwhelmingly in **Uttar Pradesh [109]** and **Madhya Pradesh [89]**, with **zero operational** even in notifying States/UTs like Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, J&K, and Ladakh!). State reluctance, jurisdictional overlap with existing taluk JMFC courts, Bar resistance, shortage of dedicated Nyayadhikaris, and meagre Central funding (₹2 crore in FY 2025–26) continue to stall rural doorstep justice.

### Commercial Arbitration and the Mediation Act, 2023

While Lok Adalats and Gram Nyayalayas serve grassroots citizens, India's aspiration to become a **$10-trillion global commercial hub** depends on predictable, internationally trusted **Arbitration** and **Mediation** institutions.

#### Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (Amendments 2015, 2019, 2021 and 2023–2026 Developments)

Modeled on the **UNCITRAL Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration (1985)**, the **Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996** provides a unified statutory regime for **(a)** domestic arbitration (`Part I`), **(b)** enforcement of foreign arbitral awards under the **1958 New York Convention** and **1927 Geneva Convention** (`Part II`), and **(c)** conciliation (`Part III` — substantially aligned/shifted following the Mediation Act, 2023).

Historically, Indian arbitration suffered from two pathologies: overwhelming reliance on **ad-hoc arbitration** (costly retired-judge tribunals sitting sporadically without institutional schedules, contrasting sharply with **institutional arbitration** centres like SIAC in Singapore or LCIA in London) and excessive **judicial interference** under `Section 34` (where courts routinely reopened arbitral awards on merits under an expansive "public policy" exception). Three successive parliamentary amendments sought to cure these ills:
- **2015 Amendment Act:** Imposed a strict **12-month time limit** for completing domestic arbitral proceedings (extendable by 6 months with party consent, thereafter only by court order); introduced a **6-month fast-track procedure**; codified strict **arbitrator impartiality and independence disclosures** via the **Fifth and Seventh Schedules**; prescribed a model fee schedule (**Fourth Schedule**); and sharply narrowed **"public policy of India"** under `Section 34` (limiting interference to fraud/corruption, violation of fundamental policy of Indian law, or basic notions of morality/justice, plus "patent illegality" strictly for domestic awards).
- **2019 Amendment Act & India International Arbitration Centre (IIAC) Act, 2019:** Acting on the **High-Level Committee chaired by Justice B.N. Srikrishna (July 2017)**, Parliament inserted **Part IA** to establish the seven-member **Arbitration Council of India (ACI)** (to grade arbitral institutions, accredit arbitrators, and maintain an electronic depository of awards), empowered the Supreme Court and High Courts to designate arbitral institutions for `Section 11` arbitrator appointments, mandated **confidentiality** of arbitral proceedings, and enacted the **IIAC Act, 2019** creating the **India International Arbitration Centre (IIAC)** in New Delhi (taking over the old ICADR) as an autonomous institution of national importance.
- **2021 Amendment Act:** Empowered courts to grant an **unconditional stay** on the enforcement of an arbitral award where a *prima facie* case exists that the underlying arbitration agreement, contract, or award was induced or effected by **fraud or corruption**; and **omitted the restrictive Eighth Schedule**, opening India's arbitral market to qualified international arbitrators.

> [!case]
> **Two Landmark 5-Judge Constitution Bench Arbitration Verdicts (2023–2025)**
> 1. ***Cox and Kings Ltd. v. SAP India Pvt. Ltd.* (6 December 2023, 5-Judge Bench):** Unanimously upheld the **"Group of Companies" doctrine** in Indian arbitration law, holding that a **non-signatory corporate affiliate** within a tightly integrated corporate group can be bound by an arbitration agreement where conduct and transactional structure establish common mutual intent to bind it.
> 2. ***Gayatri Balasamy v. ISG Novasoft Technologies Ltd.* (30 April 2025, `2025 INSC 605`, 5-Judge Bench, 4:1 Majority):** Ending years of conflicting precedents over whether courts under `Sections 34 and 37` can only *set aside* an award (forcing parties back into years of fresh arbitration) or *modify* it, **CJI Sanjiv Khanna** (writing for Justices B.R. Gavai, Sanjay Kumar, and Augustine George Masih; **Justice K.V. Viswanathan dissenting** on grounds of party autonomy and New York Convention norms) held that courts possess **strictly limited power to modify arbitral awards**—without re-appreciating merits—in **only four circumstances**:
>    - **(1) Severing** an independent invalid portion from the valid part (*omne majus continet in se minus* — the greater power to set aside includes the lesser power to partially sever);
>    - **(2) Correcting patent clerical, computational, or typographical errors** apparent on the face of the record;
>    - **(3) Modifying post-award interest** where statutory or equitable grounds plainly warrant adjustment; and
>    - **(4) Cautious exercise of `Article 142`** by the Supreme Court to do complete justice and end prolonged litigation *(applied notably in ***Bhupesh Bhayana v. Kunal Seth*, `2026 INSC 546`** to close a 14-year redevelopment dispute without forcing a fresh tribunal!)*.

#### Mediation Act, 2023 and the Operationalisation of the Mediation Council of India (2026)

Receiving Presidential assent on **14 September 2023** (published in the Gazette on 15 September 2023), the **Mediation Act, 2023** is India's **first standalone central statute** devoted comprehensively to mediation, unifying court-annexed, institutional, private, online, and community mediation under one coherent framework:
1. **Voluntary Pre-Litigation Mediation (With Commercial Courts Exception):** Parties may **voluntarily** attempt mediation before filing any civil or commercial suit (`Section 5`). Although Parliament wisely dropped the 2021 Bill's blanket *mandatory* pre-litigation mediation rule (recognising acute mediator shortages nationwide), the Act explicitly **preserves mandatory pre-institution mediation for non-urgent commercial suits under `Section 12A` of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015**!
2. **Strict 120 + 60 Day Statutory Timeline (`Section 18`):** Mediation must be completed within **120 days** from the date fixed for the first appearance before the mediator, extendable by a maximum of **60 additional days** solely by mutual written consent of the parties (hard ceiling of **180 days**).
3. **First Schedule — Disputes Excluded From Mediation (`Section 6`):** Lists non-mediable domains including criminal prosecutions (save compoundable/specified referrals), taxation/levy disputes, serious allegations of fraud/forgery/coercion, disputes involving claims against minors or persons of unsound mind, matters affecting third-party rights not participating in mediation, proceedings before statutory regulators (SEBI, CCI, TRAI, NGT), and land acquisition proceedings.
4. **Enforceable Mediated Settlement Agreements (`Sections 27–28`):** Once signed by parties and authenticated by the mediator, a **Mediated Settlement Agreement (MSA)** is **final, binding, and enforceable in the exact same manner as a judgment and decree passed by a civil court**! It can be challenged within **90 days** (+90 days condonation for sufficient cause) on **ONLY four narrow grounds**: **(i) fraud, (ii) corruption, (iii) impersonation, or (iv) that the subject matter was non-mediable under `Section 6`**.
5. **Online Mediation (`Section 30`) and Confidentiality (`Section 22`):** Expressly legitimises **Online Dispute Resolution (ODR)** via secure electronic/video channels with written party consent, backed by strict confidentiality barring mediators and parties from disclosing settlement admissions in subsequent court trials.
6. **Community Mediation (`Section 43`):** Empowers local authorities (DLSAs/SDMs/collectors) to constitute a **three-member panel** of respected local citizens, RWA representatives, or community elders to resolve neighbourhood/locality disputes threatening peace, harmony, and tranquillity *(note the Prelims nuance: community mediation settlements are purely restorative social agreements and are **NOT enforceable as court decrees**!)*.
7. **Mediation Council of India (MCI — `Section 31`):** Establishes a statutory **seven-member national regulator** headquartered in Delhi to register mediators, accredit Mediation Service Providers and Mediation Institutes, lay down professional codes, and promote domestic and international institutional mediation.

> [!news]
> **2026 Implementation Reality Check: Mediation Council Live, Yet ACI, Draft Arbitration Bill & Singapore Convention Lag**
> - **Mediation Council of India (MCI) Operational (Aug–Oct 2026):** The Union Law Ministry formally notified the establishment of the **MCI** under `Section 31(1)` on **27 August 2026** (headquartered in Delhi), and the Council **commenced operations on 19 October 2026** at the Indian Law Institute, New Delhi, under Chairperson **Dr. Rajiv Mani** to draft regulations and accredit mediation providers. However, as of **November 2026**, the **Mediation Act, 2023 remains only partially commenced** (institutional provisions notified, while key substantive chapters—including pre-litigation mediation procedure and `Section 27` decree enforcement—await phased Gazette notification).
> - **Singapore Convention on Mediation Still Unratified:** While India **signed** the **UN Singapore Convention on Mediation** (enabling cross-border enforcement of international commercial MSAs on par with the New York Convention for arbitration) on **7 August 2019**, India has **still NOT ratified it as of November 2026**, and the 2023 Act does not cover cross-border enforcement of foreign-seated MSAs!
> - **Arbitration Council of India (ACI) Unconstituted & Draft 2024 Bill Pending:** Despite being enacted in 2019, the **ACI remains unconstituted as of November 2026**—an implementation gap admitted in Parliament (Feb 2026) and criticised by **CJI Surya Kant** in July and September 2026 as a *"credibility deficit"* driving Indian corporations abroad (Indians remain the third-largest foreign users of **SIAC**). Likewise, the **Draft Arbitration and Conciliation (Amendment) Bill, 2024** (released **18 October 2024** following the **Dr. T.K. Viswanathan Committee** report—proposing statutory **emergency arbitrators [`Sec. 9A`]**, opt-in **appellate arbitral tribunals [`Sec. 34A`]**, ODR recognition, institutional empowerment, and renaming the statute the *Arbitration Act*) **remains a consultation draft not yet introduced in Parliament**.

## 1. High Courts: Structure, Judges and Independence

- **Origin & Count (`Part VI, Arts. 214–231`):** Integrated judicial pyramid (GoI Act 1935). **Indian High Courts Act, 1861** created **Calcutta (oldest), Bombay & Madras (1862)**; **Allahabad (1866)**. **25 HCs** today (Nov 2026; **Andhra Pradesh** [Amaravati] & **Telangana** [Hyderabad] split **1 Jan 2019**; Manipur, Meghalaya, Tripura 2013).
- **Common HCs & UTs (`Arts. 230, 231, 241`):** **`7th Amend (1956)`** (`Art. 231`) empowers **Parliament by ordinary law** to create common HCs. **7 multi-jurisdiction HCs:** **Gauhati** (Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram, Arunachal), **Bombay** (MH, Goa, DNH&DD), **Punjab & Haryana** (+ Chandigarh), **Calcutta** (+ A&N), **Madras** (+ Puducherry), **Kerala** (+ Lakshadweep), **J&K and Ladakh** (2 UTs). Only **Delhi (1966)** & **J&K-Ladakh** among UTs have own HCs.
- **Strength (`Art. 216`):** Fixed by **President** (**NOT Parliament**—contrast SC `Art. 124` = 38 in 2026). **DoJ (1 Nov 2026):** **1,122 sanctioned** (847 permanent + 275 additional), **827 working**, **295 vacant (26.3%)**; Allahabad (160) largest, Sikkim (3) smallest, Meghalaya (0 vacant).
- **Appointment (`Art. 217(1)`):** **President** consulting CJI, Governor(s) & HC CJ. **SC Collegium for HCs (*Third Judges Case*, 1998):** **CJI + 2 senior-most SC Judges** (+ conversant SC judges; initiated by HC CJ + 2 HC Judges); HC CJ conventionally an outsider.
- **Qualifications (`Art. 217(2)`):** **Citizen** + **10 yrs judicial office** OR **10 yrs HC advocate**. **NO "distinguished jurist"** (deleted by **`44th Amend, 1978`**) & **NO minimum age**.
- **Oath, Tenure, Resignation & Removal:** Oath by **Governor** (`Art. 219`, 3rd Schedule). Retires at **62** (**`15th Amend, 1963`**); age dispute decided by **President + CJI — final (`Art. 217(3)`)** (SC `Art. 124(2A)` = Parliament law). Resigns to **President**. Removed like SC Judge (`Art. 124(4)` & *Judges Inquiry Act 1968*: proved misbehaviour/incapacity; 100 LS / 50 RS MPs; 3-member panel; special majority in both Houses, same session + Presidential order; **Governor has ZERO role**; none removed—Soumitra Sen & Dinakaran resigned 2011).
- **Transfer (`Art. 222`):** President consulting **CJI + 4 senior-most SC Judges + BOTH HC CJs** (*Third Judges Case*, 1998); public interest only (*Sankalchand Sheth*, 1977); review only by transferred judge for non-consultation.
- **Temporary Judges (`Arts. 223–224A`):** **President** appoints Acting CJ (`223`), **Additional (`224(1)` — max 2 yrs, <=62)** & Acting (`224(2)` — <=62). **Retired (`224A`, 15th Amend):** Requested by **HC CJ with President's prior consent** & judge's consent; full powers yet **NOT deemed HC Judge**. ***Lok Prahari* (30 Jan 2025, CJI Khanna):** Kept >20% vacancy floor in abeyance; HCs may recommend **2–5 ad-hoc retired judges** (max **10%** strength) on Division Benches for **criminal appeals**.
- **Independence Traps:** Salaries (**₹2.5L CJ / ₹2.25L Judge**, fixed by Parliament; cut only in `Art. 360`) & expenses charged on **State CF (`Art. 202(3)(d)`)**, **BUT PENSIONS charged on CFI (`Art. 112(3)(d)(iii)`)**! Non-discussable conduct (`Arts. 121, 211`); own staff (`Art. 229`). **Practice bar (`Art. 220`, 7th Amend):** Retired **permanent HC judge** may practise **ONLY in SC & OTHER HCs** (SC `Art. 124(7)` = total bar).

## 2. High Court Jurisdiction and Comparison with the Supreme Court

- **Original (`Art. 225`):** Preserved pre-1950 powers & lifted colonial **revenue** bar. Covers admiralty, probate, marriage, divorce, company, contempt, **MP/MLA election petitions** (`Sec. 80A RPA 1951` — President/VP go to SC `Art. 71`), revenue, FRs, `Art. 228` transfers, and high-value **original civil suits** in **Calcutta, Bombay, Madras & Delhi** HCs (original criminal jurisdiction ended by CrPC 1973 / BNSS 2023).
- **Writ Jurisdiction (`Art. 226` vs `Art. 32`):** Five writs (*Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto*). **Substantively WIDER than `Art. 32`** (FRs **+ "any other purpose"** = legal rights), yet **discretionary** (not a FR). **`Art. 226(2)` (`15th Amend`)** reaches outside territory if **cause of action** arises wholly/partly. **`Art. 226(3)` (`44th Amend`)**: *ex parte* stay lapses if vacation plea undecided in **14 days**. **`Arts. 226/227` = Basic Structure** (*L. Chandra Kumar*, 1997).
- **Appellate & Death Confirmation:** Civil first/second/Letters Patent appeals & tribunal reviews; criminal appeals (>7 yrs) + **mandatory HC confirmation of EVERY Sessions death sentence** even without appeal (`Sec. 407 BNSS 2023`).
- **Superintendence (`Art. 227`), Control (`Art. 235`), Record (`Art. 215`) & Review:** **`Art. 227`** = **judicial + administrative** superintendence over all courts/tribunals **EXCEPT Armed Forces (`Art. 227(4)`)**; ***suo motu***; wider than *certiorari*. **`Art. 235`**: exclusive subordinate control. **`Art. 228`**: withdraws constitutional questions. **`Art. 215`**: evidentiary value + contempt (*1971 Act*; truth defence 2006) + inherent self-review (*Shivdeo Singh*, 1963). Judicial review over **BOTH Central & State laws** (`42nd Amend` bar `Art. 226A` **repealed by `43rd Amend, 1977`**). **NO Advisory Jurisdiction** (unlike SC `Art. 143`). HCs are independent constitutional courts—**NOT administratively subordinate** to SC (*Tirupati Balaji*, 2004).

## 3. Subordinate Courts and Judicial Reforms

- **Constitutional Structure (`Arts. 233–237`):** Handles >**88%** of pending cases. **`Art. 233`**: **District Judges** appointed/promoted by **Governor + binding HC consultation** (*Chandra Mohan*, 1966; `20th Amend` added `Art. 233A`; **NO SPSC**); `Art. 233(2)` requires **>=7 yrs** experience + HC recommendation. **`Art. 234`**: Other judges by **Governor + SPSC + HC**. **`Art. 235`**: Exclusive HC control operationalising **`Art. 50`**. **`Art. 236`**: Definitions. **`Art. 237`**: Extension to magistrates.
- **2025 SC District Judiciary Landmarks:**
  - ***Rejanish K.V. v. K. Deepa* (9 Oct 2025, 5 JJ, CJI Gavai, `2025 INSC 1208`):** Overruled *Dheeraj Mor* (2020); serving judicial officers with **7 yrs combined Bar + judicial experience** (continuous at application; **min age 35**; prospective) eligible for direct District Judge recruitment under `Art. 233`.
  - ***AIJA* (20 May 2025, CJI Gavai):** Restored **min 3 yrs Bar practice** (from provisional enrolment; clerkship counts) prospectively for **Civil Judge (Jr. Div.)**; HJS LDCE raised to **25%** + **10%** LDCE for Civil Judge (Sr. Div.).
- **Three-Tier Ladder (`Sec. 23, BNSS 2023`, w.e.f. 1 Jul 2024):** Same officer heads **District Judge** (unlimited civil) & **Sessions Judge** (any sentence; death needs HC confirmation) → **Sub-Judge / Civil Judge (Sr. Div.)** & **CJM** (**<=7 yrs**) → **Munsiff / Civil Judge (Jr. Div.)** & **JMFC** (**<=3 yrs**, fine **₹50,000**, or **community service**) / **JMSC** (**<=1 yr**, **₹10,000**, or **community service**). **BNSS abolished Metropolitan Magistrate & Assistant Sessions Judge cadres.** Parallel Revenue Courts (Tehsildar → SDO → Collector → Commissioner → Board of Revenue).
- **Pendency & Diagnostics (2025–2026):** **~5.85 cr+ pending** (NJDG 3 Nov 2026: **5.19 cr District**, **65.58L HCs** [10.48L civil >10 yrs], **92,755–95,658 SC**). Vacancies: **26.3% HC** (295/1,122; ~7,930 cases/judge) & **23.7% District** (7,310/30,868, Aug 2026). **~22 judges/million** vs 120th Law Comm.'s **50** (245th Report & NCMS favour **weighted disposal**). **72.5%–72.6% undertrials** (NCRB 2024, rel. 2026); **₹182/capita** judiciary spend (**no State >1%**, IJR 2025). Root causes: vacancies (*Malik Mazhar Sultan*, 2007), **govt ~46–50% litigant** (LIMBS 7.27L Union cases Mar 2025), adjournments, ~0.1% GDP budget, `Sec. 138 NI Act` cheque-bounce/traffic flooding.
- **AIJS (`Art. 312`, `42nd Amend`):** Needs **RS 2/3rd present & voting resolution** + ordinary Act (**NOT `Art. 368`** — `Art. 312(4)`); **`Art. 312(3)` bars posts below District Judge**. Pros: merit, speed, SC/ST/OBC/women diversity; Cons: regional language, federalism, `Art. 235` erosion, civil-judge promotion loss.
- **e-Courts Phase III & Reforms:** **₹7,210 cr (2023–27)**; by **Mar 2026**: **660.36 cr pages digitised**, **3.97+ cr VC hearings**, **2,444 e-Sewa Kendras**; **FASTER**, **SUVAS**, **SUPACE**, Virtual Courts, live-streaming (*Swapnil Tripathi*, 2018), Tele-Law & Nyaya Bandhu. **779 FTSCs** (**399 e-POCSO**) disposed **3.97L cases** (Jun 2026). **Commercial Courts** (₹3L limit; mandatory `Sec. 12A` mediation). **4 Apr 2025 Union Litigation Directive** replaced Cabinet NLP. **SC Regional Benches (`Art. 130`):** 229th Law Comm. (2009) Delhi Constitutional Court + 4 Cassation Benches (opposed by SC Full Court).

## 4. Tribunals and Quasi-Judicial Bodies

- **Part XIV-A (`Arts. 323A–323B`):** Not original; added by **`42nd Amend (1976)`** (Swaran Singh panel). Quasi-judicial bodies apply **natural justice** (*audi alteram partem*, *nemo judex in causa sua*, reasoned orders) without strict CPC/BSA; oldest tribunal = **ITAT (1941)**.
- **`323A` vs `323B`:** **`323A`** = **public services ONLY**, **Parliament ONLY** (Centre creates SATs/JATs on State request under *1985 Act*), **NO hierarchy**. **`323B`** = **9 matters** (tax, foreign exchange, labour, land reforms, urban ceiling, **MP/MLA elections**, foodstuffs, **rent/tenancy** [`75th Amend 1994`], connected offences), **Parliament & States**, **hierarchy allowed**.
- **CAT Exclusions:** Covers AIS, Central civil & civilian defence staff; **EXCLUDES** **(1) armed forces** (AFT 2007), **(2) SC staff**, **(3) Parliament secretariat**.
- **Jurisprudence Arc:** *Sampath Kumar* (1987, substitutes) → ***L. Chandra Kumar* (1997, 7 JJ: `Arts. 226/227` & `32` = Basic Structure; supplemental; appeals first to HC Division Bench)** → *R. Gandhi* (2010, NCLT) → *MBA* (2014, NTT void) → ***Rojer Mathew* (2019: Finance Act 2017 rules void; NTC urged; Money Bill to 7 JJ)** → *MBA-4/5* (2020–21; *2021 Act* abolished 9 tribunals incl. **IPAB/FCAT** yet kept 4-yr term, 50-yr bar, 2-name panel) → ***MBA-6* (19 Nov 2025, `2025 INSC 1330`, CJI Gavai & Chandran J.):** Voided 2021 Act clauses ("legislative override"); ordered NTC in 4 months.
- **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026 (`Act 17 of 2026`, assent 13 Aug, in force 25 Aug 2026):** Repeals 2021 Act; **5-member judiciary-led NTC** (Delhi: Chair + 2 Judicial + 2 Technical, 5 yrs/age 70) over **16 tribunals**; restores **5-yr term** (**70/67** caps), **drops 50-yr bar**, **single-name SCSC (+1 waitlist)**, **3-month** limit, **National Tribunals Data Grid**. **Gaps:** CJI *"consultation"* (not concurrence), **GSTAT & NCLT excluded** (GSTAT stays under `Sec. 110 CGST`!), direct SC appeals persist (272nd Law Comm.).
- **NGT (`2010 Act`) & GSTAT:** NGT = 3rd nation (Australia, NZ); Delhi + 4 Zonal Benches (**Bhopal, Pune, Kolkata, Chennai**); **6-month** disposal; **90-day SC appeal**; `Sec. 20` (**Sustainable Development, Precautionary, Polluter Pays**); ***suo motu*** (*Ankita Sinha*, 2021). **7 Acts:** Water 1974, Water Cess 1977 *(repealed 2017)*, Forest Conservation 1980, Air 1981, EPA 1986, PLI 1991, Biodiversity 2002. **EXCLUDES:** **Wildlife Act 1972, Indian Forest Act 1927, Forest Rights Act 2006!** **GSTAT:** Launched **24 Sept 2025** (Justice Sanjaya Kumar Mishra); hearings **16 Feb 2026**; Principal Bench Delhi (also **NAAAR**, Apr 2026 → SC) + **31 State Benches / 45 locations** (→ HC).

## 5. Alternative Dispute Resolution and Legal Aid

- **Anchors:** **`Art. 39A`** (`42nd Amend`), `Art. 21` (*Hoskot* 1978, *Hussainara Khatoon* 1979), **`Sec. 89 CPC`** (2002; *Afcons* 2010).
- **NALSA (`LSA Act, 1987`, in force 9 Nov 1995):** **CJI** Patron-in-Chief; **2nd senior-most SC Judge** Executive Chair; NALSA→SLSA→DLSA→TLSC; LADCS & helpline **15100**. **8 `Sec. 12` categories:** SC/ST, trafficking/*begar*, **ALL women & children**, PwD, mass disaster/atrocity victims, industrial workmen, custody inmates *(all 7 NO income test!)*, + income cap (**₹5L SC**; ₹1L–₹3L States).
- **Lok Adalat vs Permanent Lok Adalat:** Regular (`Secs. 19–22`; Una 1982; statutory 1987): pending + pre-litigation civil & **compoundable criminal ONLY**; zero fee (refunded); **compromise-only**; **final civil decree, NO APPEAL** (narrow `Art. 226/227` fraud writ — *Jalour Singh*, 2008); National Lok Adalats settled **2+ cr cases/sitting in 2026**. **PLA (`2002 Amend, Secs. 22A–22E`):** Pre-litigation **Public Utility Services ONLY** up to **₹1 crore** (Mar 2015); **CAN adjudicate on merits (`Sec. 22C(8)`)** if conciliation fails (zero criminal scope); bars court suits.
- **Family Courts (`1984`) & Gram Nyayalayas (`2008`, in force 2 Oct 2009):** **Family Courts:** Mandatory in cities **>10 lakh**; conciliation-first; **no lawyer as of right (`Sec. 13`)**; **30-day appeal to 2-Judge HC Bench** (none on consent decree). **Gram Nyayalayas** (114th Law Comm.): Mobile rural intermediate-panchayat courts; **Nyayadhikari = JMFC-rank judge**; civil + criminal summary & plea bargaining; natural justice; **30-day** District/Sessions appeal (6-month disposal); **489 notified, 338 operational** (UP 109, MP 89) vs 5,000+ target.
- **Arbitration (`1996`; 2015/19/21 Amends):** UNCITRAL; 12+6 month cap & 5th/7th Schedule neutrality (2015); **ACI & IIAC** (2019); fraud stay & 8th Schedule dropped (2021). ***Cox & Kings* (Dec 2023):** **Group of Companies** doctrine. ***Gayatri Balasamy* (30 Apr 2025, `2025 INSC 605`, 4:1 — CJI Khanna; Viswanathan J. dissenting):** Limited `Sec. 34/37` **modification** in **4 cases** (severing invalid part, patent clerical/computational error, post-award interest, cautious `Art. 142`; applied in *Bhupesh Bhayana*, 2026). **2026 Gaps:** **ACI unconstituted**; **Draft Arbitration Bill 2024** (Oct 2024, Viswanathan panel: emergency arbitrators `Sec. 9A`, appellate tribunal `Sec. 34A`) pending.
- **Mediation Act, 2023 (14 Sept 2023) & MCI (2026):** **Voluntary** pre-litigation (**mandatory kept under `Sec. 12A` Commercial Courts Act**); **120+60 days**; **MSA = civil decree** (`Sec. 27`; 90-day challenge on fraud, corruption, impersonation, non-mediable); **ODR** (`Sec. 30`); **community mediation** (`Sec. 43`, 3-member panel, non-decree). **MCI** notified **27 Aug 2026** (Delhi), **operational 19 Oct 2026** (**Dr. Rajiv Mani**); substantive chapters await notification; **UN Singapore Convention** signed 7 Aug 2019, **still unratified** (Nov 2026).
