## 1. Roots of rural self-government and the Constituent Assembly debate

Village self-rule in India blends indigenous tradition with colonial administration and constitutional modernism. Beyond Vedic *sabhas* and *samitis*, rural assemblies flourished under the **imperial Cholas** in Tamil Nadu: peasant settlements operated through the ***ur***, merchant towns through the ***nagaram***, and Brahmin *brahmadeyas* through the ***sabha*** or *mahasabha*.

The **Uttaramerur inscriptions** (**919 CE and 921 CE**) on the Vaikuntha Perumal temple in Kanchipuram district, issued under **Parantaka I**, codify rules for Uttaramerur's thirty wards. Functional committees (*variyams*—including *eri variyam* for tanks, *thotta variyam* for gardens, and *samvatsara variyam* for annual oversight) were chosen yearly via ***kudavolai***: names of landowning, educated, 35–70-year-old candidates were placed on palm-leaf tickets in a sealed pot and drawn publicly by a boy. Account defaulters and their relatives were disqualified.

Under British rule, five milestones structured local governance: **Lord Mayo's Resolution (1870)** began financial decentralisation; **Lord Ripon's Resolution (1882)**—the **"Magna Carta of local self-government"** (making Ripon *"Father of Local Self-Government in India"*)—backed elected non-official majorities as schools of political education; the **Royal Commission on Decentralisation (1907, C.E.H. Hobhouse)** urged reviving village panchayats; the **Government of India Act, 1919** made local government a **"transferred" subject** under provincial Indian ministers; and the **Government of India Act, 1935** made it a provincial subject (now **Entry 5, State List**, Seventh Schedule).

### Gandhi versus Ambedkar in the Constituent Assembly

In the Constituent Assembly, **Mahatma Gandhi's** ideal of ***Gram Swaraj***—self-reliant village republics as the foundation of *Purna Swaraj*—collided with **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's** warning against rural caste hierarchies. Introducing the Draft Constitution on **4 November 1948**, Ambedkar answered colonial praise of "village republics" bluntly:

> "I hold that these village republics have been the ruination of India. What is the village but a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness and communalism? I am glad that the Draft Constitution has discarded the village and adopted the individual as its unit." — B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 4 November 1948

Responding to Gandhian objections, **K. Santhanam** moved a compromise on **22 November 1948**, accepted by Ambedkar, inserting **Article 40** into Part IV (**Directive Principles of State Policy**): the State shall organise village panchayats as units of self-government. Because DPSPs are **non-justiciable** (Article 37), rural democracy remained subject to State discretion for four decades.

> **Prelims pointer:** Four traps: (1) Chola ***kudavolai*** was **drawing lots** among qualified candidates in a *brahmadeya* (*sabha*), *not* universal adult voting; (2) **Mayo (1870)** = financial decentralisation, **Ripon (1882)** = "Magna Carta"; (3) **GoI Act, 1919** made local government a **"transferred" subject**; (4) Local government sits in **Entry 5, State List** and originally appeared only in non-justiciable **Article 40**.

## 2. Evolution of Panchayati Raj committees: Balwantrai Mehta to Gadgil

Post-Independence rural programmes—the **Community Development Programme (CDP, 1952)** and **National Extension Service (NES, 1953)**—stalled because top-down bureaucratic delivery lacked people's participation. Six landmark committees steered India from administrative experimentation to constitutional status.

First, the **Balwantrai Mehta Committee (1957)** coined **"democratic decentralisation"** and proposed a **three-tier** organically linked model led operationally by the block-level **Panchayat Samiti**. Following National Development Council endorsement (1958), Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru inaugurated Panchayati Raj at **Nagaur, Rajasthan on 2 October 1959**, followed by **Andhra Pradesh** in November 1959. When state bureaucracies and suspended elections weakened early panchayats, the Janata Government's **Ashok Mehta Committee (1977–78)** urged a **two-tier** system centred on an executive **Zila Parishad** and village-cluster **Mandal Panchayats**, proposing **official political party participation** and—for the first time—**constitutional recognition**. During the 1980s, **G.V.K. Rao (1985)** warned that state schemes had reduced PRIs to **"grass without roots"**; **L.M. Singhvi (1986)** sought a **new constitutional chapter** centring the **Gram Sabha**; and **P.K. Thungon (1988)** and **V.N. Gadgil (1988)** shaped the direct constitutional blueprint.

| Committee (Year) | Tier Model | Executive Hub | Signature Recommendations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Balwantrai Mehta** (1957) | **3-tier** (Village, Block, District) | **Panchayat Samiti** | Coined **"democratic decentralisation"**; Collector chairs advisory Zila Parishad; launched at **Nagaur (2 Oct 1959)** |
| **Ashok Mehta** (1977–78) | **2-tier** (Zila Parishad + **Mandal Panchayat**, 15k–20k pop.) | **Zila Parishad** | **Official party participation**, compulsory taxes, social audit, **Nyaya Panchayats**, PRI Minister, **constitutional status** |
| **G.V.K. Rao** (1985) | District-centred PRIs | **Zila Parishad** | Warned PRIs became **"grass without roots"**; proposed **District Development Commissioner (DDC)**; regular polls |
| **L.M. Singhvi** (1986) | Village-rooted PRIs | **Gram Sabha** + PRIs | **Constitutional recognition** (new chapter), Gram Sabha as direct democracy, Nyaya Panchayats, **judicial poll tribunals** |
| **P.K. Thungon** (1988) | **3-tier** | **Zila Parishad** | Constitutional status, **fixed 5-year term**, SC/ST/women quota, **SFC**, District Planning Committee |
| **V.N. Gadgil** (1988) | **3-tier** | Elected PRIs | Direct 73rd Amdt blueprint: **direct polls to all 3 tiers**, 5-yr term, SC/ST/women quota, **SFC** & **SEC** |

Rajiv Gandhi's **64th Amendment Bill (1989)** passed the Lok Sabha but fell in the Rajya Sabha over fears of bypassing States; V.P. Singh's 1990 bill lapsed upon dissolution. P.V. Narasimha Rao's revised bill (vetted by **Nathuram Mirdha's** Joint Select Committee) passed Parliament on **22–23 December 1992**, won Presidential assent on **20 April 1993** after 17-state ratification, and took effect on **24 April 1993**—marked since 2010 as **National Panchayati Raj Day**. Karnataka passed the first conforming Act (May 1993), and Madhya Pradesh held the first polls (1994).

> **Remember:** Separate the two Mehtas: **Balwantrai (1957)** = **3-tier**, **Panchayat Samiti** executive; **Ashok (1977)** = **2-tier** (**Mandal Panchayat**), **Zila Parishad** executive, party contestation. Pair **G.V.K. Rao (1985)** with **"grass without roots"** + **DDC**, and **L.M. Singhvi (1986)** with **Gram Sabha** + **constitutional chapter**.

## 3. The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 (Part IX and Eleventh Schedule)

The **73rd Amendment**—enacted alongside its urban twin, the **74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992** (Part IXA, Municipalities, covered in the companion note)—transformed India into a three-tier constitutional democracy by inserting **Part IX** (*"The Panchayats"*, **Articles 243 to 243O**) and the **Eleventh Schedule** (**29 functional items**, **Article 243G**). Together, the 73rd and 74th Amendments constitutionalised rural and urban local self-government across India.

```text
          THE 73RD AMENDMENT: PART IX STRUCTURE
       (Arts. 243–243O | Eleventh Schedule: 29 Items)

   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  ZILA PARISHAD (District)                           │
   │  Seats: Direct poll │ Chairperson: Indirect poll    │
   └─────────────────────────▲───────────────────────────┘
                             │
   ┌─────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────┐
   │  PANCHAYAT SAMITI (Intermediate / Block)            │
   │  Seats: Direct poll │ Chairperson: Indirect poll    │
   │  [Optional if State pop. <= 20 lakh — Art. 243B(2)] │
   └─────────────────────────▲───────────────────────────┘
                             │
   ┌─────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────┐
   │  GRAM PANCHAYAT (Village)                           │
   │  Seats: Direct poll │ Chairperson: State decides    │
   └─────────────────────────▲───────────────────────────┘
                             │ Accountable to
   ┌─────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────┐
   │  GRAM SABHA (Art. 243A) — ALL Registered Voters in  │
   │  Village Electoral Roll (Powers set by State Law)   │
   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Core constitutional mechanics (Articles 243 to 243O)

- **Gram Sabha (Arts. 243(b), 243A):** Comprises **all persons registered in the village electoral rolls**—*not* all adult residents. The Constitution does not list its powers; the **State Legislature** defines them.
- **Tiers, elections & voting (Arts. 243B, 243C):** Mandatory three-tier structure—except states with population **<= 20 lakh** (e.g., Goa, Sikkim) need **not** set up the intermediate tier (**Art. 243B(2)**). **All seats** across all tiers are directly elected; Intermediate and District Chairpersons are elected **indirectly**, while the **Village Chairperson's** mode is decided by the State. States may give upper-tier representation to lower-tier chairpersons and **MPs, MLAs, and MLCs** (Art. 243C(3)). Crucially, under **Article 243C(4)**, *all* members—whether directly elected or ex-officio—enjoy the **right to vote** in Panchayat meetings (unlike nominated municipal experts under Art. 243R).
- **Reservations & OBC Triple Test (Art. 243D):** Seats and Chairperson offices are reserved for **SCs and STs** in proportion to population (rotated across wards), and **>= 1/3rd** of total seats (including SC/ST women seats, Art. 243D(2)–(3)) and Chairperson posts at each level for **women**—voluntarily raised to **50 per cent** by **21 States and 2 UTs** (Bihar first, 2006). Two critical riders: (1) under **Article 243D(5)**, SC/ST reservation lapses with **Article 334** (**25 January 2030**, 104th Amendment), **whereas women's reservation has NO sunset clause**; (2) the **83rd Amendment Act, 2000** (**Art. 243M(3A)**) exempts **Arunachal Pradesh** from SC reservation.
  - **OBC reservation (Art. 243D(6))** is **voluntary**. Upholding it in ***Dr. K. Krishna Murthy* (11 May 2010)**, operationalising it in ***Vikas Kishanrao Gawali v. State of Maharashtra* (4 March 2021)**, and enforcing it in ***Suresh Mahajan v. State of Madhya Pradesh* (10 May 2022)**, the Supreme Court mandated a **Triple Test**: (1) a **dedicated commission** for empirical inquiry into local political backwardness; (2) **local-body-wise** quota specification from its data; and (3) aggregate **SC + ST + OBC reservation <= 50 per cent**. Polls **cannot be postponed** pending compliance—OBC seats must be notified as general seats until satisfied.
- **Tenure, age, SFC, SEC & judicial bar (Arts. 243E–243O):** Fixed **five-year term** (*"and no longer"*); re-poll within **six months** of dissolution, with a reconstituted body serving **only the remainder** (no election if remainder **< 6 months**). Minimum age is **21 years** (**Art. 243F**); disqualification disputes go to a State-designated authority. **Article 243G** uses permissive **"may"** for devolving the **29 Eleventh Schedule items**; **Article 243H** enables taxation/grants; **Article 243-I** mandates a **State Finance Commission (SFC)** appointed by the Governor every **five years** (report + Action Taken Memorandum tabled in Legislature); **Article 243J** governs audit. Under **Article 243K**, the **State Election Commissioner** is appointed by the **Governor**, service conditions cannot be altered adversely, and **removal is ONLY like a High Court Judge** (by the President—*not* the Governor); ***State of Goa v. Fouziya Imtiaz Sheikh* (12 March 2021)** barred serving bureaucrats from holding SEC charge. **Article 243O** bars courts in delimitation/seat allotment; elections can be challenged **only via election petition**.

### Compulsory versus voluntary provisions

| Feature | Compulsory (Mandatory) | Voluntary (State Discretion) |
|---|---|---|
| **Gram Sabha & Tiers** | **Gram Sabha** (Art. 243A); **3-tier** (2-tier only if pop. **<= 20 lakh**) | **Powers** of Gram Sabha; delimitation rules |
| **Elections** | **Direct poll** to all seats (3 tiers); **indirect** Intermediate & District Chairpersons | Mode of **Village Chairperson** poll; **MP/MLA/MLC** representation |
| **Age & Term** | Min age **21 years**; **5-year term**; re-poll within **6 months** | Disqualification authority; literacy/two-child norms |
| **Reservations** | Proportional **SC/ST** & **>= 1/3rd women** (seats + Chairpersons) | **OBC quota** (Triple Test); raising women's quota to **50%** |
| **Institutions & 3Fs** | **SEC** (Art. 243K) & **SFC** every 5 yrs (Art. 243-I) | Devolving **29 subjects** (Art. 243G *"may"*), taxes (Art. 243H) & audit |

### Exempted states and areas (Article 243M)

Part IX does **not** apply to: (1) **Nagaland, Meghalaya, and Mizoram**; (2) **Fifth Schedule** (Scheduled Areas) and **Sixth Schedule** (Tribal Areas); (3) **Hill Areas of Manipur** with District Councils; and (4) the **district-level** Panchayat in the **Darjeeling Gorkha Hill Council** area (West Bengal). Under **Article 243M(4)(b)**, **Parliament** may extend Part IX to Scheduled/Tribal Areas with modifications by **ordinary law** (explicitly **not** an Article 368 amendment)—providing the hook for PESA.

> **Prelims pointer:** Six high-frequency traps: (1) **Gram Sabha** = registered voters, powers set by **State law**; (2) Min age = **21**, not 25; (3) **Women's quota** has **no Art. 334 sunset** (unlike SC/ST); (4) Reconstituted panchayat serves **only the remainder** (no poll if < 6 months left); (5) **SEC** is appointed by **Governor** but removed like a **High Court Judge** (by President); (6) **Art. 243M(4)(b)** extension is an **ordinary law**, not an Art. 368 amendment.

## 4. PESA Act, 1996: tribal self-rule in Fifth Schedule areas

To safeguard tribal customs from uniform panchayat structures, Parliament acted on the **Bhuria Committee (1994–95, Dileep Singh Bhuria)** to enact **The Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA)** on **24 December 1996** under **Article 243M(4)(b)**. PESA covers **Fifth Schedule areas across 10 states**—**Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan, and Telangana**—spanning **45 fully and 63 partly covered districts**, **77,564 villages, and 22,040 panchayats** (Lok Sabha Standing Committee / PIB, 2025–26). It does **not** apply to Sixth Schedule states or non-Scheduled areas.

### Gram Sabha sovereignty and mandatory powers (Section 4)

PESA makes the **Gram Sabha**—defined at the **habitation or hamlet** level managing affairs by custom (**Section 4(b)**)—the sovereign anchor of tribal self-rule. State laws must respect customary law and vest mandatory powers in the Gram Sabha (and appropriate Panchayats):

- **Governance & funds (Sec. 4(d)–(f)):** Safeguard customs, cultural identity, community resources, and customary dispute resolution; **approve development plans** and beneficiaries; issue **utilisation certificates** to the Village Panchayat.
- **Land acquisition (Sec. 4(i)):** **Mandatory consultation** before land acquisition or resettlement in Scheduled Areas (strengthened to **prior consent** under **Section 41 of the LARR Act, 2013**).
- **Minor minerals (Sec. 4(k)–(l)):** **Prior recommendation** mandatory for prospecting licences, mining leases, or auction concessions—strictly for **minor minerals** (excluding major minerals like coal or iron ore).
- **Resources & social control (Sec. 4(j), 4(m)):** (1) **Ownership of Minor Forest Produce (MFP)** (bamboo, tendu, mahua); (2) **prevent tribal land alienation** and **restore unlawfully alienated land**; (3) regulate/prohibit **intoxicants**; (4) manage **village markets**; (5) control **moneylending** to STs; (6) oversee social-sector institutions/functionaries and Tribal Sub-Plans; and (7) manage **minor water bodies**.
- **Tribal quotas (Sec. 4(g)–(h), 4(o)):** ST seat reservation **>= 50 per cent**, and **ALL Chairperson posts at ALL levels** reserved for **Scheduled Tribes**; states may nominate unrepresented STs at intermediate/district tiers up to **1/10th** of elected members and model district panchayats along **Sixth Schedule** lines.

### Landmark rulings, ground reality and 2025–2026 status

Two Supreme Court judgments reinforced tribal self-rule: ***Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (11 July 1997)** barred transferring or leasing tribal land in Scheduled Areas to non-tribals and private mining companies; and ***Orissa Mining Corporation Ltd. v. MoEF* (Niyamgiri case, 18 April 2013)** read **PESA** with the **Forest Rights Act (FRA), 2006** to empower **12 Dongria Kondh Gram Sabhas**, whose unanimous vote rejected bauxite mining and halted Stage-II clearance. Similarly, **Mendha-Lekha** village (Gadchiroli, Maharashtra) used PESA and FRA to assert collective bamboo and tendu ownership.

> **In the news:** **PESA Rules Reach 9 of 10 States (January–August 2026):** On **2 January 2026**, **Jharkhand** notified its PESA Rules (13 full and 3 partial districts, 16,022 villages, and 2,074 panchayats, after a September 2025 High Court freeze on minor-mineral auctions). Per the Ministry of Panchayati Raj's **Lok Sabha reply (4 August 2026)**, **9 of 10 PESA states** have notified rules; **Odisha** remains the **sole pending state** (draft rules framed).

Yet gaps persist: states rarely amend conflicting Forest, Excise, Land Revenue, and Minor Mineral Acts; *"consultation"* falls short of veto *"consent"* outside LARR; major minerals stay excluded; and hamlet Gram Sabhas face severe capacity shortages.

> **Prelims pointer:** Five PESA checks: (1) **Fifth Schedule** areas in **10 states** only (not Sixth Schedule); (2) **Bhuria Committee (1995)**, enacted **24 Dec 1996**; (3) ST seats **>= 50%** & **100% of Chairpersons at ALL levels** reserved for STs; (4) Mining recommendation covers **minor minerals only**; (5) PESA mandates **consultation** on land acquisition (**consent** under **LARR 2013, Sec. 41**).

## 5. Three decades on: rural devolution scorecard and 2025–2026 reforms

Over 33 years since 1993, India operates **over 2.5 lakh Gram Panchayats** and **31–32 lakh elected representatives**—yet empirical audits show a sharp divide between inclusive polls and substantive fiscal-functional autonomy.

- **Panchayat Devolution Index (PDI) 2024 (13 February 2025):** Compiled by **IIPA** for the Ministry of Panchayati Raj across **six dimensions** (**Framework, Functions, Finances, Functionaries, Capacity Enhancement, Accountability**), national devolution edged up from **39.9% (39.92) in 2013–14 to 43.9% (43.89) in 2021–22**. **Capacity Enhancement** jumped sharpest (**44% to 54.6%**) under **Rashtriya Gram Swaraj Abhiyan (RGSA, 2018; revamped 2022–23)**. Top five states: **Karnataka (72.23)**, **Kerala (70.59)**, **Tamil Nadu (68.38)**, **Maharashtra (61.44)**, and **Uttar Pradesh (60.07**, up **10 spots** on accountability). Alongside PDI, **Panchayat Advancement Index (PAI) 2.0** onboarded **2.59 lakh GPs** across 33 States/UTs by August 2026 to track local SDG progress.
- **Women's leadership versus *"Sarpanch Pati"*:** India has **over 15 lakh Women Elected Representatives (~46.6%)**, with research (Chattopadhyay & Duflo, 2004) linking women sarpanches to higher spending on drinking water, health, and schools. Yet male-relative proxy control (***Pradhan Pati / Sarpanch Pati***) erodes real agency. Acting on Supreme Court advice, the MoPR Advisory Committee chaired by **Sushil Kumar** submitted its **February 2025** report, recommending **"exemplary penalties"** for verified proxy interference, **public Gram Sabha swearing-in** of women sarpanches, **women's ombudspersons**, confidential helplines with whistleblower rewards, women's federations, and resolution tracking via **Panchayat NIRNAY**.
- **Rural fiscal dependence & 16th Finance Commission boost:** The **RBI's *Report on Finances of Panchayati Raj Institutions* (January 2024, FY 2022–23)** found own tax revenue of just **₹737 crore** out of **₹35,354 crore** total revenue (**~1–2%; ~₹21,000/GP**), with **~95% grant dependence** (**~80%** Union, **~15%** State; average revenue ranged from >₹60 lakh/GP in Kerala to <₹6 lakh in Andhra Pradesh, Haryana, and Punjab). Addressing this, the **16th Finance Commission (2026–31, accepted 1 February 2026)** recommended a record **₹4,35,236 crore** for Rural Local Bodies (part of **₹7,91,493 crore** total local grants; ~84% above 15th FC's ₹2,36,805 crore)—**80% Basic (₹3,48,188 cr**: half untied, half tied to sanitation, waste, and water) and **20% Performance (₹87,048 cr)**—contingent on **three entry criteria**: constitutional local bodies, online audited accounts, and timely SFCs. *(Detailed 3Fs, SFC, and 15th vs 16th FC local-finance analysis follows in the companion note on Urban Local Bodies.)*
- **Digital village governance (2025–2026):** **eGramSwaraj** facilitated digital payments exceeding **₹3 lakh crore** across >2.5 lakh GPs by March 2026 alongside **AuditOnline**, **Meri Panchayat**, and **SAMARTH Panchayat** (Own Source Revenue). **SVAMITVA** drone-surveyed **3.29–3.30 lakh villages** and prepared **>3.10 crore Property Cards** by March–April 2026, unlocking rural credit and property-tax rolls. Launched on **14 August 2025**, **SabhaSaar**—an AI voice-to-text Gram Sabha minutes tool integrated with **Bhashini** in **23 languages**—was adopted by **>1.1 lakh GPs by early 2026**.

> **Mains angle:** Frame GS-2 answers around **democratic deepening vs incomplete 3Fs**: highlight regular polls, **15+ lakh women (~46.6%)**, digital tools (**eGramSwaraj, SVAMITVA, SabhaSaar**), and the **16th FC's ₹4.35 lakh crore** rural grant—while diagnosing **43.9% PDI**, **~1–2% own-tax share**, ***Pradhan Pati***, and delayed PESA harmonisation. Conclude with clear **activity mapping**, Own Source Revenue via **SVAMITVA + SAMARTH**, **Sushil Kumar panel** anti-proxy enforcement, PESA–FRA alignment, and enforcing the 16th FC's three entry conditions.
