## 1. Parliamentary privileges: scope, sources and boundaries (Article 105)

Parliamentary privileges are not personal perks for lawmakers; they are functional legal immunities designed to protect the dignity, independence, and fearless deliberative authority of legislatures. **Article 105** establishes these privileges for Parliament (with an exact state parallel in **Article 194**).

> "Parliamentary privilege, codified in Articles 105 and 194 of the Constitution, is integral to deliberative democracy in facilitating the functioning of a parliamentary form of governance." — Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud, *Sita Soren v. Union of India*, 4 March 2024

Under **Article 105(4)**, privileges extend to **both Houses collectively, their committees, all MPs, Union Ministers, and the Attorney General of India** (every person entitled to speak and participate in proceedings). Crucially, **the President of India—though an integral part of Parliament under Article 79—does NOT enjoy parliamentary privileges under Article 105** (presidential immunities flow separately from **Article 361**; similarly, Governors stand outside Article 194, whereas Advocates General are covered).

### Five sources and the 44th Amendment shift

Unlike Fundamental Rights, parliamentary privileges remain largely uncodified and derive from **five sources**: (1) **Constitutional provisions** (**Articles 105, 122, and 361A**); (2) **statutes** (notably **Section 135A of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908**); (3) **Rules of Procedure** of each House; (4) **parliamentary conventions**; and (5) **judicial interpretations**. Originally, Article 105(3) equated uncodified privileges with those of the **British House of Commons** as on 26 January 1950. The **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978** deleted this colonial verbal reference, substituting privileges as they existed immediately before Section 15 of the 44th Amendment took effect—preserving their substantive scope until Parliament defines them by law.

### Collective versus individual privileges

| Category | Key Privileges & Constitutional / Legal Anchor | Critical Prelims Boundaries |
|---|---|---|
| **Collective** (of each House) | Publish debates & prohibit outside publication (**Art 361A** — 44th Amdt protects **substantially true** media reports without malice) | **Art 361A does NOT cover secret sittings** |
| **Collective** | Exclude strangers; regulate internal procedure (**Art 118**); courts barred from probing mere procedural irregularity (**Art 122**) | Courts CAN still review **substantive illegality, unconstitutionality, or *mala fides*** (*Raja Ram Pal*, 2007) |
| **Collective** | Punish members or outsiders for **breach of privilege** or **contempt of the House** (admonition, reprimand, imprisonment, suspension, expulsion) | Imprisonment lapses on prorogation; suspension **cannot exceed remainder of ongoing session** (*Ashish Shelar*, 2022) |
| **Collective** | Immediate intimation of arrest, detention, conviction, or release of an MP; no arrest or legal process **within House precincts** without presiding officer's permission | Applies to both civil and criminal service inside precincts |
| **Individual** (of MPs) | **Freedom of speech & vote** inside Parliament/committees (**Art 105(1)–(2)**)—absolute immunity from courts | Subject to **Art 121** (no discussion of SC/HC judges' conduct except on removal motion) & House rules; **NO immunity for bribery** (*Sita Soren*, 2024) |
| **Individual** | **Freedom from arrest in CIVIL cases only** during a session and **40 days before and 40 days after** (**Sec 135A CPC**) | **ZERO immunity in criminal cases or preventive detention!** |
| **Individual** | Exemption from jury service and from appearing as a court witness while Parliament is in session | May waive privilege only with House permission |

> **Prelims pointer:** Distinguish **breach of privilege** (violating a specific recognised privilege of a House or MP) from **contempt of the House** (a wider offence—any act or omission obstructing proceedings or lowering the House's dignity, even without infringing a named privilege). Also remember the **two classic traps**: (1) the **Attorney General** enjoys Article 105 privileges, the **President** does not; (2) the **40-day pre/post-session arrest shield** applies **ONLY to civil cases**, never criminal or preventive detention!

## 2. Privileges versus Fundamental Rights, landmark rulings and codification

Because India rests on **constitutional supremacy** rather than British parliamentary sovereignty, uncodified privileges have repeatedly collided with **Part III Fundamental Rights** and judicial review:

| Landmark Case | Core Constitutional Holding |
|---|---|
| ***M.S.M. Sharma* (*Searchlight I*, 1959)** | Specific legislative privileges under **Art 194(3)** prevail over general **Art 19(1)(a)** free speech (prohibiting publication of expunged speech), though **Art 21** remains applicable |
| ***Keshav Singh / UP Assembly Ref.* (1964)** | 7-judge bench held legislatures cannot punish High Court judges for granting bail; House warrants remain subject to **Art 21** and **Arts 32/226** writ review |
| ***Raja Ram Pal v. Speaker, LS* (2007)** | Upheld Parliament's power to **expel MPs** (11 MPs in "cash-for-query" scam); drew sharp line between **procedural irregularity** (immune under Art 122) and **substantive illegality/*mala fides*** (reviewable) |
| ***State of Kerala v. K. Ajith* (2021)** | Vandalising Assembly microphones/furniture is a crime, not protected speech—**"privileges are not gateways to claim exemptions from the general law of the land"** |
| ***Ashish Shelar v. Maharashtra Assembly* (Jan 2022)** | Struck down 1-year suspension of 12 BJP MLAs; suspension is a **"self-security"** tool and **cannot extend beyond the remainder of the ongoing session** |
| ***Sita Soren v. Union of India* (4 Mar 2024)** | **7-judge bench unanimously overruled *P.V. Narasimha Rao* (1998)**; **bribery to vote or speak is NOT protected by Arts 105(2)/194(2)** |

> **In the news:** ***Sita Soren v. Union of India* (4 March 2024, `2024 INSC 161`).** A seven-judge Constitution Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud unanimously overturned the 1998 3:2 ruling in ***P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State* (JMM Bribery Case)**, which had produced the absurd paradox of immunising bribe-takers who fulfilled their bargain by voting while prosecuting those who did not (like Jharkhand MLA Sita Soren in the 2012 Rajya Sabha poll). Establishing a **two-fold test**—a privilege claim must be (i) tethered to the **collective functioning of the House** and (ii) **necessary** to discharge legislative duties—the Court held that bribery fails both tests and that **the offence completes the moment a bribe is offered or accepted**, regardless of how or whether the lawmaker later votes.

### The codification debate (Mains)

Should Parliament enact a comprehensive statute defining privileges under **Article 105(3)**?

- **Case for codification:** Brings legal certainty for citizens and journalists; harmonises privileges with **Part III Fundamental Rights** (a codified statute becomes "law" under **Article 13**); curbs arbitrary contempt actions against critics; endorsed by the **NCRWC (2002)** and Press Commission.
- **Case against codification:** Exposing every privilege to **Article 13** litigation could chill fearless floor scrutiny and dilute institutional autonomy; uncodified flexibility handles unforeseen situations (even the UK Parliament and Lok Sabha's Privileges Committee in 2008 opposed comprehensive codification).
- **Balanced view:** Retain strong functional immunity for floor speech and votes while **narrowly defining contempt**—especially against citizens and media—and guaranteeing natural justice in privilege hearings.

## 3. The anti-defection law: Tenth Schedule architecture

After the 1967 elections ushered in chronic floor-crossing—immortalised as **"Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram"** after Haryana MLA Gaya Lal changed parties thrice in a fortnight—Parliament enacted the **52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985**, inserting the **Tenth Schedule** and amending **Articles 101, 102(2), 190, and 191(2)**.

> "The evil of political defections has been a matter of national concern. If it is not combated, it is likely to undermine the very foundations of our democracy and the principles which sustain it." — Statement of Objects and Reasons, Constitution (52nd Amendment) Bill, 23 January 1985

Finding that retail splits had exploited the original law's loophole, Parliament passed the **91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003**, which: (1) **deleted Paragraph 3** (the **one-third split** exception); (2) capped Union and State Councils of Ministers at **15%** of Lok Sabha/Assembly strength (**Arts 75(1A), 164(1A)**, min 12 in States); and (3) barred Tenth Schedule defectors from holding **ministerial posts (Arts 75(1B), 164(1B))** or any **remunerative political post (Article 361B)** until re-elected or term expiry.

### Grounds for disqualification and exemptions

| Member Category | Disqualification Ground (Tenth Schedule) | Key Precedent / Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| **Political party members** (Para 2(1)) | (a) **Voluntarily giving up membership** of the party; OR (b) **voting/abstaining** contrary to a **party whip** without prior permission and uncondoned within **15 days** | "Voluntarily giving up" is **wider than formal resignation**—inferred from conduct inside/outside the House (***Ravi S. Naik*, 1994**; contesting on rival ticket); an **expelled** member deemed "unattached" loses seat if they join another party (***G. Viswanathan*, 1996**) |
| **Independent members** (Para 2(2)) | **Joining ANY political party** after election | May extend outside support or become a minister without joining a party |
| **Nominated members** (Para 2(3)) | Joining a political party **AFTER six months** from taking their seat | Allowed a **6-month window** to join a party without disqualification |
| **Merger exception** (Para 4) | **NO disqualification** if original party merges with another AND **≥ 2/3rd** of legislature party members agree | Both the merging ≥2/3rd majority AND any dissenting minority choosing to function as a separate group are protected (**1/3rd split deleted by 91st Amdt, 2003**) |
| **Presiding Officers** (Para 5) | **Exempt** if Speaker/Deputy Speaker (LS/Assembly) or Deputy Chairman (RS) / Chairman & Deputy Chairman (Council) voluntarily resigns party on election and rejoins after demitting office | Designed to promote British-style Speaker neutrality (rarely exercised in India) |

> **Prelims pointer:** Watch three high-frequency traps: (1) **The phrase "political party" IS explicitly mentioned in the Constitution**—inserted for the first time by the **52nd Amendment (1985)** in the **Tenth Schedule**, and echoed in Articles 75(1B), 164(1B), and 361B (tested in Prelims 2025); (2) the Tenth Schedule **does NOT apply** to **Presidential and Vice-Presidential elections** (secret ballot—whips barred) or automatic whip disqualification in **Rajya Sabha elections** (open ballot upheld in ***Kuldip Nayar*, 2006**; cross-voting triggers party discipline, though wider anti-party conduct can still attract Para 2(1)(a)); and (3) **Article 102(1)/191(1)** disqualifications (office of profit, RPA 1951) are decided by the **President/Governor on binding ECI opinion (Arts 103/192)**, whereas **Tenth Schedule** disqualifications are decided by the **Presiding Officer (Para 6)**—never the President on ministerial advice!

## 4. Deciding authority, judicial milestones and 2025–2026 updates

Because **Paragraph 6** makes the Speaker or Chairman the adjudicator—often a ruling-party politician reluctant to disqualify defectors aiding their own government—the Supreme Court has spent three decades curbing partisan delay and abuse:

```text
  Defection Petition → Speaker / Chairman (Para 6)
     (Acts as a Tribunal — Kihoto Hollohan, 1992)
                         │
       ┌─────────────────┴─────────────────┐
       ▼                                   ▼
  3-MONTH TIMELINE                  FINAL ORDER REVIEW
  (Keisham 2020; Padi               (Arts 136, 226, 227)
   Kaushik Reddy 2025)              • Mala fides /
  • Adverse inference on              perversity
    delaying MLAs                   • Natural justice
  • Contempt on inaction            • Constitutionality
```

1. ***Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu* (1992, 5-judge bench, 3:2):** Upheld the Tenth Schedule's basic validity (does not violate Basic Structure or Art 105), but **struck down Paragraph 7** (barring judicial review, enacted without state ratification under Art 368(2) proviso). Held the Presiding Officer acts as a **Tribunal** whose **final order** is reviewable under **Articles 136, 226, and 227** for *mala fides*, perversity, constitutional violation, or breach of natural justice (ordinarily no pre-decision intervention).
2. ***Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur* (January 2020):** Ruled that absent exceptional reasons, Speakers must decide disqualification petitions within a **reasonable period of 3 months**, and strongly urged Parliament to replace the Speaker with an **independent permanent tribunal** headed by a retired SC judge or retired HC Chief Justice.
3. ***Subhash Desai v. Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra* (11 May 2023, 5-judge bench):** Distinguished the **"political party"** (organisation) from the **"legislature party"** (MLAs), holding that the **political party—not a legislative faction—appoints the whip**. Critically, it referred ***Nabam Rebia* (2016)** (which barred a Speaker facing a removal notice from deciding Tenth Schedule petitions—a loophole defectors exploited to freeze proceedings) to a **7-judge Constitution Bench**, which remained **pending as of November 2026**.

> **In the news:** ***Padi Kaushik Reddy v. State of Telangana* (31 July 2025, `2025 INSC 912`) & 2026 follow-up.** Confronting over a year of inaction by the Telangana Speaker on disqualification pleas against **10 Bharat Rashtra Samithi (BRS) MLAs** who crossed over to the ruling Congress in 2024, a Supreme Court bench of **CJI B.R. Gavai and Justice A.G. Masih** set aside a High Court Division Bench order and issued binding deadlines: (1) while the Speaker decides at first instance (*Kihoto Hollohan* / *Subhash Desai*), the Speaker acts as a **tribunal** and cannot frustrate the Tenth Schedule through delay; (2) directed the Speaker to **conclude all 10 petitions within 3 months** and draw **adverse inferences** against MLAs trying to protract hearings; and (3) urged **Parliament to reconsider whether entrusting Speakers/Chairmen with defection adjudication still combats defections effectively**. After contempt petitions and a stern Supreme Court ultimatum, the Speaker decided all 10 petitions, prompting the Court to close contempt proceedings on **12 March 2026**—though the Telangana High Court later overturned a Speaker dismissal on merits where an MLA had even contested Lok Sabha polls on a rival party ticket!

### Critique and reform of the Tenth Schedule (Mains)

While curbing retail floor-crossing, the Tenth Schedule suffers from four chronic flaws: (1) **stifling representative democracy**, reducing MPs to whip-driven voting machines unable to voice constituency concerns on routine Bills; (2) **rewarding wholesale defections** via Para 4's 2/3rd merger shield and "resort politics"; (3) the **resignation-and-reelection bypass** (Karnataka 2019, MP 2020), where rebel MLAs resign to topple a ministry and re-contest on rival tickets (in *Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil*, 2019, SC upheld disqualification despite resignation but struck down barring re-contest for the full term); and (4) **partisan Speaker bias and delay**.

Key committee-backed reforms include: restricting whip-based disqualification **only to confidence/no-confidence motions, Money Bills, and Financial Bills** (**Dinesh Goswami Committee, 1990** & **Law Commission 170th Report, 1999**); shifting adjudication to an **independent permanent tribunal** (*Keisham Meghachandra*, 2020) or the **President/Governor acting on binding ECI advice** (Dinesh Goswami / 2nd ARC); codifying a **3-month decision deadline**; abolishing or tightening the 2/3rd merger loophole; and barring resigning defectors from ministership for the remainder of the term.

## 5. Issues in parliamentary functioning: data, causes and gaps

Beyond privileges and defections, GS-2 repeatedly asks whether India's Parliament is weakening as a deliberative check on the executive. Verified tracking by **PRS Legislative Research (2024–2026)** and the **Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR, June 2024)** points to five systemic stresses:

| Health Indicator | Historical Benchmark | Recent Reality (PRS / ADR, up to Nov 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| **Annual sitting days** | **135 days/yr** in 1st LS (677 total); 116 in 2nd LS | **55 days/yr** in **17th LS** (274 total—**shortest full-term LS since 1952**; 11/15 sessions adjourned early); **62 days in 2025** |
| **Bills sent to committees** | **60%** (14th LS); **71%** (15th LS) | **25%** (16th LS) → **16%** (17th LS) → **21%** in **18th LS up to Monsoon 2026** |
| **Speed of passage** | Broad floor + panel debate | **58%** of 17th LS Bills passed within 2 weeks; **35%** with <1 hr LS debate; in **Monsoon 2026**, 9 of 11 LS Bills passed with only the minister speaking |
| **Question Hour & productivity** | Core daily accountability slot | **17th LS:** QH ran **60% (LS) / 52% (RS)**; only **24%** of starred questions answered orally in LS. **Monsoon 2026:** LS worked **15%** (QH **1%**!), RS **33%** |
| **Budget guillotining** | Detailed ministry scrutiny | **89%** guillotined in 2024–25; **77%** in **Budget Session 2026** (only 2 of 5 listed ministries debated) |
| **Criminal background (LS)** | 23% (2004), 30% (2009), 34% (2014) | **18th LS (2024, ADR):** **251/543 MPs (46%)** with declared criminal cases; **170 (31%)** with **serious criminal charges**; **93%** crorepatis |

Five interrelated structural issues drive these figures:

1. **Disruptions, eroding decorum, and mass suspensions:** Planned walkouts and sloganeering routinely wipe out Question Hour—Parliament's sharpest daily oversight instrument. In response, presiding officers have increasingly invoked **LS Rules 373, 374, and 374A** (Rule 374A, added 2001, enables automatic 5-sitting suspension without a motion) and **RS Rules 255–256**. The 17th Lok Sabha recorded **206 suspension instances**, peaking in **December 2023 (Winter Session)** when **146 MPs (100 LS + 46 RS)** were suspended in one week while major criminal-law Bills passed—followed by fresh suspensions (8 MPs) in February 2026.
2. **Bypassing legislative scrutiny and Money Bill controversies:** Alongside low DRSC referrals and blanket Budget guillotines, certifying contested non-tax laws (such as the Aadhaar Act, 2016 and Finance Act, 2017 tribunal amendments) as **Money Bills** bypassed Rajya Sabha scrutiny; the 7-judge Constitution Bench reference in ***Rojer Mathew* (2019)** remained **pending as of November 2026**.
3. **Ordinance raj:** Frequent executive lawmaking under **Articles 123 and 213** (45 Union ordinances in 16th LS, ~35 in 17th LS, and **127 state ordinances in 2025** alone; detailed mechanics sit in the President note) persists despite Supreme Court censure in ***D.C. Wadhwa* (1987)** (re-promulgation is a **"fraud on the Constitution"**) and ***Krishna Kumar Singh* (2017, 7-judge bench)** (mandatory laying before legislature; rights under unapproved ordinances do not automatically survive).
4. **Institutional vacancies and fading Private Members' Bills:** Despite **Article 93** requiring Lok Sabha to elect a **Deputy Speaker** "as soon as may be", the **17th Lok Sabha** was the first ever to remain without one for its entire term, and the post stayed **vacant in the 18th Lok Sabha as of November 2026** (over seven years—though formal **Leader of the Opposition** status returned in 2024 after a decade). Meanwhile, only **14 Private Members' Bills** have become law since 1952, the **last in 1970** (*Supreme Court (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Act, 1970*).
5. **Criminalisation and capacity deficit:** Rising criminal charges (**46% of 18th LS MPs**, ADR 2024) damage Parliament's moral authority, while a shortage of dedicated research staff leaves individual MPs outmatched by executive ministries.

## 6. Reforming Parliament: a balanced way forward for Mains

Restoring Parliament as India's supreme deliberative and accountability forum requires structural institutional reforms across six fronts:

1. **Mandate a minimum annual calendar:** Fix at least **120 sitting days/year for Lok Sabha and 100 for Rajya Sabha** (**NCRWC, 2002**, echoed by Presiding Officers' Conferences) through a predictable year-round calendar rather than ad-hoc executive summoning.
2. **Institutionalise "Opposition Days":** Adopt the UK House of Commons model allocating fixed days (e.g., one day per week or 15–20 days annually) where the Opposition selects urgent motions for debate—channelling protest from the well of the House back into structured scrutiny.
3. **Make committee & pre-legislative scrutiny the default:** Mandate DRSC referral for all major Bills (barring recorded emergencies), enforce the **Pre-Legislative Consultation Policy (2014)** (30-day public draft window), and attach **Legislative Impact Assessments** (fiscal, federal, regulatory) to Bills.
4. **Insulate presiding officers & overhaul the Tenth Schedule:** Move toward the UK convention of non-partisan Speakers, promptly fill the **Deputy Speaker** post (conventionally from the Opposition), transfer defection adjudication to an **independent tribunal / ECI-advised body**, and confine whips to confidence and financial votes.
5. **Build research & fiscal capacity:** Establish an independent **Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO)** and expand legislative research fellowships for MPs so scrutiny matches executive expertise.
6. **Enforce decorum and clean politics:** Pair an enforceable **MP Code of Conduct** (respecting *Ashish Shelar* limits on suspensions while imposing zero tolerance for vandalism under *K. Ajith*) with fast-track MP/MLA criminal courts and transparent political funding.

> **Mains angle:** Link **privileges, anti-defection, and parliamentary productivity** into one coherent GS-2 thesis: Parliament's decline is not merely behavioural—it is **institutional**. Anchor answers with **2024–2026 benchmarks** (*Sita Soren* 2024, *Padi Kaushik Reddy* 2025–26, PRS 55 sitting days / 21% committee referral / 77% Budget guillotine, ADR 46% criminal cases), and close with balanced institutional fixes—100–120 minimum sitting days (NCRWC), Opposition Days, mandatory DRSC scrutiny, an independent anti-defection tribunal, and a Parliamentary Budget Office.
