## 1. The President: Election, Qualifications, Term and Impeachment

**Part V (`Articles 52` to `78`)** of the Constitution of India establishes the **Union Executive**, defining the apex governing architecture of the Indian Republic. A perennial UPSC Prelims detail demands immediate attention: the Union Executive consists of **five** distinct constitutional offices—the **President**, the **Vice-President**, the **Prime Minister**, the **Council of Ministers**, and the **Attorney General of India** (`Article 76`). Candidates frequently overlook the inclusion of the Attorney General (India's highest law officer), yet because `Article 76` sits directly inside Chapter I ("The Executive") of Part V, the Constitution treats the Attorney General as an integral component of the Union Executive alongside the four political and constitutional offices.

At the summit of this structure stands the **President of India** (`Article 52`: *"There shall be a President of India"*). Because India adopted a **republican** polity rather than a hereditary monarchy, the head of the Indian State is elected rather than born to a dynasty; and because the Constituent Assembly simultaneously chose a **parliamentary system of government** on the British Westminster pattern rather than the American presidential system, it separated executive authority into two complementary offices.

The **Prime Minister and Council of Ministers** constitute the real (*de facto*) executive, accountable daily to the directly elected Lok Sabha, whereas the **President** serves as the nominal (*de jure*) executive, the **Head of the Indian State**, the **first citizen of India**, and the living symbol of national unity, integrity, and constitutional solidarity. **Droupadi Murmu** has served as the **15th President of India** since **25 July 2022** (the first President from a tribal community, the second woman, the youngest individual to assume the presidency, and the first President born after Independence), while **C.P. Radhakrishnan** assumed office as the **15th Vice-President of India** on **12 September 2025**.

> "He is the head of the State but not of the Executive. He represents the Nation but does not rule the Nation. He is the symbol of the nation. His place in the administration is that of a ceremonial device on a seal by which the nation's decisions are made known." — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, Vol. VII, 4 November 1948

```keyfacts
# The Indian Presidency at a Glance (Articles 52–62, 71)
Articles 52–78 | Five Offices of Union Executive | President, Vice-President, Prime Minister, Council of Ministers, and **Attorney General** (`Art. 76`)
Article 54 & 55 | Indirect Federal Election | Elected MPs + Elected MLAs of 28 States, Delhi & Puducherry (`70th Amend, 1992`); PR-STV secret ballot; 1971 Census frozen till post-2026
Article 58 & 60 | Eligibility & Unique Oath | Indian citizen (birth or naturalisation), 35 yrs, Lok Sabha eligible; oath by CJI to *"preserve, protect and defend the Constitution"*
Article 61 | Strictest Removal Majority | Quasi-judicial impeachment for undefined *"violation of the Constitution"*; **2/3rd of TOTAL membership** of each House
```

### The Electoral College and the Mechanics of Election

Unlike the Prime Minister, who emerges from parliamentary majority, or the President of the United States and France, who are chosen through nationwide popular mandates, the President of India is elected **indirectly** by a specially constructed **Electoral College** designed to reflect India's federal character while preserving parliamentary supremacy.

#### Composition of the Electoral College (Article 54)

Under **`Article 54`**, the Presidential Electoral College comprises **strictly elected representatives** drawn from both tiers of India's federal polity:

1. **The elected members of both Houses of Parliament** — currently **543** elected members of the Lok Sabha and **233** elected members of the Rajya Sabha (**776** elected MPs in total).
2. **The elected members of the Legislative Assemblies (Vidhan Sabhas) of all 28 States** — across India's 28 States.
3. **The elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the Union Territories of Delhi (NCT) and Puducherry** — explicitly brought within the meaning of "State" for `Articles 54` and `55` by the **70th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992** (effective from 1 June 1995), which inserted an Explanation at the end of `Article 54`.

Equally decisive for UPSC Prelims is knowing **who is strictly EXCLUDED** from the Presidential Electoral College:

- **Nominated members of both Houses of Parliament:** The **12** nominated members of the Rajya Sabha (`Art. 80(3)`) cannot vote. *(The two erstwhile nominated Anglo-Indian seats in the Lok Sabha under `Art. 331` were discontinued from 25 January 2020 by the **104th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019**.)*
- **Nominated members of State Legislative Assemblies:** Previously applicable to nominated Anglo-Indian MLAs (`Art. 333`, also discontinued by the **104th Amendment Act, 2019**), and any nominated MLAs in Union Territory Assemblies (such as the 3 Central Government-nominated MLAs in Puducherry or 5 nominated MLAs in Jammu and Kashmir).
- **ALL members (both elected AND nominated) of State Legislative Councils (Vidhan Parishads):** In the six bicameral States (Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, and Uttar Pradesh), **no MLC participates** in the Presidential election, because second chambers do not exist uniformly across all States; admitting them would distort inter-state electoral parity.
- **Members of a dissolved Legislative Assembly:** If a State Legislative Assembly stands dissolved at the time of the Presidential poll, its former members cease to be electors and cannot vote—even if fresh Assembly elections have not yet taken place before the Presidential poll.

#### The Constitutional Position of the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir

A subtle, contemporary constitutional question concerns the **Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir**, which was carved out of the erstwhile State of Jammu and Kashmir under the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** (effective 31 October 2019) and elected a new 90-member Legislative Assembly in **September–October 2024**. Prior to 2019, as a full State, Jammu and Kashmir's elected MLAs participated in every Presidential election (except when its Assembly stood dissolved). In the **2022 Presidential Election**, the UT of J&K had no constituted Assembly, so its MLAs did not participate, reducing total elected MLAs to **4,033** and dropping the value of each MP's vote from **708** (in 2017) to **700** (in 2022).

Even after the reconstitution of the J&K Legislative Assembly in October 2024, however, the **Explanation to `Article 54`** still explicitly names **only the National Capital Territory of Delhi and the Union Territory of Puducherry** (as inserted by the 70th Amendment Act, 1992). In response to an RTI query, the Election Commission of India pointed directly to the text of `Article 54`. Constitutional scholars remain divided into two schools:
- **The Strict Textual View (Majority Consensus):** Including the UT of J&K in the Electoral College requires a **formal constitutional amendment** to `Article 54` passed by a **special majority of Parliament AND ratified by at least half the State Legislatures** under the proviso to **`Article 368(2)`** (precisely as was done for Delhi and Puducherry via the 70th Amendment), unless full statehood is restored to Jammu and Kashmir before the **2027 Presidential election**.
- **The Statutory Extension View:** Some jurists point to **Section 13 of the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019**, which declares that the provisions of `Article 239A` applicable to the UT of Puducherry shall also apply to the UT of J&K, arguing Parliament sought parity with Puducherry. Yet ordinary parliamentary legislation under `Article 3` cannot amend the federal election clause entrenched in the proviso to `Article 368(2)`.

#### Value of Votes: Uniformity Among States and Parity with the Union (Article 55)

If every MLA and every MP simply cast one unweighted vote, 4,000+ MLAs would overwhelm 776 MPs, and a sparsely populated state's Assembly would outweigh a heavily populated state's Assembly. **`Article 55`** therefore builds an ingenious mathematical weighting formula anchored on **two constitutional principles**:

1. **Uniformity in the scale of representation of the different States** (`Article 55(1)`): So that the aggregate voting weight of a State's Assembly is proportionate to its population, regardless of how many Assembly seats the State has.
2. **Parity between the States as a whole and the Union** (`Article 55(2)(c)`): So that the **combined vote value of all elected MPs** equals, as nearly as practicable, the **combined vote value of all elected MLAs** across the country—giving both the Union and the States an equal 50:50 voice in choosing the Head of the Republic.

The exact statutory formulas laid down under `Article 55(2)` are:

- **Value of the vote of an MLA:**
  `Value of an MLA's vote = (Total Population of the State [1971 Census] ÷ Total Number of Elected MLAs in the State Assembly) × (1 ÷ 1,000)`
  *Rounding Rule (`Art. 55(2)(b)`):* If, after dividing by 1,000, the remainder is **500 or more**, the vote value of each MLA is increased by **1**; if less than 500, the remainder is ignored.

- **Value of the vote of an MP:**
  `Value of an MP's vote = Total Value of Votes of All Elected MLAs of All States (and Delhi & Puducherry) ÷ Total Number of Elected MPs of Both Houses of Parliament (LS + RS)`
  *Rounding Rule (`Art. 55(2)(c)`):* Fractions **exceeding one-half (> 0.5)** are counted as **1**, while fractions of **one-half or less (≤ 0.5)** are disregarded.

**The 1971 Census Freeze (`42nd` and `84th` Amendments):** Under the Explanation to `Article 55`, "population" originally meant the population ascertained at the last preceding census. However, because southern and progressive states that successfully implemented family-planning policies faced losing electoral weight relative to rapidly growing states, the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** froze the population base at the **1971 Census** until the year 2000, and the **84th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2001** extended this freeze **until the relevant figures for the first census taken after the year 2026 have been published**. Consequently, even in the **2022 Presidential Election**, all MLA vote values were computed using **1971 Census population figures**—giving **Uttar Pradesh** the highest per-MLA vote value (**208**), followed by **Jharkhand (176)**, **Tamil Nadu (176)**, **Maharashtra (175)**, and **Bihar (173)**, whereas **Sikkim** had the lowest (**7**), preceded by **Arunachal Pradesh (8)**, **Mizoram (8)**, **Nagaland (9)**, and **Puducherry (16)**.

Notice a critical mathematical insight directly tested by UPSC in **Prelims 2023**: the value of an MLA's vote does **NOT** increase merely because a State has more Assembly seats! On the contrary, the number of Assembly seats sits in the **denominator**, so per-MLA vote value depends strictly on the **ratio of 1971 population to elected seats** (average constituency population). Thus, **Kerala** (1971 pop. ~2.13 crore ÷ 140 seats → vote value **152**) has a **higher** per-MLA vote value than **Madhya Pradesh** (1971 pop. ~3.00 crore ÷ 230 seats → vote value **131**), even though Madhya Pradesh has far more MLAs and a larger population! Similarly, **Puducherry** (vote value **16**) exceeds **Arunachal Pradesh** (vote value **8**) because Puducherry's population-to-seat ratio is higher.

In the **16th Presidential Election (18 July 2022; counting 21 July 2022)**, **4,033 elected MLAs** commanded a total vote value of **5,43,231**, while **776 elected MPs** (each valued at **700**) commanded **5,43,200**—yielding a near-exact federal parity of **10,86,431** total electoral votes (halfway mark: 5,43,216). NDA candidate **Droupadi Murmu** secured **2,824 votes** worth **6,76,803 (64.03% of valid votes)**, defeating joint Opposition candidate **Yashwant Sinha** (**1,877 votes** worth **3,80,177**).

#### Proportional Representation by Single Transferable Vote (PR-STV) and Secret Ballot

Under **`Article 55(3)`**, the President's election is conducted in accordance with the system of **proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote (PR-STV)**, and voting is strictly by **secret ballot**. Unlike ordinary Lok Sabha or Assembly elections governed by First-Past-the-Post (where a plurality suffices), a Presidential candidate **must secure an absolute majority of valid votes polled**—known as the **Electoral Quota**:

`Electoral Quota = [Total Number of Valid Votes Polled ÷ (1 + 1)] + 1 = (Total Valid Votes Polled ÷ 2) + 1` *(ignoring any remainder)*

Each elector receives a single ballot paper listing all contesting candidates and marks preferences using numerals (`1`, `2`, `3`, `4`...) against candidates' names (marking the first preference `1` is mandatory for a valid ballot; subsequent preferences are optional). Counting proceeds iteratively:

1. **First-Preference Count:** All valid ballots are sorted by first preference (`1`). If any candidate reaches or exceeds the Electoral Quota in the first round, that candidate is immediately declared elected.
2. **Elimination and Transfer:** If no candidate attains the quota in the first count, the candidate with the **lowest number of first-preference votes** is eliminated, and their ballots are transferred to the surviving candidates according to the **second preference** marked on those ballots. This process repeats until one candidate crosses the quota.

Only **once** in Indian constitutional history did a Presidential election go to the **second-preference count**: in **1969**, following President Zakir Husain's death and the Congress split, independent candidate **V.V. Giri** trailed the quota in the first round against official Congress candidate Neelam Sanjiva Reddy and third candidate C.D. Deshmukh, and clinched victory narrowly on second-preference transfers (4,20,077 votes against the quota of 4,18,169). Conversely, **Neelam Sanjiva Reddy (1977)** remains the **only President elected unopposed** after the nominations of 36 other contenders were rejected during scrutiny.

**Secret Ballot and Immunity from Anti-Defection Law:** Because `Article 55(3)` mandates a **secret ballot**, political parties are **strictly prohibited from issuing any whip** to MPs or MLAs in a Presidential (or Vice-Presidential) election. The Election Commission of India has repeatedly reaffirmed that **cross-voting or voting contrary to a party's preference does NOT attract disqualification under the Tenth Schedule** (Anti-Defection Law). Moreover, electors must mark ballots using only the special violet-ink pen supplied by the ECI, and showing a marked ballot to any unauthorised person invalidates the vote.

**Academic Critique of "Proportional Representation":** Constitutional jurists observe that calling the Presidential election "proportional representation" is technically a misnomer. True proportional representation distributes **two or more seats** among competing groups in proportion to vote shares; where only **one seat** is at stake, the correct political-science label is the **Preferential Vote** or **Alternative Vote (Instant Runoff Voting)**. Likewise, every elector does not hold a literally "single" vote of uniform value (since MLAs' votes range from 7 to 208 and MPs hold 700), though each elector casts one transferable ballot.

#### Statutory Nomination Safeguards (1952 Act, Amended 1974 and 1997)

Under `Article 324`, the **Election Commission of India (ECI)** supervises and conducts Presidential and Vice-Presidential elections, appointing the **Secretary-General of the Lok Sabha** and the **Secretary-General of the Rajya Sabha** by rotation as the **Returning Officer**. Under the **Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952** (amended in **1974** and **1997** to weed out frivolous candidacies):
- Every nomination paper must be subscribed by at least **50 electors as proposers** and **50 electors as seconders** (raised from 10 proposers and 10 seconders in **1997**; no elector may propose or second more than one candidate).
- Every candidate must make a **security deposit of ₹15,000** in the Reserve Bank of India (raised from ₹2,500 in **1997**). The deposit is **forfeited** if the candidate fails to secure **one-sixth (1/6th)** of the valid votes polled.
- In ***Charan Lal Sahu v. Neelam Sanjeeva Reddy* (1978, 7-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court upheld the proposer/seconder and security deposit requirements as reasonable statutory regulations under `Article 71(3)` that neither violate `Article 14` nor contradict `Article 58`.

> - **Trap 1 (Nominated Members):** Nominated MPs (12 RS) and nominated MLAs are **NEVER** part of the Presidential Electoral College—even though nominated MPs *do* vote in Vice-Presidential elections and *do* participate in Presidential impeachment!
> - **Trap 2 (Assembly Seats vs Vote Value — Prelims 2023 Q80):** A higher number of elective Assembly seats does **NOT** mean a higher vote value per MLA! Because elective seats are in the **denominator**, per-MLA vote value depends on the **1971 population-to-seat ratio**. Thus, **Kerala's MLA vote value (152) > Madhya Pradesh's (131)**, and **Puducherry's (16) > Arunachal Pradesh's (8)**.
> - **Trap 3 (Vidhan Parishads):** **ZERO MLCs** (neither elected nor nominated) participate in the Presidential election.
> - **Trap 4 (Anti-Defection Law):** Because `Art. 55(3)` mandates a **secret ballot**, **no party whip** can be issued and cross-voting does **NOT** trigger Tenth Schedule disqualification.
> - **Trap 5 (MP Vote Value Drop):** In 2022, each MP's vote value fell from **708** to **700** because the UT of Jammu & Kashmir had no functioning Assembly (total MLA value fell while total elected MPs stayed 776).

#### Why Indirect Election? Constituent Assembly Debate and Comparative Perspective

During the Constituent Assembly Debates, several members—including Prof. K.T. Shah and H.V. Kamath—urged direct election of the President by universal adult franchise to invest the Head of State with democratic majesty. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar and Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru firmly rejected direct election on **four enduring constitutional grounds**:

1. **Harmony with the Parliamentary System:** Under India's Westminster design, real executive power vests in the Council of Ministers led by the Prime Minister, who is responsible to the Lok Sabha. Electing the President directly by hundreds of millions of citizens while denying them real executive authority would create an anomalous constitutional dyarchy—a President tempted to claim an independent popular mandate rivaling the Prime Minister.
2. **Disproportionate Cost, Time, and Administrative Burden:** Conducting a nationwide direct election across India's colossal electorate solely to choose a constitutional, non-executive head of state would expend staggering financial and administrative resources every five years.
3. **Preserving Federal Balance Beyond Parliament:** Equally, the Assembly rejected proposals to let **Parliament alone** elect the President (as proposed by some critics of the Electoral College), because a ruling party with a parliamentary majority could unilaterally install a partisan nominee, denying the States any voice in choosing the head of the federal Union. Including State MLAs with 50:50 parity makes the President a truly **national-federal representative**, not merely a nominee of the Central ruling party.
4. **Dignity Above Partisan Mass Campaigning:** Indirect election shields the highest constitutional office from the rough-and-tumble of populist street campaigns, fostering consensus candidates capable of commanding broad multi-party respect.

Nevertheless, UPSC **Mains GS-2 (2022, Q14)** directly required candidates to **"critically examine the procedures through which the Presidents of India and France are elected."** Comparing India's indirect federal college with the **American Electoral College** and **France's direct two-round system** (introduced under the Fifth Republic via Charles de Gaulle's **1962 constitutional referendum**) illuminates how each election method aligns strictly with the actual powers of the presidency:

```compare
# Presidential Election Architecture: India vs USA vs France
India (Parliamentary Republic) | USA (Presidential Republic) | France (Semi-Presidential Fifth Republic)
Constitutional Role :: **Nominal (*de jure*) Head of State**; bound by ministerial advice (`Art. 74`) | **Real Head of State AND Government**; independent of Congress | **Substantial executive Head of State** (defence, foreign policy, appoints PM, dissolves National Assembly)
Mode of Election :: **Indirect** via Federal Electoral College (`Art. 54`: elected MPs + elected MLAs) | **Indirect in form, direct in substance** via 538-member State-based Electoral College (winner-take-all in 48 States) | **Direct universal adult suffrage** (`Art. 6`, since 1962 referendum)
Voting System :: **PR-STV** (preferential secret ballot); absolute majority quota `(V/2)+1` | State popular vote awards pledged electors; absolute majority (**270 of 538**) required | **Two-Round Majority System** (`Art. 7`): >50% in Round 1, else top-two **run-off** 14 days later
Nomination Filter :: **50 proposers + 50 seconders** from Electoral College + ₹15,000 deposit | Party primaries/caucuses + state ballot-access petitions | **500 sponsorships (*parrainages*)** from elected officials across at least **30 départements**
Term & Re-election :: **5 years**; **unlimited** re-election (`Art. 57`) | **4 years**; **maximum 2 terms** (**22nd Amendment, 1951**) | **5 years** (cut from 7 yrs in **2000 referendum**); max **2 consecutive terms**
Dispute Arbiter :: **Supreme Court of India** (exclusive & final, `Art. 71`) | State courts, Congress (electoral count), and US Supreme Court | **Constitutional Council (*Conseil constitutionnel*)** (`Art. 58`)
```

Critically evaluated, **India's indirect procedure** succeeds in preventing executive collision and reflecting federal weight, yet critics note that party discipline in practice still lets a dominant Union coalition steer the outcome and that the 1971 Census freeze creates demographic distortion. **France's direct two-round system** confers unmistakable popular legitimacy and forces broad coalition-building in the second round, yet it encourages hyper-presidentialism, personality cults, and tense periods of **"cohabitation"** when the directly elected President faces a hostile majority in the National Assembly—demonstrating why each election design is functionally inseparable from the constitutional powers attached to the office.

### Election Disputes and Article 71 Jurisprudence

Because any prolonged legal uncertainty over the validity of a Presidential (or Vice-Presidential) election could paralyse the Union executive, **`Article 71`** creates a fast-track, exclusive judicial mechanism:

1. **Exclusive and Final Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court (`Article 71(1)`):** All doubts and disputes arising out of or in connection with the election of a President or Vice-President shall be inquired into and decided **exclusively by the Supreme Court of India**, whose decision is **final**. Neither a High Court nor the Election Commission can adjudicate such a dispute. Under the 1952 Act, an election petition can be filed **only after** the result is declared, within **30 days**, and **only by a contesting candidate or at least 20 electors** (10 electors for VP).
2. **Validity of Prior Acts (`Article 71(2)`):** If the Supreme Court declares the election of a President or Vice-President **void**, acts done by them in the exercise and performance of the powers and duties of office **on or before the date of the Supreme Court's decision are NOT invalidated**! *(Directly tested as Statement 1 in **UPSC Prelims 2023 Q36**, where UPSC falsely claimed prior acts become invalid.)*
3. **Parliamentary Regulation (`Article 71(3)`):** Subject to the Constitution, Parliament may by law regulate any matter relating to or connected with Presidential or Vice-Presidential elections (exercised via the *Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952*).
4. **Incomplete Electoral College Not a Ground (`Article 71(4)`):** Inserted by the **11th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1961**, this clause declares that the election of a President or Vice-President **cannot be called in question on the ground of the existence of any vacancy for whatever reason** among the members of the Electoral College electing them.

**The Emergency Detour (`39th` vs `44th` Amendments):** During the 1975 Emergency, following the Allahabad High Court's invalidation of Indira Gandhi's Lok Sabha election, Parliament passed the **39th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975**, substituting `Article 71` to strip the Supreme Court of jurisdiction over Presidential and Vice-Presidential election disputes (vesting it in a parliamentary body) and inserting `Article 329A` to shield the Prime Minister and Lok Sabha Speaker. While the Supreme Court struck down `Article 329A(4)` as violating the Basic Structure in ***Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975)**, it was the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978** that comprehensively repealed the 39th Amendment's distortion and **restored `Article 71`** to its original judicial framework, permanently reaffirming the Supreme Court as the sole arbiter.

> [!case]
> ***In re: Presidential Poll* (1974 — 7-Judge Constitution Bench, authored by CJI A.N. Ray):**
> - **Facts:** Ahead of the August 1974 Presidential election (when President V.V. Giri's term was expiring on 24 August 1974), the **Gujarat Legislative Assembly stood dissolved** following the Navnirman agitation, and fresh elections could not be held before August. President Giri invoked **`Article 143(1)`** asking whether the Presidential election could proceed with Gujarat's Assembly dissolved.
> - **Core Holding:** The Supreme Court unanimously answered **YES**—the Presidential election **MUST be held before the expiry of the incumbent's five-year term** (`Article 62(1)` is mandatory!), and it **cannot be postponed** merely because one or more State Legislative Assemblies stand dissolved. Relying on **`Article 71(4)`** (11th Amendment), the Bench ruled that the "Electoral College" means the duly constituted Houses and existing elected members on the date of the poll; vacancies—even of an entire dissolved State Assembly—neither halt the election nor invalidate its outcome. *(Directly tested in **UPSC Prelims 2023 Q36**!)*
> - **Caveat on Mala Fide Dissolution:** The Court left open the hypothetical scenario where a Central Government *mala fide* dissolved a majority of State Assemblies purely to rig a Presidential election—an abuse that would separately fail constitutional scrutiny under *S.R. Bommai* (1994).

### Qualifications, Oath, Conditions of Office and Constitutional Immunities

#### Qualifications for Election (Article 58)

Under **`Article 58(1)`**, no person is eligible for election as President unless they satisfy **four explicit constitutional qualifications**:

1. **Citizen of India:** Crucially, India permits **both a citizen by birth AND a naturalised citizen** to become President—in sharp contrast to the **United States Constitution (`Art. II, Sec. 1`)**, which strictly restricts the presidency to a **"natural-born citizen"**.
2. **Minimum Age of 35 Years:** The candidate must have completed **35 years** of age. *(Note: The Constitution specifies **no upper age limit** and **no minimum educational qualification**.)*
3. **Qualified for Election to the Lok Sabha:** The candidate must possess all qualifications required to be elected as a member of the **House of the People (Lok Sabha)**—including being registered as a voter in any parliamentary constituency in India (contrast with the Vice-President, who must be qualified for the **Rajya Sabha**).
4. **No Office of Profit:** The candidate must not hold any **office of profit** under the Government of India, any State Government, or any local or other authority subject to the control of any of the said governments.

**The Constitutional Exemption (`Article 58(2)` Explanation):** To prevent sitting constitutional functionaries and cabinet leaders from having to resign merely to contest the presidency, `Article 58(2)` expressly declares that a person **shall NOT be deemed to hold any office of profit** merely by reason of being:
- The **President** of India;
- The **Vice-President** of India;
- The **Governor** of any State; or
- A **Minister** either for the **Union** or for any **State**.

#### Oath or Affirmation (Article 60)

A frequent UPSC Prelims snare is assuming the President's oath appears in the **Third Schedule**. In fact, **neither the President's oath (`Article 60`), nor the Vice-President's oath (`Article 69`), nor the Governor's oath (`Article 159`) is in the Third Schedule**! Each is enshrined in its own dedicated constitutional Article.

Before entering office, the President takes an oath or affirmation in the presence of the **Chief Justice of India** (and in the CJI's absence, the **senior-most Judge of the Supreme Court** available). The President swears or affirms a distinctive three-fold constitutional covenant:
1. To **faithfully execute the office** of President (or discharge the functions of the President);
2. To the best of their ability to **"preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law"**; and
3. To **devote themselves to the service and well-being of the people of India**.

Note that the solemn phrase **"preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law"** is unique to the **President (`Art. 60`)** and the **Governor (`Art. 159`)**—symbolising their ultimate fiduciary responsibility as constitutional guardians (whereas Ministers, MPs, and Judges swear to *"bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution"* and *"uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India"*).

#### Conditions of Office and Emoluments (Article 59)

**`Article 59`** stipulates four constitutional conditions protecting the independence and dignity of the presidency:

1. **No Dual Legislative Membership (`Art. 59(1)`):** The President shall not be a member of either House of Parliament or of a House of any State Legislature. If any sitting MP, MLA, or MLC is elected President, they are **deemed to have vacated their legislative seat** on the date they enter upon office as President (not upon election declaration!).
2. **Bar on Other Offices (`Art. 59(2)`):** The President shall not hold any other office of profit.
3. **Official Residence and Statutory Emoluments (`Art. 59(3)`):** The President is entitled without payment of rent to the use of their official residences (**Rashtrapati Bhavan** in New Delhi, plus retreats at Mashobra/Shimla and Hyderabad) and to such emoluments, allowances, and privileges as Parliament determines by law (originally specified in the **Second Schedule**). Under the **President's Emoluments and Pension Act, 1951**, as amended by the **Finance Act, 2018** (with retrospective effect from **1 January 2016**), the President's monthly emolument was raised more than threefold from ₹1.50 lakh to **₹5,00,000 (₹5 lakh) per month**—the highest constitutional pay in India. Upon demitting office (by expiry or resignation), a former President receives a **lifetime pension equal to 50% of emoluments (₹2,50,000/month)**, alongside a rent-free furnished residence, secretarial staff, free medical care, free air/rail travel with a companion, and lifelong pension/housing benefits for a surviving spouse. All Presidential emoluments and allowances are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (`Art. 112(3)(a)`)**—meaning they are non-votable by Parliament.
4. **Non-Diminution Guarantee (`Art. 59(4)`):** The emoluments and allowances of the President **cannot be diminished during their term of office**, insulating the Head of State from retaliatory legislative cuts.

#### Personal and Official Immunities (Article 361)

To safeguard the dignity and uninterrupted functioning of the Head of State, **`Article 361`** confers sweeping personal and official immunities upon the President (and State Governors):

- **Official Immunity (`Art. 361(1)`):** The President is **not answerable to any court** for the exercise and performance of the powers and duties of office, or for any act done or purporting to be done in that capacity. **Two critical provisos apply:** (i) the conduct of the President **may be brought under review** by any court, tribunal, or body appointed or designated by either House of Parliament to investigate an **impeachment charge under `Article 61`**; and (ii) nothing restricts any person's right to bring appropriate legal proceedings **against the Government of India** (`Art. 300`). Moreover, in ***Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India* (2006)**, the Supreme Court clarified that personal immunity under `Article 361` does **NOT** insulate official acts from judicial review on grounds of *mala fides* or unconstitutionality—the court simply scrutinises the action through the Government without summoning the President/Governor personally.
- **Complete Immunity from Criminal Proceedings (`Art. 361(2)` & `(3)`):** **No criminal proceedings whatsoever** shall be instituted or continued against the President in any court during their term of office—even in respect of **personal acts** committed prior to or during office—and **no process for arrest or imprisonment** shall issue from any court during their tenure.
- **Conditional Immunity in Civil Proceedings (`Art. 361(4)`):** Civil proceedings in respect of **personal acts** done before or after entering office **may be instituted** during the President's term, **ONLY after giving two months' written notice** delivered to or left at the President's office stating the nature of the proceedings, the cause of action, the name and address of the claimant, and the relief claimed.

### Term of Office, Re-Election and Vacancy Succession

#### Five-Year Term, Holdover and Unlimited Re-Election (Articles 56 and 57)

Under **`Article 56(1)`**, the President holds office for a term of **five years from the date on which they enter upon office** (not from the date of election result). Three constitutional features govern tenure:
- **Resignation (`Art. 56(1)(a)` & `56(2)`):** The President may resign at any time by writing under their hand addressed to the **Vice-President of India**. Crucially, **`Article 56(2)`** commands that any resignation addressed to the Vice-President **shall forthwith be communicated by the Vice-President to the Speaker of the House of the People (Lok Sabha)**—a favourite UPSC Prelims detail!
- **Constitutional Holdover (`Art. 56(1)(c)`):** Notwithstanding the expiration of their five-year term, the President **continues to hold office until their successor enters upon office**, guaranteeing that India never suffers an interregnum without a Head of State.
- **Unlimited Eligibility for Re-Election (`Article 57`):** A person who holds, or has held, office as President is **eligible for re-election any number of times**. This contrasts sharply with the **United States (`22nd Amendment, 1951`)**, which strictly limits a President to **two terms** (enacted after Franklin D. Roosevelt won four terms), and **France (`Art. 6`)**, which caps incumbents at **two consecutive terms**. In practice, however, Indian political convention has leaned toward single terms: **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** (1950–1952 interim, 1952–1957, 1957–1962) remains the **only Indian President elected to two full constitutional terms**.

#### Filling Vacancies and Succession Mechanics (Articles 62, 65 and 70)

A vacancy in the President's office can arise in **five** ways: (1) expiry of the five-year tenure; (2) resignation; (3) removal by impeachment; (4) death in office; or (5) otherwise—for instance, when the incumbent becomes disqualified or the Supreme Court declares their election void.

- **Normal Expiry (`Article 62(1)`):** An election to fill a vacancy caused by expiration of term **must be completed BEFORE the expiration of the term**. If any unforeseen delay occurs, the outgoing President holds over under `Article 56(1)(c)`—the **Vice-President does NOT step in** merely because an election is delayed after term expiry!
- **Mid-Term Vacancy (`Article 62(2)`):** When a vacancy arises due to death, resignation, removal, or otherwise, an election must be held **as soon as possible, and in no case later than SIX MONTHS** from the date of occurrence of the vacancy. Importantly, the person elected to fill a mid-term vacancy is entitled to hold office for a **full term of five years** from the date they enter office (unlike the US Vice-President, who serves only the remainder of the departed President's four-year term).
- **Acting President (`Article 65`):** During a mid-term vacancy (`Art. 65(1)`), the **Vice-President acts as President** until the newly elected President enters office (maximum six months). Similarly, when the sitting President is unable to discharge functions owing to **absence, illness, or any other cause** (`Art. 65(2)`), the Vice-President discharges the President's functions until the President resumes duty. While acting as President or discharging Presidential functions, the Vice-President possesses **all powers and immunities of the President** and draws the **President's emoluments, allowances, and privileges (`Art. 65(3)`)**—not the Rajya Sabha Chairman's salary.
- **Simultaneous Vacancy — The 1969 Statutory Chain (`Article 70`):** What if the offices of **BOTH the President and the Vice-President** fall vacant simultaneously? Empowering Parliament to provide for unlisted contingencies under **`Article 70`**, Parliament enacted the **President (Discharge of Functions) Act, 1969**, establishing a clear statutory chain of succession: **Vice-President → Chief Justice of India → senior-most Judge of the Supreme Court available**.

> [!example]
> **Historical Precedents of Acting Presidents and the 25 July Tradition (1969 & 1977):**
> India has witnessed **two** Presidents die in office and **three** Acting Presidents (who are NOT counted in the official 1–15 numbering of elected Presidents):
> 1. **1969 Double Transition:** Upon the sudden death of 3rd President **Dr. Zakir Husain** on **3 May 1969**, Vice-President **V.V. Giri** became Acting President (`Art. 65(1)`). When Giri resigned on **20 July 1969** to contest the Presidential election as an independent candidate, both top offices fell vacant simultaneously! Invoking the freshly enacted **President (Discharge of Functions) Act, 1969**, **Chief Justice of India M. Hidayatullah** served as **Acting President** for 35 days (**20 July – 24 August 1969**)—becoming the **only CJI** to act as President (and later serving as 6th Vice-President, 1979–84). When Giri won, he became the **only person to serve as both Acting President and elected President**.
> 2. **1977 Transition & 25 July Date:** When 5th President **Dr. Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed** died on **11 February 1977**, Vice-President **B.D. Jatti** acted as President until **25 July 1977**, when unopposed winner **Neelam Sanjiva Reddy** took oath for a full five-year term. Because every subsequent President completed a full five-year term without mid-term vacancy, **25 July** has permanently remained India's Presidential inauguration day from 1977 right through **Droupadi Murmu's** swearing-in on **25 July 2022**.

### Impeachment of the President (Article 61)

Because the President is the supreme constitutional symbol of the Republic, the framers insulated the office from whimsical parliamentary removal while still upholding democratic accountability. Under **`Article 61`**, the President can be removed from office **ONLY by impeachment**—a solemn **quasi-judicial procedure conducted inside Parliament**.

#### Sole Ground: "Violation of the Constitution"

The **only constitutional ground** for impeaching the President is **"violation of the Constitution"** (`Article 61(1)`). Crucially—and repeatedly tested in UPSC Prelims—**the Constitution does NOT define the phrase "violation of the Constitution."** Unlike the United States Constitution (which specifies *"Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors"*) or India's own judge-removal standard (*"proved misbehaviour or incapacity"* under `Art. 124(4)`), Parliament itself determines what grave constitutional breach—such as persistently defying binding ministerial advice under `Article 74(1)`, refusing to summon Parliament, or usurping emergency powers—constitutes a "violation of the Constitution."

#### Step-by-Step Quasi-Judicial Procedure

```flow
# Article 61 Impeachment Process of the President of India
Stage 1: Initiation in EITHER House | Lok Sabha OR Rajya Sabha may prefer the charge of 'violation of the Constitution'
Stage 2: Threshold & Notice | Resolution signed by at least 1/4th (25%) of members of the initiating House + minimum 14 days' written notice
Stage 3: First House Passage | Resolution MUST be passed by a majority of NOT LESS THAN 2/3rd OF TOTAL MEMBERSHIP of that House
Stage 4: Investigation by Second House | Other House investigates the charge (or causes investigation via committee/tribunal) || Right of Defence | President has constitutional right to appear and be represented during investigation
Stage 5: Final Sustaining Vote | Second House sustains the charge by NOT LESS THAN 2/3rd OF ITS TOTAL MEMBERSHIP → President stands removed immediately from date of passage
```

Two extraordinary structural features make **`Article 61`** unique in the entire Indian Constitution:

1. **The Strictest Majority Threshold in the Constitution:** While constitutional amendments (`Art. 368`) and removal of Supreme Court Judges/CAG/CEC require a *special majority* of *"a majority of total membership PLUS two-thirds of members present and voting"*, Presidential impeachment demands **two-thirds (2/3rd) of the TOTAL MEMBERSHIP of EACH House**! In a 543-member Lok Sabha, that requires at least **362 votes** regardless of absenteeism; in a 245-member Rajya Sabha, at least **164 votes**. **No President of India has ever been impeached**, nor has an impeachment motion ever been initiated.
2. **The Asymmetric Composition Paradox (Electoral College vs Impeachment):** A classic UPSC Prelims trap lies in contrasting who *elects* the President (`Art. 54`) with who *removes* the President (`Art. 61`):
   - **Nominated Members of Parliament** (12 Rajya Sabha MPs) **DO NOT vote in the Presidential election**, yet they **DO participate and vote in Presidential impeachment**!
   - **Elected MLAs of States and UTs (Delhi & Puducherry)** **DO vote in the Presidential election**, yet they have **NO role whatsoever in Presidential impeachment**!

| Constitutional Event | Elected MPs (LS & RS) | Nominated MPs (RS) | Elected MLAs (28 States) | Elected MLAs (Delhi & Puducherry) | State MLCs (Vidhan Parishads) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Presidential Election (`Art. 54`)** | **YES** (weighted) | **NO** | **YES** (weighted) | **YES** (weighted; `70th Amend`) | **NO** |
| **Presidential Impeachment (`Art. 61`)** | **YES** | **YES** | **NO** | **NO** | **NO** |
| **Vice-Presidential Election (`Art. 66`)** | **YES** (equal value 1) | **YES** (equal value 1) | **NO** | **NO** | **NO** |
| **Vice-Presidential Removal (`Art. 67(b)`)** | **YES** (RS Effective + LS Simple) | **YES** (RS Effective + LS Simple) | **NO** | **NO** | **NO** |

> [!remember]
> **Memory Anchor — The Two Swaps & Four Majorities:**
> - **The Nominated–MLA Swap:** For the **President**, *"MLAs put him IN (`Art. 54`), Nominated MPs can put him OUT (`Art. 61`)."* For the **Vice-President**, *"Only Parliament (ALL MPs, elected + nominated) puts him IN (`Art. 66`) and puts him OUT (`Art. 67(b)`)."*
> - **The Impeachment Super-Majority:** `Article 61` is the **ONLY** provision in the Indian Constitution requiring **2/3rd of the TOTAL membership** of each House—even stricter than amending the Constitution under `Article 368`!

## 2. Powers and Functions of the President

Although the Indian Constitution casts the President as a constitutional head who acts overwhelmingly on the aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers (`Article 74(1)`), every thread of sovereign Union authority is constitutionally anchored in the President's office. To grasp both the legal breadth of the presidency and the exact checks embedded in each domain, standard constitutional scholarship (M. Laxmikanth, D.D. Basu) classifies the President's powers and functions into **seven distinct categories**: **Executive, Legislative, Financial, Judicial, Diplomatic, Military, and Emergency powers**.

### Executive Powers

Under **`Article 53(1)`**, the executive power of the Union is vested in the President and shall be exercised by them either directly or through officers subordinate to them in accordance with the Constitution. The Union's executive power is co-extensive with Parliament's legislative competence (`Article 73`). The President's specific executive functions span five core spheres:

1. **Formal Executive Actions and Business Rules (`Article 77`):**
   - Every executive action of the Government of India is formally expressed to be taken **in the name of the President** (`Art. 77(1)`).
   - The President frames rules specifying how orders and instruments made in their name shall be authenticated (`Art. 77(2)`), insulating duly authenticated orders from challenges on the ground that they were not personally signed by the President.
   - The President makes **Rules for the More Convenient Transaction of the Business of the Government of India** and for the **allocation of such business among Ministers** (`Art. 77(3)` — operationalised via the *Government of India (Allocation of Business) Rules, 1961* and *Transaction of Business Rules, 1961*).

2. **Constitutional Channel of Communication (`Article 78`):**
   - The President can require the **Prime Minister** to: (a) communicate all decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to Union administration and proposals for legislation; (b) furnish such administrative or legislative information as the President calls for; and (c) submit for the consideration of the Council of Ministers any matter on which a decision has been taken by an individual Minister but which has **not been considered by the Council** (reinforcing collective cabinet responsibility).

3. **Key Constitutional Appointments:**
   - **Union Political Executive:** Appoints the **Prime Minister** and, on the Prime Minister's advice, the **other Ministers** (`Art. 75(1)`), who hold office during the President's pleasure (`Art. 75(2)`).
   - **Chief Legal Officer:** Appoints the **Attorney General of India** (`Art. 76`), determines their remuneration, and the AG holds office during the President's pleasure.
   - **Independent Constitutional Functionaries:** Appoints the **Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG)** (`Art. 148`), the **Chief Election Commissioner and other Election Commissioners** (`Art. 324`, regulated by the *CEC and Other ECs Act, 2023*), the **Chairman and members of the Union Public Service Commission (UPSC)** (`Art. 316`), the **Governors of States** (`Art. 155`), and the **Chairman and members of the Finance Commission** (`Art. 280`).
   - **Social-Justice, Linguistic & Federal Commissions:** Appoints the **National Commission for Scheduled Castes (`Art. 338`)**, the **National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (`Art. 338A`)**, the **National Commission for Backward Classes (`Art. 338B`, 102nd Amendment 2018)**, the **Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (`Art. 350B`)**, the **Official Language Commission (`Art. 344`)**, and constitutes an **Inter-State Council (`Art. 263`)** to promote Centre–State and inter-state cooperation.
   - *(Note on Judicial Appointments: Appoints the Chief Justice and Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts—grouped under Judicial Powers below.)*

4. **Administration of Union Territories (`Articles 239 & 240`):**
   - Directly administers every **Union Territory** through an Administrator (designated Lieutenant-Governor, Chief Commissioner, or Administrator) appointed by the President (`Art. 239`).
   - Under **`Article 240`**, exercises a unique **legislative-cum-executive regulation-making power** for the peace, progress, and good government of **Andaman & Nicobar Islands**, **Lakshadweep**, **Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu**, and—only when its Assembly is suspended or dissolved—**Puducherry** (a regulation under `Art. 240` has the same force as an Act of Parliament and can repeal or amend any parliamentary law applying to that UT!).

5. **Scheduled Areas and Tribal Administration (`Fifth Schedule` & `Article 339`):**
   - Empowered to declare any area as a **Scheduled Area** (and alter its boundaries after consulting the State Governor), possesses executive powers regarding the administration of Scheduled Areas and Tribal Areas, and appoints a Commission under `Article 339(1)` to report on the administration of Scheduled Areas and the welfare of Scheduled Tribes.

### Legislative Powers

Under **`Article 79`**, the Parliament of India consists of **three** constituent parts: the **President**, the **Council of States (Rajya Sabha)**, and the **House of the People (Lok Sabha)**. Even though the President is **not a member of either House** and does not sit inside parliamentary proceedings, no Bill passed by both Houses becomes law without Presidential assent. The President's legislative functions are extensive:

1. **Summoning, Proroguing, Dissolving and Joint Sittings (`Articles 85 & 108`):**
   - **Summons** each House of Parliament from time to time (ensuring that no more than **six months** intervene between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next), **prorogues** either House (ending a session), and **dissolves the Lok Sabha** (`Art. 85`; the Rajya Sabha is a permanent House and is never dissolved). *(Prelims distinction: **Adjournment**—suspending a sitting for hours or days—is done by the **Presiding Officer**, NEVER by the President!)*
   - **Summons a Joint Sitting** of both Houses (`Article 108`) to resolve legislative deadlocks over Ordinary Bills or Financial Bills Category I (never Money Bills or Constitutional Amendment Bills). *(Prelims trap: The President **summons** the Joint Sitting, but the **Speaker of the Lok Sabha presides** over it under `Art. 118(4)`!)*

2. **Addressing Parliament and Sending Messages (`Articles 86 & 87`):**
   - **`Article 86`:** May address either House or both Houses assembled together, and may send **messages** to either House with respect to a pending Bill or otherwise.
   - **`Article 87` (Mandatory Special Address):** At the commencement of the **first session after each general election** to the Lok Sabha AND at the commencement of the **first session of each year**, the President **must address both Houses assembled together** and inform Parliament of the causes of its summons. Prepared by the Union Cabinet, this Special Address outlines government policy for the coming year and is voted upon via the **Motion of Thanks** (defeat of the Motion of Thanks in the Lok Sabha amounts to defeat of the government!).

3. **Temporary Presiding Appointments:**
   - Appoints any member of the **Lok Sabha** to preside over its proceedings when the offices of **both the Speaker and the Deputy Speaker** fall vacant (`Art. 95(1)`), and appoints the **Speaker *pro tem*** after a general election to administer oaths to newly elected MPs.
   - Likewise appoints any member of the **Rajya Sabha** to preside when the offices of **both the Chairman and the Deputy Chairman** are vacant (`Art. 91(1)`).

4. **Nominations to Parliament (`Article 80(3)` and the 104th Amendment):**
   - Nominates **12 members to the Rajya Sabha** from among persons having special knowledge or practical experience in **four** specific fields: **Literature, Science, Art, and Social Service**. *(High-yield Prelims contrast: State Governors nominate 1/6th of Legislative Council members from **five** fields—adding **"Cooperative Movement"** under `Art. 171(5)`, which is ABSENT from the President's Rajya Sabha list!)*
   - **End of Anglo-Indian Nominations (`104th Amendment Act, 2019`):** Originally, `Article 331` empowered the President to nominate up to **two members from the Anglo-Indian community** to the Lok Sabha if inadequately represented. The **104th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019** (effective **25 January 2020**) extended SC/ST legislative reservations by ten years (until 2030) while letting the Anglo-Indian nomination provision in both the Lok Sabha (`Art. 331`) and State Assemblies (`Art. 333`) **lapse permanently**.

5. **Deciding Questions of MP Disqualification (`Article 103`):**
   - Decides questions regarding whether a sitting MP has incurred any of the **general disqualifications** enumerated in `Article 102(1)` (office of profit, unsound mind, undischarged insolvent, loss of citizenship, or statutory RPA 1951 disqualification) **after obtaining the opinion of the Election Commission of India**, and **the President MUST act in accordance with the ECI's opinion** (`Art. 103(2)` — binding consultation!).
   - *(Crucial Distinction: Questions of disqualification on the ground of **defection under the Tenth Schedule (`Art. 102(2)`)** are decided by the **Chairman (RS) or Speaker (LS)**, NOT by the President!)*

6. **Prior Recommendation or Previous Sanction for Specific Bills:**
   Certain sensitive Bills **cannot be introduced or moved** without the **prior recommendation/sanction of the President**:
   - **Money Bills (`Art. 117(1)` read with `Art. 110`)** and **Financial Bills Category I (`Art. 117(1)`)**;
   - **Financial Bills Category II (`Art. 117(3)`)** — *subtle Prelims nuance:* prior Presidential recommendation is NOT required at the **introduction** stage, ONLY at the **consideration** stage in either House!
   - **State Reorganisation Bills (`Article 3`)** involving formation of new States or alteration of boundaries, areas, or names of existing States;
   - **Taxation Bills Affecting State Interests (`Article 274`)** — any Bill imposing/varying any tax in which States are interested, altering the meaning of "agricultural income", affecting principles of Union–State tax distribution, or imposing a Union surcharge under `Art. 271`;
   - **State Trade-Restriction Bills (`Article 304(b)` Proviso)** — any State Bill imposing reasonable public-interest restrictions on freedom of trade, commerce, or intercourse within or across that State requires the **previous sanction of the President** before introduction in the State Legislature!
   - *(Note: Under `Article 255`, the absence of prior recommendation/sanction is a procedural defect cured if the Bill subsequently receives Presidential assent. Also note: **Constitutional Amendment Bills (`Art. 368`)** NEVER require prior Presidential permission!)*

7. **Assent, Ordinances and Laying Constitutional Reports:**
   - Gives assent to, withholds assent from, or returns Parliamentary Bills (`Art. 111`) and Reserved State Bills (`Art. 201`), and promulgates **Ordinances (`Art. 123`)** during parliamentary recess (analysed in depth in Section 3).
   - Causes the reports of key independent constitutional bodies to be **laid before Parliament**: **CAG** (`Art. 151`), **UPSC** (`Art. 323`), **Finance Commission** (`Art. 281`), **NCSC** (`Art. 338`), **NCST** (`Art. 338A`), **NCBC** (`Art. 338B`), and the **Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities** (`Art. 350B`).

> [!prelims]
> **High-Yield Prelims Traps on Presidential Legislative Powers:**
> - **Adjournment vs Prorogation:** The **Presiding Officer** (Speaker/Chairman) adjourns a sitting; the **President** summons, prorogues, and dissolves Lok Sabha (`Art. 85`).
> - **Rajya Sabha vs Legislative Council Nominations:** President nominates 12 RS MPs from **4 fields** (Literature, Science, Art, Social Service — **NO "Cooperative Movement"**!). Anglo-Indian nominations (`Art. 331`) **ended on 25 Jan 2020** (`104th Amend`).
> - **Disqualification Split:** `Art. 102(1)` non-defection disqualifications → **President (bound by ECI, `Art. 103`)**. Tenth Schedule defection (`Art. 102(2)`) → **Speaker/Chairman** (no Presidential role).
> - **Prior Recommendation Nuances:** Required for Money Bills, Finance Bill-I, `Art. 3` Bills, `Art. 274` Bills, and `Art. 304(b)` State Bills; for **Finance Bill-II (`Art. 117(3)`)**, required only at **consideration**, not introduction; **NOT required** for **Constitutional Amendment Bills (`Art. 368`)**.

### Financial Powers

Because executive accountability over the public purse is the bedrock of parliamentary democracy, the Constitution routes all major Union financial initiatives through the President:

1. **Prior Recommendation for Money Bills (`Article 117(1)`):** No **Money Bill** (`Art. 110`) can be introduced in the Lok Sabha except on the prior recommendation of the President (and can never be introduced in the Rajya Sabha).
2. **Laying the Annual Financial Statement (`Article 112`):** The President causes to be laid before both Houses of Parliament every financial year the **Annual Financial Statement** (the Union Budget—note that the word *"Budget"* appears nowhere in the Constitution!), showing estimated receipts and expenditure of the Government of India.
3. **Recommendation for Demands for Grants (`Article 113(3)`):** No **Demand for a Grant** can be made in the Lok Sabha except on the recommendation of the President—preventing private members from proposing unplanned public expenditure or inflating ministerial outlays (private members may only move cut motions to *reduce* demands).
4. **Contingency Fund of India (`Article 267(1)`):** Placed strictly at the disposal of the President (operated on their behalf by the Union Finance Secretary under the *Contingency Fund of India Act, 1950*; corpus enhanced to **₹30,000 crore** via the Finance Act, 2021) to make urgent advances for **unforeseen expenditure** pending retrospective parliamentary authorisation.
5. **Constituting the Finance Commission (`Article 280`):** Constitutes a quasi-judicial **Finance Commission** every **five years** (or at such earlier time as deemed necessary) to recommend the vertical and horizontal distribution of net tax proceeds between the Centre and States and principles governing `Article 275` grants-in-aid (most recently, the **16th Finance Commission** chaired by Dr. Arvind Panagariya, constituted in December 2023 for the 2026–2031 award period).

### Judicial, Diplomatic, Military and Emergency Powers

#### Judicial Powers

1. **Appointment of the Higher Judiciary (`Articles 124 & 217`):** Appoints the **Chief Justice of India** and Judges of the **Supreme Court** (`Art. 124(2)`), as well as the Chief Justices and Judges of all **High Courts** (`Art. 217(1)`), and transfers High Court Judges (`Art. 222`). Following the **Second Judges Case (1993)**, **Third Judges Case (1998)**, and **Fourth Judges Case / NJAC Judgment (2015)**, these appointments are made on the binding recommendation of the **Supreme Court Collegium**.
2. **Advisory Jurisdiction Reference (`Article 143`):** Empowered to seek the opinion of the Supreme Court on any question of law or fact of public importance that has arisen or is likely to arise. Two subtle clauses must be distinguished for Prelims:
   - **`Article 143(1)` (General Public-Importance Questions):** The Supreme Court **may** tender or decline to tender its opinion (discretionary for the Court — e.g., SC declined to opine in the *Ayodhya / Ismail Faruqui Reference*, 1994, whereas it answered President Droupadi Murmu's 14-question reference in ***Special Reference No. 1 of 2025*** on 20 November 2025).
   - **`Article 143(2)` (Pre-Constitution Treaties, Agreements & Covenants):** In disputes arising out of pre-Constitution princely treaties excluded from `Article 131`, the Supreme Court **MUST** tender its opinion.
   - **Common Rule:** Under **BOTH** `143(1)` and `143(2)`, the Supreme Court's opinion is purely **advisory and NOT legally binding** on the President—though in practice, Constitution Bench advisory opinions carry immense persuasive and constitutional authority.
3. **Pardoning Power (`Article 72`):** Confers comprehensive clemency authority across Union offences, court-martial sentences, and all death sentences (examined exhaustively in Section 3).

#### Diplomatic Powers

All **international treaties, conventions, and diplomatic agreements** are negotiated and concluded **on behalf of the President** (although domestic implementation affecting citizens' rights, financial obligations, or territorial boundaries requires Parliamentary legislation under `Article 253` and, for territorial cession, an `Article 368` amendment per *Berubari Union*, 1960). The President represents the Republic of India in international forums and state visits, accredits Indian Ambassadors and High Commissioners abroad, and formally receives the Letters of Credence of foreign diplomats posted to New Delhi.

#### Military Powers

Under **`Article 53(2)`**, the **supreme command of the Defence Forces of the Union** is vested in the President—making the President the **Supreme Commander** of the Indian Army, Navy, and Air Force. In this capacity, the President appoints the **Chiefs of the Army, Navy, and Air Force** (as well as the **Chief of Defence Staff**) and formally declares **war** or concludes **peace**. Crucially, however, `Article 53(2)` explicitly adds that the exercise of supreme command **"shall be regulated by law"**—meaning Parliament retains full statutory control over defence organisation, funding, and war-making, preventing the Indian President from wielding unilateral military command comparable to the American Commander-in-Chief.

#### Emergency Powers (Part XVIII)

To safeguard India's sovereignty, unity, constitutional order, and financial stability during extraordinary crises, **Part XVIII (`Articles 352` to `360`)** equips the President with three potent Emergency proclamations *(analysed exhaustively in Chapter 11)*:
1. **National Emergency (`Article 352`):** Proclaimed on grounds of **war, external aggression, or armed rebellion** (44th Amendment replaced "internal disturbance"), strictly upon the **written recommendation of the Union Cabinet (`Art. 352(3)`)**—the **only** place in the Constitution mentioning the word "Cabinet."
2. **President's Rule / Failure of Constitutional Machinery in States (`Articles 356 & 365`):** Proclaimed upon a Governor's report or *"otherwise"* (including non-compliance with Union directions under `Art. 365`), subject to strict judicial review and floor-test safeguards laid down in ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994)**.
3. **Financial Emergency (`Article 360`):** Proclaimed when the financial stability or credit of India or any part of it is threatened (never invoked since 1950); empowers salary reductions even of Supreme Court and High Court Judges.

## 3. Veto Powers, Ordinance-Making and Pardoning Powers

Three signature constitutional powers elevate the Indian President far above a purely ornamental figurehead and generate the highest volume of UPSC Prelims traps and Mains analytical questions: the **Veto Power** (`Articles 111` and `201`), the **Ordinance-Making Power** (`Article 123`), and the **Pardoning Power** (`Article 72`). Each sits at a delicate intersection of legislative supremacy, executive urgency, federalism, and judicial review.

### Veto Powers of the President (Articles 111 and 201)

A Bill passed by both Houses of Parliament cannot become an Act unless it receives the **assent of the President**. Under **`Article 111`**, when a Bill is presented for assent, the President has **three constitutional alternatives**:
1. **Give assent** to the Bill (converting it immediately into an Act of Parliament);
2. **Withhold assent** to the Bill (causing the Bill to lapse); or
3. **Return the Bill** (provided it is **NOT a Money Bill**) to the Houses with a message requesting reconsideration of the whole Bill or specified provisions (including suggested amendments). If Parliament passes the Bill again—with or without amendments—and presents it to the President, **the President MUST give assent** (*"shall not withhold assent therefrom"*).

Constitutional theory confers the Presidential veto with a **two-fold institutional object**: (a) to prevent **hasty and ill-considered legislation** by Parliament; and (b) to check **manifestly unconstitutional legislation**. Globally, political scientists classify executive vetoes into **four categories**: **Absolute Veto**, **Qualified Veto** (overridden by a legislative super-majority, e.g., 2/3rd of both Houses of the US Congress), **Suspensive Veto** (overridden by an ordinary legislative majority, e.g., France and India), and **Pocket Veto** (taking no action indefinitely). Critically, **the Indian President possesses THREE vetoes—Absolute, Suspensive, and Pocket—and has NO Qualified Veto!**

#### Absolute Veto

**Absolute Veto** is the power to **withhold assent** to a Bill passed by the legislature, terminating the Bill permanently so that it never enters the statute book. In parliamentary practice, the President exercises the Absolute Veto in **two specific situations**:
- **Private Member's Bills:** Bills introduced by any Member of Parliament who is not a Minister (where the Cabinet advises withholding assent).
- **Government Bills After Cabinet Fall:** When the Cabinet that piloted a Bill resigns after parliamentary passage but before Presidential assent, and the **new Cabinet advises the President not to assent**.

**Two Verified Historical Precedents:**
1. **1954 — *PEPSU Appropriation Bill*:** President **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** withheld assent because the Bill had been passed by Parliament while Patiala and East Punjab States Union (PEPSU) was under President's Rule, but President's Rule was revoked before the Bill reached the President for assent.
2. **1991 — *Salary, Allowances and Pension of Members of Parliament (Amendment) Bill*:** President **R. Venkataraman** withheld assent because the outgoing Lok Sabha had hurried the Bill through on its final day **without obtaining the President's mandatory prior recommendation** under `Article 117(1)`.

#### Suspensive Veto

**Suspensive Veto** is exercised when the President **returns a Bill** to Parliament for reconsideration. It is termed "suspensive" because Parliament can **override it simply by re-passing the Bill by an ordinary majority** (with or without accepting the President's suggested changes), upon which Presidential assent becomes constitutionally mandatory.

- **Critical Exclusion — Money Bills:** The President **CANNOT exercise Suspensive Veto over a Money Bill** (`Art. 111` proviso explicitly excludes Money Bills from return). For a Money Bill, the President may only give assent or withhold assent—and because Money Bills are introduced only with the President's prior recommendation (`Art. 117(1)`), the President normally grants assent.
- **Landmark Precedent (2006):** In May 2006, President **Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam** exercised the Suspensive Veto to return the ***Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Amendment Bill, 2006*** (the "Office of Profit Bill"), raising concerns about retroactive exemptions and unequal application across States. When Parliament re-passed the Bill unaltered in July 2006, President Kalam duly gave his assent as mandated by `Article 111`.

#### Pocket Veto

**Pocket Veto** occurs when the President **neither ratifies nor rejects nor returns a Bill**, simply keeping it pending on their desk indefinitely. This potent constitutional silence exists because **`Article 111` prescribes NO time limit** within which the President must act upon a Bill! (Directly tested in **UPSC Prelims 2023 Q36**, where Statement 3 falsely claimed the Constitution prescribes a time limit!) Constitutional scholars famously remark that **"the pocket of the Indian President is bigger than that of the American President"**—because the US President must return a Bill within **10 days** (excluding Sundays) unless Congress adjourns within those 10 days, whereas the Indian President faces **zero textual deadline**.

- **Landmark Precedent (1986):** President **Giani Zail Singh** exercised the Pocket Veto over the ***Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986*** (passed by Rajiv Gandhi's government), which authorised sweeping interception of private mail and drew nationwide criticism as an assault on press freedom and privacy. Zail Singh kept the Bill pending until demitting office in 1987; three years later, in **1989**, his successor **R. Venkataraman** returned it to Parliament for reconsideration, and the newly elected V.P. Singh government dropped the Bill.

#### Complete Absence of Veto Over Constitutional Amendment Bills

Under **`Article 368(2)`**, as amended by the **24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971**, when a Constitutional Amendment Bill duly passed by both Houses (and ratified by half the States where required) is presented to the President, **"he shall give his assent to the Bill."** Consequently, the President enjoys **NO veto power whatsoever—neither Absolute, nor Suspensive, nor Pocket—over Constitutional Amendment Bills**.

#### Presidential Veto Over Reserved State Bills (Article 201)

India's asymmetric federalism extends Presidential veto control into State legislation. Under **`Article 200`**, when a Bill passed by a State Legislature reaches the Governor, the Governor may **reserve the Bill for the consideration of the President** (reservation is **mandatory** whenever a State Bill endangers the constitutional position of the High Court, and optional in cases of Concurrent List repugnancy under `Art. 254(2)`, `Art. 31A/31C`, or national interest).

Once a State Bill is reserved, the Governor's role ceases and **`Article 201`** vests exclusive decision-making in the President, who holds **three options**:
1. **Give assent** to the Bill;
2. **Withhold assent** to the Bill; or
3. **Direct the Governor to return the Bill** (if it is **NOT a Money Bill**) to the State Legislature with a message for reconsideration.

When returned by the President, the State Legislature must reconsider the Bill within a period of **six months**. Here lies one of the sharpest **federal asymmetries** and UPSC Prelims traps in the entire Constitution: **even if the State Legislature re-passes the Bill (with or without amendments) within six months and presents it again to the President, the President is NOT bound to give assent!** Why? Because **`Article 201` deliberately omits** the binding phrase *"shall not withhold assent therefrom"* that appears in both `Article 111` (for Parliamentary Bills) and the first proviso to `Article 200` (for Governors). Thus, the President can reject a State Bill even on the second round—and because `Article 201` likewise specifies no time limit, the President also enjoys an indefinite **Pocket Veto over State Bills**. State Money Bills reserved under `Article 200` cannot be returned for reconsideration; the President may only assent or withhold assent.

| Bill Category | Constitutional Article | Absolute Veto | Suspensive Veto | Pocket Veto | Governing Constitutional Rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ordinary Bill (Parliament)** | `Article 111` | **Yes** (withhold) | **Yes** (repassage by simple majority **binds** President) | **Yes** (no time limit) | Must assent if Parliament passes it a second time |
| **Money Bill (Parliament)** | `Articles 110 & 111` | **Yes** (technically; rarely used) | **NO** (expressly barred) | **Yes** (no time limit) | Cannot be returned for reconsideration; prior recommendation required (`Art. 117(1)`) |
| **Constitutional Amendment Bill** | `Article 368(2)` | **NO** | **NO** | **NO** | **24th Amendment (1971)** made Presidential assent **strictly mandatory** |
| **Reserved State Ordinary Bill** | `Article 201` | **Yes** | **Yes — BUT repassage DOES NOT bind President!** | **Yes** (no time limit) | State Legislature has **6 months** to reconsider; `Art. 201` omits *"shall not withhold assent"* |
| **Reserved State Money Bill** | `Article 201` | **Yes** | **NO** (barred) | **Yes** (no time limit) | President can only assent or withhold assent; cannot direct return |

> [!news]
> ***Special Reference No. 1 of 2025* (`2025 INSC 1333`, 20 November 2025) — Supreme Court's Landmark 5-Judge Opinion on Articles 200 & 201:**
> - **Trigger (*Tamil Nadu Governor Case*, 8 April 2025):** Responding to prolonged gubernatorial delays over ten Tamil Nadu Assembly Bills, a two-judge Bench (*State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu*) prescribed fixed constitutional timelines—1 to 3 months for Governors under `Art. 200` and **3 months for the President under `Art. 201`**—and invoked `Article 142` to declare **"deemed assent"** to the pending Bills.
> - **Presidential Reference (`Article 143(1)`):** On **13 May 2025**, President **Droupadi Murmu** referred **14 constitutional questions** to the Supreme Court concerning `Articles 200` and `201`, separation of powers, justiciability, and `Article 142`.
> - **Unanimous 5-Judge Constitution Bench Opinion (`20 November 2025`):** Comprising **CJI B.R. Gavai** and Justices **Surya Kant, Vikram Nath, P.S. Narasimha, and Atul S. Chandurkar**, the Court issued a landmark single opinion holding:
>   1. **No Judicially Imposed Timelines:** Because `Articles 200` and `201` deliberately preserve constitutional elasticity across polycentric federal situations, courts **cannot impose rigid numerical timelines** on either the Governor (`Art. 200`) or the President (`Art. 201`)—terming the April 2025 `Art. 201` timeline remarks non-binding *obiter*.
>   2. **No "Deemed Assent" via `Article 142`:** Judicial "deemed assent" substitutes the Court for the Governor or President, directly breaching **Separation of Powers (Basic Structure)**.
>   3. **Non-Justiciability Before Law Takes Effect:** The merits of decisions under `Articles 200` and `201` are **non-justiciable** anterior to a Bill becoming law; nor is the President legally required to invoke `Article 143` whenever a reserved Bill raises constitutional doubts.
>   4. **No Indefinite "Withholding Simpliciter" & Limited Mandamus:** Crucially balancing federal accountability, the Bench affirmed that a Governor **cannot withhold assent simpliciter** without returning the Bill to initiate the constitutional dialogue—and in glaring cases of **prolonged, unexplained, indefinite inaction**, Constitutional Courts may issue a **limited mandamus** directing discharge of constitutional duty within a reasonable time without commenting on merits. The Bench also underscored that `Article 201`'s omission of *"shall not withhold therefrom"* remains a conscious textual distinction.

> [!debate]
> **Federal Friction Over Reserved State Bills — Should Article 201 Carry a Constitutional Timeline?**
> - **Case for Statutory/Constitutional Timeframes:** Both the **Sarkaria Commission (1988)** and the **Punchhi Commission (2010)** flagged indefinite central sit-ons over reserved State Bills as a silent threat to state legislative autonomy—with Punchhi explicitly recommending a **6-month outer ceiling** for Presidential decisions under `Article 201` and Sarkaria urging that reasons for withholding assent be communicated to State Governments to promote cooperative federalism.
> - **Case for Constitutional Elasticity:** As reaffirmed in ***Special Reference No. 1 of 2025* (Nov 2025)**, reserved Bills frequently touch inter-state river rights, national security, uniform Concurrent List codes, or constitutional validity, requiring extensive inter-ministerial and inter-state consultations that cannot be squeezed into a mechanical stopwatch.
> - **Balanced Mains Synthesis:** Rather than rigid judicial timelines or unchecked executive silence, India needs **institutionalised Centre–State consultation protocols** through a revitalised **Inter-State Council (`Art. 263`)**, transparent tracking of pending State Bills in the Ministry of Home Affairs, and principled adherence to the *Special Reference* dialogic standard.

### Ordinance-Making Power (Article 123)

Described by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar as a necessary mechanism to deal with urgent, unforeseen situations when the legislature is not sitting, **`Article 123`** confers upon the President the **most consequential legislative power** in the Constitution: the authority to promulgate **Ordinances** during the recess of Parliament. Structurally descended from the **Indian Councils Act, 1861** and **Section 42 of the Government of India Act, 1935**, executive ordinance-making is **unique among major constitutional democracies**—it exists **neither in the United Kingdom nor in the United States**! (Correspondingly, State Governors hold parallel ordinance power under `Article 213`.)

#### Four Strict Constitutional Limitations on Article 123

Far from being a parallel source of executive legislation, `Article 123` hems in the President's ordinance power with **four rigid constitutional boundaries**:

1. **Parliamentary Recess Precondition (`Art. 123(1)`):** An ordinance can be promulgated **ONLY when BOTH Houses of Parliament are NOT in session, OR when EITHER of the two Houses is NOT in session**.
   - *Classic UPSC Prelims Trap:* An ordinance promulgated when **only ONE House is in session** is **COMPLETELY VALID**, because a statute requires passage by **both** Houses—so Parliament cannot enact a law if one House is prorogued! Conversely, an ordinance issued while **BOTH Houses are in session is VOID** (*ab initio* unconstitutional).
2. **Satisfaction of Urgent Necessity (`Art. 123(1)`):** The President (acting strictly on the aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers, NOT personal discretion!) must be satisfied that *"circumstances exist which render it necessary for him to take immediate action."*
   - **Judicial Review Arc:** In ***R.C. Cooper v. Union of India* (1970, Bank Nationalisation case)**, the Supreme Court held that Presidential satisfaction is **justiciable on grounds of *mala fides*** or where the executive prorogues a House deliberately to bypass parliamentary debate. During the Emergency, the **38th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975** made Presidential satisfaction *"final and conclusive and beyond judicial review"*; subsequently, the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978** **deleted that ouster clause**, permanently restoring judicial review of *mala fide* ordinances!
3. **Co-Extensive Legislative Competence & Fundamental Rights Limits (`Art. 123(2)` & `(3)`):** An ordinance carries the **exact same force and effect as an Act of Parliament**, and therefore faces the **exact same constitutional limitations**:
   - It can be promulgated **only on subjects where Parliament can legislate** (Union List, Concurrent List, and State List exclusively during Emergency/President's Rule).
   - It is a **"law" within the meaning of `Article 13(3)`** (affirmed in ***A.K. Roy v. Union of India*, 1982**) and **cannot abridge or take away any Fundamental Right**.
   - **Crucial Prelims Trap — Cannot Amend the Constitution:** An ordinance **CAN NEVER be used to amend the Constitution**, because `Article 368` requires a Constitutional Amendment Bill passed by a special majority of both Houses.
   - **Permissible Scope:** Like an Act of Parliament, an ordinance **can operate retrospectively**, **can modify or repeal any Act of Parliament** or previous ordinance, **can alter or amend a tax law**, and **can be withdrawn at any time** by the President (on CoM advice).
4. **Mandatory Laying and Six-Week Lifespan (`Art. 123(2)`):** Every ordinance promulgated during recess **MUST be laid before BOTH Houses of Parliament** when it reassembles. Thereafter, **three outcomes** are possible:
   - **Approved by both Houses** → becomes a permanent **Act of Parliament**;
   - **Disapproved by resolutions passed by BOTH Houses** → ceases to operate **immediately** (even before six weeks expire);
   - **No parliamentary action taken** → automatically **lapses at the expiration of SIX WEEKS from the reassembly of Parliament**. If the two Houses are summoned to reassemble on **different dates**, the six-week period is calculated from the **LATER of those two dates**!
   - **Maximum Possible Unapproved Life:** Because `Article 85` caps the maximum gap between two sessions of Parliament at **six months**, the maximum possible lifespan of an ordinance without parliamentary approval is **six months and six weeks**!

```flow
# The Article 123 Ordinance Lifecycle and Constitutional Guardrails
Trigger Condition | Both Houses OR Either House of Parliament NOT in session (VOID if both sit!)
Promulgation | President issues Ordinance on binding CoM aid & advice; co-extensive with Parliament; NO Art. 368 amendments; subject to Art. 13 FRs
Reassembly & Mandatory Laying | Must be laid before BOTH Houses on reassembly (*Krishna Kumar Singh* 2017: failure = constitutional fraud)
Approved by Both Houses | Converted into a permanent Act of Parliament || Disapproved by Both Houses | Ceases immediately before 6 weeks || No Action / Expiry of 6 Weeks | Lapses automatically (6 wks from LATER House's reassembly; max life 6 mo + 6 wks)
```

#### Judicial Curb on "Ordinance Raj": From D.C. Wadhwa (1987) to Krishna Kumar Singh (2017)

Over decades, both Union and State executives repeatedly abused ordinance powers—re-promulgating lapsed ordinances mechanically during inter-session gaps to govern without facing legislative scrutiny. The Supreme Court erected formidable constitutional barriers against this **"Ordinance Raj"**:

- ***A.K. Roy v. Union of India* (1982):** Upheld the validity of the National Security Ordinance, 1980, clarifying that an ordinance is "law" under `Article 13` and `Article 21`, while cautioning that the President's power should be used sparingly and never to usurp legislative authority.
- ***D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar* (1987, 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** Exposing the astonishing practice of Bihar re-promulgating **256 ordinances** between 1967 and 1981 (some kept alive for **14 years** without ever approaching the Assembly!), the Supreme Court struck down mechanical re-promulgation as a **"colourable exercise of power"** and a **"fraud on the Constitution"**—permitting re-promulgation only in rare exceptions where the legislature had genuinely too little time to consider the Bill.
- ***Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar* (2 January 2017, 7-Judge Constitution Bench):** Widely regarded as the definitive judgment on `Articles 123` and `213`, a seven-judge Bench decisively plugged the final loopholes left by earlier rulings (*T. Venkata Reddy*, 1985).

> "Re-promulgation of ordinances is a fraud on the Constitution and a sub-version of democratic legislative processes, as laid down in the judgment of the Constitution Bench in D C Wadhwa." — Justice D.Y. Chandrachud, *Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar*, 2 January 2017

> [!case]
> ***Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar* (2 January 2017 — 7-Judge Constitution Bench, 6–1 Majority, lead opinion by Justice D.Y. Chandrachud):**
> - **Facts:** Bihar issued a series of seven successive ordinances between 1989 and 1992 taking over 429 private Sanskrit schools without ever laying them before the State Legislature. Employees claimed permanent salaries and status even after the final ordinance lapsed, citing *T. Venkata Reddy* (1985) ("enduring rights").
> - **Four Landmark Holdings:**
>   1. **Mandatory Duty to Lay:** Placing an ordinance before Parliament (`Art. 123`) or the State Legislature (`Art. 213`) upon reassembly is a **mandatory constitutional obligation**—deliberate failure to lay constitutes a serious constitutional abuse.
>   2. **Re-Promulgation = Constitutional Fraud:** Re-promulgating ordinances is an unconstitutional subversion of legislative supremacy and a **fraud on the Constitution** (tightening *D.C. Wadhwa* by removing its permissive "lack of time" escape hatch!).
>   3. **No Automatic "Enduring Rights" After Lapse (Overruling *T. Venkata Reddy*):** Rights, obligations, or privileges created by an ordinance do **NOT** survive automatically once it lapses; they survive **ONLY** if undoing them would violate **overwhelming public interest, constitutional necessity, or irreversibility**.
>   4. **Expanded Judicial Review:** Satisfaction of the President/Governor is not immune from scrutiny—courts may examine whether the power was exercised for an oblique motive or to circumvent the legislature.

**Mains Evaluation (UPSC Mains 2015 GS-2 Q5 — Should `Article 123` Be Repealed?):** Critics rightly note that frequent recourse to ordinances—averaging over 10 per year nationally in several decades, including high-stakes measures such as the **three Farm Ordinances (2020)** and the **GNCTD Services Ordinance (2023)**—erodes the spirit of Separation of Powers by bypassing Standing Committee scrutiny and public consultation. Yet outright **repeal of `Article 123` is neither practical nor advisable**: genuine emergencies (sudden economic shocks, border/security crises, urgent statutory deadlines, or judicial strike-downs while Parliament is prorogued) demand swift, reversible legal responses. The constitutional remedy lies not in abolition, but in **strict enforcement of *Krishna Kumar Singh* (2017)**, requiring a **statement of urgent circumstances** when laying every ordinance, capping re-promulgation completely, and increasing Parliamentary sitting days (from ~60–70 days toward the NCRWC-recommended **100–120 days/year**) so legislation happens primarily inside the chamber.

### Pardoning Power of the President (Article 72)

**`Article 72`** empowers the President to grant pardons, reprieves, respites, or remissions of punishment, or to suspend, remit, or commute the sentence of any person convicted of any offence. Resting on the humanitarian conviction that human justice is fallible, the pardoning power serves a **two-fold constitutional objective**: (1) keeping a final door open to **correct judicial errors** in the operation of law; and (2) affording **relief from a sentence the executive regards as unduly harsh**, tempering strict legalism with mercy. Importantly, `Article 72` is an **executive power of constitutional grace**—the President does **NOT** sit as a court of appeal over the Supreme Court, nor do they alter the judicial record.

#### Three Constitutional Jurisdictions of Article 72

The President's clemency jurisdiction extends across **three** distinct categories (`Article 72(1)`):
1. All cases where the punishment or sentence is by a **Court Martial** (military court);
2. All cases where the punishment or sentence is for an offence against any **Union Law** (any law relating to a matter to which the executive power of the Union extends); and
3. **ALL cases where the sentence is a sentence of death**—crucially including death sentences awarded under **State laws**!

#### The Five Modes of Presidential Clemency

UPSC Prelims routinely tests the precise legal distinctions among the **five** forms of clemency named in `Article 72`:

| Mode of Clemency | Exact Constitutional Meaning | Concrete Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| **Pardon** | Removes **BOTH the sentence AND the conviction** completely, absolving the convict from all punishments, penalties, and legal disqualifications. | Restores the convict to the legal status of an innocent citizen who never committed the offence. |
| **Commutation** | Substitutes **one form of punishment for a lighter form** (changes the *character/nature* of punishment). | Converting a **death sentence** into **rigorous imprisonment**, or rigorous imprisonment into **simple imprisonment**. |
| **Remission** | Reduces the **period/duration** of a sentence **without changing its character**. | Shortening a sentence of **two years' rigorous imprisonment** to **one year's rigorous imprisonment**. |
| **Respite** | Awards a **lesser sentence** in place of the originally awarded sentence due to some **special fact**. | Reducing punishment because of the **physical disability** of a convict or the **pregnancy** of a woman offender. |
| **Reprieve** | Grants a **temporary stay of the execution** of a sentence (especially a death sentence) for a temporary period. | Pausing a scheduled hanging to allow the convict time to seek **pardon or commutation** from the President. |

#### President (Article 72) vs Governor (Article 161)

While `Article 161` vests a parallel pardoning power in every State Governor for offences against State laws, **two decisive constitutional asymmetries** divide `Article 72` from `Article 161`:

```compare
# Pardoning Powers: President (Article 72) vs Governor (Article 161)
President of India (Article 72) | Governor of a State (Article 161)
Legislative Scope :: Offences against **Union Laws** (Union executive power) + **ALL death sentences** | Offences against **State Laws** (State executive power) only
Court Martial (Military Court) :: **YES** — Exclusive power to pardon, remit, suspend, or commute court-martial sentences | **NO POWER WHATSOEVER** over court-martial punishments or sentences
Death Sentence — Full Pardon :: **YES** — **ONLY the President** can grant a complete **pardon** in a death sentence case | **NO** — A Governor **CANNOT pardon** a death sentence (even under a State law!)
Death Sentence — Suspend/Remit/Commute :: **YES** — Complete power under `Article 72(1)(c)` | **YES (Concurrent)** — May suspend, remit, or commute a death sentence under statutory saving (`Art. 72(3)` / BNSS)
Binding Advice :: Bound by advice of **Union Council of Ministers** (`Art. 74`; *Maru Ram*, 1980) | Bound by advice of **State Council of Ministers** (`Art. 163`; ***A.G. Perarivalan*, 2022**)
Judicial Review :: Limited 5-ground review (*Epuru Sudhakar*, 2006) & delay relief (*Shatrughan Chauhan*, 2014) | Identical limited 5-ground review (*Epuru Sudhakar*, 2006) & delay relief
```

#### Landmark Jurisprudence on Article 72 and Statutory Regulation Under BNSS, 2023

Over six decades, the Supreme Court has sculpted a sophisticated body of constitutional principles governing clemency—balancing executive sovereignty with `Article 14` (non-arbitrariness) and `Article 21` (human dignity):

```cases
# Landmark Supreme Court Jurisprudence on Clemency (Articles 72 & 161)
K.M. Nanavati v. State of Bombay (1961) | 5-Judge Bench · Sub Judice Rule | Held that while an appeal is pending in the Supreme Court, Governor's suspension under `Art. 161` cannot operate contrary to Supreme Court bail/surrender rules (`Art. 142`).
Maru Ram v. Union of India (1980) | 5-Judge Bench · Binding CoM Advice | Held that `Arts. 72` and `161` are NOT personal powers—President and Governor are constitutional shorthand for the Government and **MUST act on binding ministerial advice**; public power cannot be arbitrary or *mala fide*.

Union of India (1989) | 5-Judge Bench · Scope & Oral Hearing | President may **scrutinise evidence afresh** and take a view different from the Court without altering judicial records; petitioner has **NO right to an oral hearing**; Court refused to lay down rigid guidelines; President not bound to record detailed reasons.
Epuru Sudhakar v. Govt. of Andhra Pradesh (2006) | 2-Judge Bench · 5 Grounds of Review | Settled that clemency orders are subject to **limited judicial review** on **five grounds**: (1) non-application of mind; (2) *mala fides*; (3) extraneous/wholly irrelevant considerations (e.g., political/caste bias); (4) relevant material ignored; (5) arbitrariness.

UoI (2 Sept 2025) | 2-Judge Bench · Natural-Life Remission | Affirmed that even where a statute mandates imprisonment for the *"remainder of natural life"* (e.g., `Sec. 376DA IPC` / `Sec. 70(2) BNS`), **constitutional remission remedies under `Articles 72` and `161` remain untouched and fully available**!
```

**Statutory Codification — Section 472 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023:** Effective from **1 July 2024**, the **BNSS, 2023** (replacing CrPC, 1973) introduced **`Section 472`**, codifying for the first time a structured statutory timeline for **mercy petitions in death sentence cases** (directly addressing the chronic delays flagged in *Shatrughan Chauhan* and **UPSC Mains 2014 GS-2 Q5**):
- A death-row convict (or legal heir/relative) may file a mercy petition under `Article 72` or `Article 161` within **30 days** of being informed by the Jail Superintendent of the dismissal of SC appeal/review/SLP (or expiry of appeal period after HC confirmation).
- If initially filed before the Governor, a fresh petition to the President must be submitted within **60 days** of the Governor's rejection/disposal.
- Where multiple convicts exist in the same case, the Jail Superintendent must ensure all co-convicts file within **60 days** (preventing staggered, serial mercy petitions designed to stall execution).
- Upon receiving the petition, the **Central Government** must seek State comments and transmit its recommendations to the President **"as expeditiously as possible, within 60 days"** of receiving State comments and jail records.
- **Constitutional Friction in `Section 472(7)` BNSS:** Crucially, `Section 472(7)` declares that *"No appeal shall lie in any Court against the order of the President or of the Governor made under article 72 or article 161... and any question as to the arriving of the decision... shall not be inquired into in any Court."* Constitutional scholars immediately pointed out a fatal flaw: while an ordinary *appeal* never lay against clemency, **an ordinary statute CANNOT oust Constitutional Judicial Review under `Articles 32` and `226`**, which forms part of the **Basic Structure** (*Kesavananda Bharati*; *Minerva Mills*; *Epuru Sudhakar*)!

#### Comparative Analysis: India vs USA Pardoning Powers and "Pre-Emptive Pardons"

Both **UPSC Prelims 2025 (Q18)** and **UPSC Mains 2025 (GS-2, Q3)** placed the President's pardoning power at centre stage—with Mains 2025 explicitly asking aspirants to **compare India (`Art. 72`) and the USA (`Art. II, Sec. 2`), examine limits in both nations, and explain "pre-emptive pardons."**

- **Constitutional Provision & Real Decision-Maker:** In India (`Art. 72`), the President exercises clemency **strictly on the binding advice of the Union Council of Ministers** (*Maru Ram*, 1980 — tested directly in **Prelims 2025 Q18**!). In the United States (`Article II, Section 2, Clause 1`), the President wields the power **"to grant Reprieves and Pardons for Offenses against the United States, except in Cases of Impeachment"** as an **independent, personal constitutional prerogative** (the Justice Department's Office of the Pardon Attorney is purely advisory).
- **Federal Scope:** India's President covers Union-law offences, **court-martial sentences**, and **ALL death sentences (even under State laws)**. The US President can pardon **ONLY federal offences**—never State-law convictions (which belong solely to State Governors or State Pardon Boards) and **never in cases of Impeachment**.
- **Limits & Judicial Review:** In India, clemency is checked by **binding ministerial advice** (`Art. 74`) and **limited five-ground judicial review** (*Epuru Sudhakar*, 2006) plus `Article 21` delay relief (*Shatrughan Chauhan*, 2014). In the USA, substantive judicial review over a Presidential pardon is **virtually non-existent** (*Schick v. Reed*, 1974), limited only by the impeachment bar, federal-crime restriction, and the unresolved constitutional question of whether a President can issue a **self-pardon**.
- **What Are "Pre-Emptive Pardons"?** A **pre-emptive pardon** is an act of executive clemency granted **after an alleged offence may have been committed, but BEFORE any criminal charge, indictment, prosecution, or conviction has taken place**. In the USA, the Supreme Court in ***Ex parte Garland* (1866)** upheld broad timing—enabling President **Gerald Ford** to pre-emptively pardon former President **Richard Nixon (1974)** for any Watergate offences, and President **Joe Biden (January 2025)** to grant high-profile pre-emptive pardons to public officials and family members ahead of a change in administration. **In India, pre-emptive pardons are constitutionally unavailable under `Article 72`:** the text explicitly confines clemency to *"any person **convicted** of any offence"*, meaning Presidential pardon operates strictly **post-conviction** and cannot forestall an investigation or criminal trial!

II Sec. 2` → 3 crisp differences (ministerial advice vs personal; federal + all death sentences vs federal-only/no impeachment; 5-ground judicial review vs near-zero review) → define **pre-emptive pardon** (*Ex parte Garland* 1866; Ford–Nixon 1974; Biden Jan 2025) → conclude that India's post-conviction text (*"convicted"*), `Art. 74` advice, and *Epuru Sudhakar* review guard against executive cronyism while `Sec. 472 BNSS` seeks timeliness.

## 4. Constitutional Position and Situational Discretion

Few debates in Indian constitutional history have attracted as much scholarly and political scrutiny as the true constitutional stature of the President of India: is the President merely a ceremonial "rubber stamp" bound mechanically by the Prime Minister, or an active constitutional guardian wielding latent independent powers? The answer rests on reading **`Articles 53`, `74`, `75`, and `78`** together with landmark Supreme Court jurisprudence and six decades of presidential precedent.

### Constitutional Framework: Articles 53, 74, 75 and 78

Three core constitutional Articles construct India's parliamentary dual executive:

1. **`Article 53(1)`:** Vests the executive power of the Union in the President, to be exercised directly or through subordinate officers **in accordance with the Constitution**.
2. **`Article 74(1)` & `(2)` (The Binding Aid-and-Advice Core):**
   - **Original 1950 Text:** Simply stated that *"There shall be a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to aid and advise the President in the exercise of his functions."* Although the word *"shall act in accordance"* was omitted in 1950 (relying on unwritten British parliamentary conventions), the Constituent Assembly—including Dr. Ambedkar, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, and Dr. Rajendra Prasad himself as Assembly President—unanimously affirmed that ministerial advice was binding.
   - **Judicial Crystallisation Before 1976:** Even before any amendment, the Supreme Court settled the issue in three landmark verdicts:
     - ***Rai Sahib Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab* (1955):** Held that the Indian Constitution establishes a British-style parliamentary executive where the President is a formal constitutional head while real executive authority vests in Ministers.
     - ***U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi* (1971, 5-Judge Bench):** Held that **`Article 74(1)` is mandatory**—even after the Lok Sabha is dissolved, the Council of Ministers **must continue in office** to aid and advise the President, because the President **cannot exercise executive powers personally** without a Council of Ministers!
     - ***Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab* (1974, 7-Judge Constitution Bench):** Authoritatively declared that wherever the Constitution requires the "satisfaction" of the President or Governor, it means **NOT personal satisfaction, but the constitutional satisfaction of the Council of Ministers** (Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer famously wrote that India's President is a constitutional head whose powers operate strictly on ministerial aid and advice).
   - **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976:** Put the convention into explicit black-letter text, amending `Article 74(1)` to command that the President **"shall, in the exercise of his functions, act in accordance with such advice."**
   - **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978:** Inserted the celebrated **proviso to `Article 74(1)`**, empowering the President to **require the Council of Ministers to reconsider such advice** (either generally or otherwise), while stipulating that the President **shall act in accordance with the advice tendered AFTER such reconsideration**.
   - **`Article 74(2)` (Non-Inquirability of Advice):** Bars courts from inquiring into whether any, and if so what, advice was tendered by Ministers to the President. *(Crucial nuance from ***S.R. Bommai*, 1994**: while courts cannot examine the advice itself under `Art. 74(2)`, they **CAN scrutinise the underlying material** on which that advice rested!)*
3. **`Article 75(3)` (The Democratic Linchpin):** Declares that the Council of Ministers shall be **collectively responsible to the House of the People (Lok Sabha)**. Because Ministers—not the President—answer to elected representatives for every policy and rupee spent, real executive power must reside where constitutional accountability resides.
4. **`Article 78` (The Right to Know and Warn):** Empowers the President to receive all Cabinet decisions, call for administrative/legislative information from the Prime Minister, and require an individual Minister's unconsidered decision to be placed before the entire Council of Ministers.

**The Historic Nehru–Rajendra Prasad Debate (1950–1960):** Early in the Republic, India's first President **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** twice tested the boundaries of `Article 74`—first in **1951** when he expressed reservations about enacting the **Hindu Code Bill** through the provisional Parliament without a popular mandate, and again in **November 1960** at the Indian Law Institute, when he suggested legal scholars study whether an elected Indian President was as rigidly bound by ministerial advice as the hereditary British Monarch. On both occasions, Prime Minister **Jawaharlal Nehru** relied on authoritative legal opinions from Attorney General **M.C. Setalvad** and Drafting Committee jurist **Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar**, confirming that departing from binding ministerial advice would shatter `Article 75(3)` collective responsibility and invert India's parliamentary democracy into an unaccountable presidential autocracy.

### Constitutional Discretion vs Situational Discretion

A decisive **Prelims and Mains distinction** separates the **President** from a **State Governor**:
- Under **`Article 163(1)`**, the Constitution expressly grants State Governors areas of **"Constitutional Discretion"** (*"except in so far as he is by or under this Constitution required to exercise his functions or any of them in his discretion"* — e.g., reserving a State Bill under `Art. 200`, recommending President's Rule under `Art. 356`).
- By contrast, **`Article 74(1)` contains NO such exception**: the **President of India has NO Constitutional Discretion**!
- Instead, the President enjoys **Situational Discretion**—independent constitutional judgment triggered solely when abnormal political contingencies make binding ministerial advice either unavailable or constitutionally suspect.

#### Five Spheres of Presidential Situational Discretion and Influence

1. **Appointment of Prime Minister in a Hung Lok Sabha (or Sudden Death of Incumbent):** When no single party or pre-poll alliance wins an absolute majority in the Lok Sabha—or when a sitting Prime Minister dies suddenly without an obvious successor—the President must exercise independent judgment to choose a leader most likely to command Lok Sabha confidence.
   - *Historical Evolution of Conventions:* In **1979**, President **Neelam Sanjiva Reddy** controversially appointed **Charan Singh** (who never faced a floor test). In **1984**, after Indira Gandhi's assassination, **Giani Zail Singh** bypassed the seniority precedent of an interim PM (unlike Gulzarilal Nanda in 1964 and 1966) to swear in **Rajiv Gandhi** immediately. During the hung-Parliament era (**1989–1998**), Presidents **R. Venkataraman, Shankar Dayal Sharma, and K.R. Narayanan** institutionalised transparent constitutional norms: inviting the **single largest party or pre-poll alliance** first (V.P. Singh 1989, P.V. Narasimha Rao 1991, Atal Bihari Vajpayee 1996), requiring **written letters of support** (Narayanan's 1998 innovation before appointing Vajpayee), and mandating a **time-bound vote of confidence on the floor of the Lok Sabha** (typically 10–30 days).
2. **Dismissal of a Council of Ministers Refusing to Resign:** If a ministry loses a Vote of No-Confidence (or fails a Confidence Motion) in the Lok Sabha and stubbornly refuses to tender its resignation, the President is constitutionally empowered to **dismiss the Council of Ministers** to uphold `Article 75(3)`.
3. **Dissolution of the Lok Sabha on Advice of a Defeated Ministry:** While a Prime Minister commanding a majority can ordinarily obtain dissolution, a Prime Minister who has **lost Lok Sabha confidence** (or never proved majority) **cannot bind the President** to dissolve the House. The President must first explore whether a **viable alternative government** can be formed from the existing Lok Sabha before ordering expensive mid-term polls (e.g., President Venkataraman invited Chandra Shekhar in 1990 after V.P. Singh fell, but dissolved the House in 1991 when no alternative emerged; President K.R. Narayanan dissolved the 12th Lok Sabha in April 1999 only after confirming no alternative coalition existed after Vajpayee lost a confidence motion by a single vote).
4. **Enforcing Caretaker Government Restraint:** Once a defeated ministry continues in a **caretaker capacity** pending general elections, Presidents have enforced constitutional conventions barring major policy reversals, long-term financial commitments, or high-level appointments that would fetter the incoming elected government (exemplified by President Narayanan's close scrutiny of caretaker policy decisions in 1999).
5. **Constitutional Suasion — `Article 74(1)` Proviso, `Article 78`, and Suspensive/Pocket Veto:** Even with a majority government, the President retains **Walter Bagehot's classic three rights of a constitutional head** (*The English Constitution*, 1867): **"the right to be consulted, the right to encourage, and the right to warn."** By returning a Cabinet advice once under the **44th Amendment proviso (`Art. 74(1)`)**, seeking files under **`Art. 78`**, or returning a Bill under **`Art. 111`**, a conscientious President forces the executive to pause, deliberate, and face public scrutiny—a powerful moral brake in a constitutional democracy.

> [!example]
> **Assertive Constitutionalism in Action — Landmark Precedents of Presidential Restraint:**
> - **President K.R. Bommai* (1994); once again, the Union Cabinet refrained from resending it!
> - **President Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam & Suspensive Veto (2006):** Returned the *Office of Profit Bill (2006)* raising reasoned constitutional objections on retrospective exemptions and uniform criteria, catalysing national parliamentary scrutiny even though the House eventually re-passed it.
> - **President Giani Zail Singh & `Art. 78` / Pocket Veto (1986–87):** Used the Pocket Veto to stall the controversial *Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986*, and forcefully asserted his `Article 78` right to be informed on Bofors and national affairs.

### Nominal Head, Rubber Stamp, or Constitutional Statesman?

Dismissing the Indian President as a mere **"rubber stamp"** is analytically inaccurate. Unlike the British Monarch—who inherits a throne, faces no written duties, and possesses no impeachment clause—the Indian President is an **elected federal Head of State** who swears a solemn oath under `Article 60` to **"preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law."** During eras of **single-party majority**, the President functions primarily as a non-partisan moral conscience, institutional advisor, and constitutional alarm bell through `Articles 74(1)` and `78`. During eras of **coalition politics and hung Parliaments**, the President becomes an indispensable **constitutional umpire**, ensuring smooth democratic transitions without partisan bias.

## 5. The Vice-President of India

Created by **Part V (`Articles 63` to `71`)**, the **Vice-President of India** occupies the **second-highest constitutional office** in the Republic—ranking immediately below the President and above the Prime Minister in the official Warrant (Order) of Precedence. Structurally modelled on the **American Vice-President**, the office was incorporated by the Constituent Assembly to fulfil two complementary institutional purposes: ensuring seamless political continuity at Rashtrapati Bhavan during any sudden Presidential vacancy or incapacity, and supplying a dignified, non-partisan presiding officer for the federal Upper House, the **Rajya Sabha**.

```keyfacts
# The Vice-President of India at a Glance (Articles 63–71)
Article 66(1) | Parliamentary Electoral College | Elected by **ALL MPs (elected + nominated)** of LS & RS (**788** sanctioned); **ZERO State/UT MLAs** (`11th Amend, 1961`)
Article 67(b) | Unique Removal Without Ground | Initiated **ONLY in Rajya Sabha** (14-day notice); **RS Effective Majority + LS Simple Majority**; **no ground stated**
Articles 64 & 65 | Dual Constitutional Mandate | Ex-officio **Chairman of Rajya Sabha** + **Acting President** (max **6 months** during vacancy; pauses RS duties)
Article 97 | Emoluments Anomaly | **NO separate salary as Vice-President**; draws **₹4,00,000/month** strictly as **RS Chairman** (charged on CFI)
```

### Election, Electoral College and Statutory Safeguards

Like the President, the Vice-President is elected **indirectly** (`Article 66(1)`), yet the Electoral College diverges sharply from the Presidential college in **two decisive ways**:

1. **Includes BOTH Elected AND Nominated Members of Parliament:** Every member of the Lok Sabha (543) and every member of the Rajya Sabha (233 elected + **12 nominated** = 245) votes—making a sanctioned Electoral College of **788 MPs**.
2. **Excludes ALL Members of State and UT Legislative Assemblies:** State MLAs and MLCs have **no role whatsoever** in electing the Vice-President. Explaining this deliberate omission in the Constituent Assembly, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar noted that whereas the President heads the entire federation and therefore requires State participation, the Vice-President's normal function is presiding over the Rajya Sabha and acting as President only for a brief transitional window—rendering the involvement of thousands of State legislators unnecessary.

**11th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1961:** Originally, `Article 66(1)` required the Vice-President to be elected by members of both Houses *"assembled at a joint meeting."* To eliminate practical hurdles, the **11th Amendment (1961)** replaced that joint-meeting clause with an **"electoral college consisting of the members of both Houses of Parliament"**—while simultaneously inserting **`Article 71(4)`** so that vacancies in the Electoral College cannot invalidate either a Presidential or Vice-Presidential poll.

**Voting Mechanics, Statutory Rules and Disputes:** Voting uses **PR-STV by secret ballot** (so **no party whip** applies and cross-voting never attracts Tenth Schedule disqualification). Unlike the weighted Presidential vote, **every MP's vote carries an equal value of 1**, and the winning quota is `(Total Valid Votes Polled ÷ 2) + 1`. Under the **Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952** (amended in **1997**), a candidate requires **20 proposers and 20 seconders** (raised from 5+5) and a **₹15,000 security deposit** with the RBI (forfeited below 1/6th of valid votes). Under **`Article 71`**, all election disputes lie **exclusively before the Supreme Court** (whose decision is final), and acts performed before an election is declared void remain valid (`Art. 71(2)`).

> - **Sudden Mid-Term Vacancy:** On **21 July 2025** (the opening day of Parliament's Monsoon Session), 14th Vice-President **Jagdeep Dhankhar** (in office since August 2022) abruptly resigned citing health reasons, triggering India's first early Vice-Presidential election since 1987 under **`Article 68(2)`**.

### Qualifications, Oath, Term and Removal Procedure

#### Qualifications, Conditions and Oath (Articles 66 and 69)

Under **`Article 66(3)`**, a Vice-Presidential candidate must: (1) be a **citizen of India** (by birth or naturalisation); (2) have completed **35 years** of age; (3) be **qualified for election as a member of the Rajya Sabha** *(contrast President = Lok Sabha!)*; and (4) hold **no office of profit** under the Union, any State, or any local/controlled authority—with the identical **exempt offices** (`Art. 66(4)` Explanation): sitting **President, Vice-President, Governor, or Union/State Minister**. Under `Article 66(2)`, the Vice-President cannot be a member of Parliament or any State Legislature (a sitting legislator's seat is deemed vacated upon entering office).

The Vice-President's oath sits in **`Article 69`** (NOT the Third Schedule!) and is administered by the **President** (or a person appointed by the President). Notably, the Vice-President swears to **"bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution of India"** and to **"faithfully discharge the duty upon which he is about to enter"**—differing subtly from the President's `Article 60` pledge to *"preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law."*

#### Term, Vacancy and the Unique Article 67(b) Removal Mechanism

Under **`Article 67`**, the Vice-President holds office for **five years** from entering office, continues in office beyond five years until a successor assumes charge, may resign at any time by writing addressed to the **President**, and is eligible for **unlimited re-election**. Historically, **two** Vice-Presidents served two full terms (**Dr. S. Radhakrishnan**, 1952–62, and **M. Hamid Ansari**, 2007–17); **four** elections were **unopposed** (Radhakrishnan 1952 & 1957, M. Hidayatullah 1979, Shankar Dayal Sharma 1987); **six** Vice-Presidents later became President (Radhakrishnan, Zakir Husain, V.V. Giri, R. Venkataraman, S.D. Sharma, and K.R. Narayanan); **three** resigned mid-term (V.V. Giri 1969, R. Venkataraman 1987, and Jagdeep Dhankhar 2025); and only **Krishan Kant** died in office (27 July 2002).

**Removal Without Impeachment (`Article 67(b)`):** Unlike the President, the Vice-President requires **NO formal impeachment**, and remarkably, **NO ground for removal is mentioned anywhere in the Constitution!** Removal follows a three-step parliamentary route:
1. **Initiated ONLY in the Rajya Sabha** (NEVER in the Lok Sabha!) after giving at least **14 days' advance notice** of the intention to move the resolution.
2. **Passed by the Rajya Sabha by an Effective Majority** (*"a majority of all the then members of the Council"*—i.e., more than 50% of the House's effective strength excluding vacant seats).
3. **Agreed to by the Lok Sabha by a Simple Majority** (more than 50% of members present and voting).

Under **`Article 92`**, while a resolution for removal is under consideration in the Rajya Sabha, the Vice-President **shall NOT preside** over the sitting, retains the right to **speak and take part in proceedings**, and—**crucial Prelims distinction!**—**CANNOT vote at all** (unlike the Lok Sabha Speaker under `Art. 96`, who is an elected MP and may vote in the first instance during their removal motion!).

**Mid-Term Vacancy (`Article 68(2)`):** When a vacancy arises from death, resignation, removal, or otherwise, an election must be held **"as soon as possible"**—notice that unlike the President (`Art. 62(2)`), **the Constitution fixes NO six-month deadline for filling a Vice-Presidential vacancy!** The newly elected Vice-President serves a **full five-year term**. During a Vice-Presidential vacancy, there is **no "Acting Vice-President"**; the **Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** presides over the Upper House (`Art. 91`).

> [!prelims]
> **High-Yield Prelims Traps on the Vice-President (`Articles 64–69, 92`):**
> - **No Ground Stated:** Constitution lists **NO ground** for removing the VP (`Art. 67(b)`), unlike the President's *"violation of the Constitution"*.
> - **RS-Only Initiation + Asymmetric Majorities:** Initiated **exclusively in Rajya Sabha** → **Effective Majority** in RS + **Simple Majority** in LS.
> - **No Vote Under `Article 92`:** Because the VP is **NOT a member of the Rajya Sabha**, they **cannot vote** during their own removal resolution (unlike the LS Speaker under `Art. 96`!).
> - **No Six-Month Rule:** Mid-term VP election is held **"as soon as possible"** (`Art. 68(2)`), NOT within a mandatory 6-month limit.
> - **Never Chairs a Joint Sitting:** Under `Art. 118(4)`, a Joint Sitting is chaired by **Speaker → Deputy Speaker → Deputy Chairman of RS**; the Vice-President **NEVER presides** over a Joint Sitting!

### Powers, Functions and Emoluments

The Vice-President performs **two distinct constitutional roles** (`Articles 64`, `65`, and `89`):

1. **Ex-Officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (`Articles 64 & 89`):** As presiding officer of the Council of States, the Vice-President exercises powers closely resembling those of the Lok Sabha Speaker—maintaining order, interpreting rules, referring Bills to committees, adjudicating Tenth Schedule defection petitions for RS MPs, and exercising a **casting vote only in the event of a tie** (`Article 100`, having no vote in the first instance because they are not a member of the House). Crucially, however, the Chairman lacks **two special powers exclusive to the Lok Sabha Speaker**: certifying whether a Bill is a **Money Bill (`Art. 110(3)`)** and presiding over a **Joint Sitting of Parliament (`Art. 118(4)`)**.
2. **Acting President / Discharging Presidential Functions (`Article 65`):** Acts as President during a Presidential vacancy for a **maximum of six months** (`Art. 65(1)` read with `Art. 62(2)`), or discharges Presidential functions during the President's temporary absence, illness, or inability (`Art. 65(2)`). Under the **proviso to `Article 64`**, throughout any period in which the Vice-President acts as President, they **shall NOT perform the duties of Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** (the Deputy Chairman presides) and **shall NOT be entitled to the RS Chairman's salary**—instead receiving the **President's salary, allowances, powers, and `Article 361` immunities (`Art. 65(3)`)**.
3. **Emoluments and Pension (`Article 97` & Second Schedule):** The Constitution provides **NO separate salary for the Vice-President as Vice-President**! Instead, the Vice-President draws emoluments solely as **ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** under the *Salaries and Allowances of Officers of Parliament Act, 1953*—fixed at **₹4,00,000 (₹4 lakh) per month** by the **Finance Act, 2018** (effective 1 January 2016) and **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**, alongside a rent-free residence, allowances, and a lifetime pension equal to 50% of salary (**₹2,00,000/month**) under the *Vice-President's Pension Act, 1997*.

### Comparative Perspectives: President vs Vice-President and Indian vs American Vice-President

Because the Indian Vice-President carries no independent executive duties when the President is healthy, some scholars echoed Benjamin Franklin's colonial quip describing the Vice-President as **"His Superfluous Highness."** Yet that caricature overlooks the office's dual constitutional value: presiding impartially over India's federal second chamber without representing any state constituency, and guaranteeing an instant, constitutionally legitimate caretaker at Rashtrapati Bhavan during national shocks (proven indispensably in 1969 and 1977).

## 1. The President: Election, Qualifications, Term and Impeachment

- **Union Executive (`Part V, Arts. 52–78`):** Comprises **5 offices** — **President, Vice-President, Prime Minister, Council of Ministers, and Attorney General of India (`Art. 76`)**. Incumbents: **Droupadi Murmu** (15th President, 25 July 2022 — first tribal, youngest, first post-Independence born) & **C.P. Radhakrishnan** (15th VP, 12 Sept 2025). Dr. Ambedkar (4 Nov 1948): *"Head of the State, not of the Executive."*
- **Electoral College (`Art. 54`):** STRICTLY **elected MPs** (543 LS + 233 RS = 776) + **elected MLAs** of 28 States + **elected MLAs of NCT Delhi & UT Puducherry** (**70th Amend, 1992**). **EXCLUDED:** Nominated MPs (12 RS; 2 Anglo-Indian LS seats ended Jan 2020 via **104th Amend**), nominated MLAs, **ALL MLCs** (elected + nominated), dissolved Assemblies. **J&K UT:** Not named in `Art. 54` Explanation despite 2024 Assembly polls; adding as UT needs `Art. 368(2)` special majority + half-States ratification (unless statehood restored pre-2027).
- **Vote Value (`Art. 55`) & 1971 Freeze:** Inter-state **uniformity** + Union–State **50:50 parity**. **MLA vote** = `(1971 State Pop ÷ Elected MLAs) × (1/1,000)` (+1 if remainder ≥500). **MP vote** = `Total MLA vote value ÷ Total Elected MPs` (+1 if fraction >0.5). **1971 Census** frozen by **42nd & 84th Amendments** till first census post-2026. **Prelims 2023 Traps:** More seats ≠ higher MLA vote (seats are in denominator!); **UP = 208 (max)**, Jharkhand/TN = 176, **Kerala (152) > MP (131)**, **Puducherry (16) > Arunachal (8)**, **Sikkim = 7 (min)**. In 2022, MP vote fell from **708 → 700** (no J&K Assembly); Murmu won 64.03% (6,76,803 vs Yashwant Sinha 3,80,177).
- **PR-STV, Secret Ballot & Nomination:** Quota = `(Valid Votes ÷ 2) + 1`. **Secret ballot (`Art. 55(3)`)** → **NO party whip, NO Tenth Schedule** anti-defection! **V.V. Giri (1969)** = only 2nd-preference winner; **Neelam Sanjiva Reddy (1977)** = only unopposed President. **1952 Act (1997 amend):** **50 proposers + 50 seconders**, **₹15,000 RBI deposit** (forfeited < 1/6th votes; upheld in *Charan Lal Sahu*, 1978). **Mains 2022 Comparison (India vs France):** India = indirect federal college (parliamentary); France = direct 2-round universal suffrage (1962), 500 sponsorships across 30 departments, 5-yr term, max 2 consecutive terms.
- **Disputes (`Art. 71`):** **Supreme Court exclusive & final** jurisdiction; **prior acts remain valid** if voided (`Art. 71(2)` — Prelims 2023 trap); **incomplete Electoral College NOT a ground** (`Art. 71(4)`, **11th Amend 1961**; ***In re: Presidential Poll*, 1974** — dissolved Gujarat Assembly cannot stall poll); 39th Amend ouster reversed by **44th Amend (1978)**.
- **Qualifications (`Art. 58`), Oath (`Art. 60`), Conditions (`Art. 59`) & Immunity (`Art. 361`):** Citizen (**birth OR naturalisation**, unlike US natural-born), **35 yrs**, **Lok Sabha** eligible, no office of profit (**exempt:** sitting President, VP, Governor, Union/State Minister). Oath NOT in 3rd Schedule; administered by **CJI** (*"preserve, protect and defend Constitution & law"*). Emoluments: **₹5,00,000/month** (Finance Act 2018 w.e.f. 1 Jan 2016; 1951 Act; 50% pension ₹2.5L), **charged on CFI**, non-diminishable. **`Art. 361`:** Official non-answerability; **absolute criminal immunity** during term; **civil suits for personal acts** only after **2 months' written notice**.
- **Term (`Art. 56`), Re-election (`Art. 57`) & Vacancy (`Arts. 62, 65, 70`):** **5 years** from entering office; holds over till successor joins; resigns to **VP** (who forthwith informs **LS Speaker**, `Art. 56(2)`). **Unlimited re-election** (vs US 22nd Amend 2-term cap; only **Rajendra Prasad** served 2 terms). Mid-term vacancy filled within **6 months** for a **full 5-yr term**. Succession: **VP → CJI → senior-most SC Judge** (**1969 Act**, `Art. 70`); 1969 double precedent (Zakir Husain died → Giri acted & resigned → **CJI M. Hidayatullah** acted 35 days); 1977 (F.A. Ahmed died → **B.D. Jatti** acted → 25 July inauguration norm).
- **Impeachment (`Art. 61`):** **Quasi-judicial**; sole ground **"violation of the Constitution"** (**undefined!**); **EITHER House** initiates (**1/4th signatures + 14 days' notice**); passed by **2/3rd of TOTAL membership** of EACH House (**strictest majority in Constitution!**); 2nd House investigates (right to appear/defend); never invoked. **Swap Trap:** **Nominated MPs PARTICIPATE** in impeachment (excluded from election); **MLAs DO NOT participate** (vote in election).

## 2. Powers and Functions of the President

- **Executive (`Arts. 53, 77–78`):** Actions in President's name (`77(1)`); Business Rules (`77(3)`); PM's duty to inform & submit unconsidered ministerial decisions (`78`); appoints PM/CoM, AG, CAG, CEC/ECs, UPSC, Governors, FC, NCSC/ST/BC, Linguistic Minorities Officer, ISC (`263`); administers UTs (`239`) & frames **UT regulations (`Art. 240`)**; declares **Scheduled Areas** (5th Schedule).
- **Legislative (`Arts. 79–87, 103, 108`):** Integral part of **Parliament (`79`)**; summons, prorogues, dissolves LS (`85` — Presiding Officer adjourns!); summons **Joint Sitting (`108` — Speaker chairs!)**; **Special Address (`87`)** after general election & first session each year; appoints **Speaker pro tem**; nominates **12 RS MPs** (`80(3)` — **4 fields**: Literature, Science, Art, Social Service; **NO Cooperative Movement!**; 2 Anglo-Indian LS seats ended 2020, `104th Amend`); decides **`Art. 102(1)` disqualifications** bound by **ECI opinion (`Art. 103`)** (Tenth Schedule defection = Presiding Officer!); **Prior Recommendation needed** for Money Bills, Finance Bill-I (`117(1)`), Finance Bill-II (**consideration only**, `117(3)`), `Art. 3`, `Art. 274`, `Art. 304(b)` (**NOT `Art. 368` Bills!**); lays CAG/UPSC/FC/NCSC/ST/BC reports.
- **Financial:** Prior recommendation for Money Bills & **Demands for Grants (`113(3)`)**; lays **Annual Financial Statement (`112`)**; holds **Contingency Fund (`267`, ₹30,000 cr)**; constitutes **Finance Commission (`280`)** every 5 yrs (16th FC: Dr. Arvind Panagariya).
- **Judicial, Diplomatic, Military & Emergency:** Appoints SC/HC Judges (`124/217`, Collegium); seeks non-binding SC advisory opinion (`Art. 143(1)` discretionary for SC; `143(2)` mandatory for SC); concludes treaties (implemented by Parliament `Art. 253`); **Supreme Commander (`Art. 53(2)`)** expressly **regulated by Parliamentary law**; proclaims **National (`352` — written Union Cabinet advice!), State (`356/365`), Financial (`360`)** Emergencies.

## 3. Veto Powers, Ordinance-Making and Pardoning Powers

- **Veto Powers (`Arts. 111 & 201`):** India has **3 vetoes — Absolute, Suspensive, Pocket** (**NO Qualified Veto!**). **Absolute:** PEPSU Bill 1954 (Rajendra Prasad), MPs' Salaries Bill 1991 (Venkataraman). **Suspensive:** Overridden by ordinary majority; **BARRED on Money Bills**; Kalam returned Office of Profit Bill (2006). **Pocket:** **NO time limit** in `Art. 111` (vs US 10 days — Prelims 2023 trap); Zail Singh pocketed **Indian Post Office Bill, 1986**. **Constitutional Amendment Bills (`Art. 368`):** **ZERO veto** — **24th Amend (1971)** made assent mandatory.
- **Reserved State Bills (`Art. 201`) & *Special Reference No. 1 of 2025*:** State Legislature gets **6 months** to reconsider returned Bill, **yet repassage DOES NOT bind President** (`Art. 201` omits *"shall not withhold"*). On **20 Nov 2025** (`2025 INSC 1333`), a unanimous **5-Judge Bench** (CJI B.R. Gavai, Surya Kant, Vikram Nath, P.S. Narasimha, A.S. Chandurkar) answering President Murmu's 14-question `Art. 143(1)` reference rejected the 8 April 2025 *Tamil Nadu Governor* rigid timelines (1–3 mo / 3 mo `Art. 201`) and `Art. 142` **"deemed assent"** as breaching Separation of Powers, held merits non-justiciable pre-enactment, yet barred indefinite Governor "withholding simpliciter" and permitted **limited mandamus** for glaring inaction.
- **Ordinances (`Art. 123`):** Origin: 1861 Act / Sec. 42 GoI 1935 (absent in UK & USA). Issued when **both OR either House** not in session (valid if 1 House sits; **void if both sit!**); co-extensive with Parliament; subject to **Fundamental Rights (`Art. 13(3)` — *A.K. Roy*, 1982)**; **CANNOT amend Constitution**; retrospective/tax/repeal/withdrawal permitted. Lapses **6 weeks** after reassembly of **LATER** House (**max life: 6 months + 6 weeks**). Judicial review restored by **44th Amend** (*R.C. Cooper*, 1970). ***D.C. Wadhwa* (1987)** & 7-Judge ***Krishna Kumar Singh* (2 Jan 2017)**: laying is **mandatory**, re-promulgation is a **"fraud on the Constitution"**, and lapsed ordinances do **NOT** create automatic enduring rights (overruling *T. Venkata Reddy*).
- **Pardoning Power (`Art. 72`):** Covers **(1) Court Martial, (2) Union laws, (3) ALL death sentences**. **5 Modes:** **Pardon** (erases conviction + sentence), **Commutation** (lighter *form*), **Remission** (shorter *period*, same form), **Respite** (lesser sentence for *special fact* — disability/pregnancy), **Reprieve** (*temporary stay*). **`Art. 72` vs Governor `Art. 161`:** Governor has **NO court-martial power** and **CANNOT pardon death sentences** (may suspend/remit/commute under `Art. 72(3)`).
- **Clemency Cases, BNSS & USA Comparison:** ***Maru Ram* (1980)** & ***Perarivalan* (2022)** = binding CoM advice (**Prelims 2025 Q18**); ***Kehar Singh* (1989)** = fresh merits check, no oral hearing; ***Epuru Sudhakar* (2006)** = **5-ground limited review** (non-application of mind, *mala fides*, extraneous grounds, material ignored, arbitrariness); ***Shatrughan Chauhan* (2014)** = unexplained delay violates `Art. 21` → commutation; ***Mahendra Vishwanath Kawchale* (2 Sept 2025)** = statutory natural-life sentence cannot extinguish `Arts. 72/161` remission. **`Sec. 472 BNSS` (w.e.f. 1 Jul 2024):** 30-day filing, 60-day post-Governor & Central recommendation timelines (`Sec. 472(7)` ouster cannot defeat Basic Structure writ review). **Mains 2025 (India vs USA):** US President decides personally (`Art. II Sec. 2`), federal crimes only, barred in impeachment, near-zero review, allows **pre-emptive pardons** pre-charge (*Ex parte Garland* 1866; Ford–Nixon 1974; Biden Jan 2025); India's `Art. 72` says *"convicted"* → strictly **post-conviction**.

## 4. Constitutional Position and Situational Discretion

- **Binding Ministerial Advice (`Arts. 53, 74, 75, 78`):** ***Ram Jawaya Kapur* (1955)**, ***U.N.R. Rao* (1971)** (CoM mandatory even after LS dissolution), and 7-Judge ***Shamsher Singh* (1974)** (satisfaction = CoM satisfaction). **42nd Amend (1976)** made advice expressly binding; **44th Amend (1978)** inserted **one-time reconsideration proviso** (reconsidered advice binds). `Art. 74(2)` bars inquiring into advice, but *Bommai* allows examining underlying material.
- **No Constitutional Discretion — ONLY Situational Discretion:** Unlike Governors (`Art. 163`), President has **zero constitutional discretion**; exercises **situational discretion** in: (1) hung LS PM appointment / sudden PM death (written support letters + floor test); (2) dismissing CoM refusing to quit after losing majority; (3) dissolving LS when advised by a defeated ministry; (4) caretaker ministry restraint; (5) **Bagehot's triad** (*consulted, encourage, warn*) via `Art. 78` & `Art. 74(1)` return — e.g., **K.R. Narayanan** returning UP (1997) & Bihar (1998) `Art. 356` advice; **Kalam** returning 2006 Office of Profit Bill.

## 5. The Vice-President of India

- **Election (`Art. 66`):** **2nd highest office** (US VP model). Elected by **ALL MPs (elected + nominated)** of LS & RS (**788**; **NO MLAs!** — **11th Amend, 1961** replaced joint sitting with Electoral College); equal vote value (**1**); PR-STV secret ballot (no whip); **20 proposers + 20 seconders**, ₹15,000 deposit; SC sole dispute arbiter (`Art. 71`). **2025 Mid-Term Poll:** After **Jagdeep Dhankhar** resigned (**21 Jul 2025**), **C.P. Radhakrishnan** won (**9 Sept 2025**, 452–300 vs B. Sudershan Reddy; 752 valid) and took oath (**12 Sept 2025**) as **15th VP** for a **full 5-year term**.
- **Eligibility, Oath, Term & Removal (`Arts. 66–69, 92`):** Citizen, **35 yrs**, **Rajya Sabha eligible**, no office of profit (same 4 exempt posts); oath by **President (`Art. 69`)** (*"true faith & allegiance"*); **5-yr term**, unlimited re-election (2-term VPs: **S. Radhakrishnan & Hamid Ansari**; 4 unopposed polls; 6 became President; **Krishan Kant** died in office 2002); resigns to President. **Removal (`Art. 67(b)`):** **NO impeachment, NO constitutional ground**, initiated **ONLY in RS** (14 days' notice), **RS Effective Majority + LS Simple Majority**; under **`Art. 92`**, cannot preside & **CANNOT vote** during own removal (not an RS member!). **First-ever `Art. 67(b)` notice (10 Dec 2024)** against Dhankhar dismissed by Deputy Chairman Harivansh (**19 Dec 2024**). Mid-term vacancy filled **"as soon as possible"** (`Art. 68(2)` — **no 6-month limit!**).
- **Powers & Emoluments (`Arts. 64, 65, 89, 97`):** **Ex-officio RS Chairman** (casting vote on tie only; **cannot** certify Money Bills or chair Joint Sittings!); **Acting President** up to **6 months max** (contrast US VP serving full remainder!) — pauses RS duties & draws President's salary. **NO separate VP salary** — draws **₹4,00,000/month** strictly as **RS Chairman** (Finance Act 2018; 1953 Act; charged on CFI; 50% pension).
