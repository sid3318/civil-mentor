## 1. Horizontal federalism: why inter-state harmony matters

Cooperative federalism is not only a vertical equation between New Delhi and state capitals; it depends equally on **horizontal harmony** among the states themselves. In a continental federation reorganised along linguistic lines, shared river basins, contested border villages, cross-border policing, and internal trade barriers routinely test national cohesion.

To manage horizontal friction without extinguishing state autonomy, the Constitution pairs **Part XI** (Articles 261–263) and **Part XIII** (Articles 301–307) with **statutory** bodies—the five **Zonal Councils (1956)** and the **North-Eastern Council (1971)**—alongside extra-constitutional platforms like the **NITI Aayog Governing Council**. Separating constitutional, statutory, and executive bodies is one of UPSC Prelims' favourite tests.

## 2. Inter-state water disputes (Article 262)

### Constitutional framework and the two 1956 laws

Under the Seventh Schedule, **water** is a **State List subject (Entry 17)**, whereas regulating and developing **inter-state rivers and river valleys** in the public interest lies on the **Union List (Entry 56)**. Because inter-state river litigation can turn rigid and politically explosive, **Article 262** empowers **Parliament** by law to **(1)** provide for adjudicating disputes over waters of inter-state rivers or river valleys, and **(2)** **bar the Supreme Court and all other courts** from jurisdiction over such disputes.

Parliament enacted two statutes in 1956:

- **River Boards Act, 1956:** Empowers the Centre to create **advisory River Boards** on the request of concerned state governments—**yet not a single River Board has ever been constituted**, as states resisted central oversight.
- **Inter-State River Water Disputes (ISRWD) Act, 1956:** Lets the Centre set up an ad hoc **Water Disputes Tribunal** when a state requests adjudication and negotiations fail. Amended in **2002** on the **Sarkaria Commission's** recommendations, it mandates tribunal setup within **one year**, an award within **three years** (extendable by **two years**), and grants published awards the force of a **Supreme Court decree** (final and binding).

> **Prelims pointer:** Under **Article 262(2)** read with Section 11 of the ISRWD Act, 1956, river water disputes fall **outside the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction under Article 131**. Yet the bar is not total: the Court still entertains **Special Leave Petitions (Article 136)** on legal, procedural, or implementation questions (*Cauvery*, 2018) and directs tribunal constitution when negotiations fail (*Pennaiyar*, February 2026). Remember too that **no River Board has ever been set up** under the 1956 River Boards Act.

### Major tribunals and live river disputes

The Centre has constituted **nine tribunals** so far under the ISRWD Act, 1956, and on 2 February 2026 the Supreme Court directed it to notify a tenth, for the **Pennaiyar**.

Several live flashpoints illustrate the political sensitivity of shared waters. On the **Cauvery**, the Supreme Court's **February 2018** verdict raised Karnataka's share by 14.75 TMC for Bengaluru's drinking needs and led to the **Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA)**, yet Karnataka's proposed **Mekedatu** reservoir is still opposed by Tamil Nadu. On the **Ravi–Beas**, the Supreme Court struck down Punjab's 2004 law terminating its water agreements in 2016, but Punjab's stretch of the **Sutlej–Yamuna Link (SYL) Canal** remains unbuilt and the Court keeps sending the two states back to talks. On the **Godavari**, Telangana contests Andhra Pradesh's plan to carry Polavaram water to the Banakacherla regulator. Beyond tribunals, the 130-year-old **Mullaperiyar Dam** on the Periyar (in Kerala, operated by Tamil Nadu under an 1886 lease) is watched by a committee the Supreme Court set up, while the **Upper Yamuna River Board** (1995) works through a cooperative 1994 MoU.

### Bottlenecks and the ISRWD (Amendment) Bill

Despite the 2002 timelines, ad hoc tribunals drag on for decades (Ravi–Beas at **40 years**, Krishna-II at **22 years**) due to slow tribunal constitution, absence of shared **hydrological data**, endless Article 136 appeals, regional identity politics, and weak award enforcement. To overhaul this system, the **ISRWD (Amendment) Bill, 2019** proposed: **(1)** a pre-tribunal **Dispute Resolution Committee (DRC)** (1 year + 6 months); **(2)** a **single permanent Standing Tribunal** with benches replacing all ad hoc tribunals (4.5-year adjudication cap); **(3)** automatic binding finality without separate gazette-notification delays; and **(4)** a **national hydrological data bank**. Passed by the Lok Sabha on 31 July 2019, it remained pending in the Rajya Sabha and **lapsed in June 2024** upon dissolution of the 17th Lok Sabha (Article 107(5)). The reform had not been enacted by November 2026.

## 3. The Inter-State Council (Article 263)

**Article 263** empowers the **President** to establish an **Inter-State Council (ISC)** whenever **public interests** require, charging it with three duties: **(a)** inquiring into and advising upon inter-state disputes; **(b)** investigating and discussing subjects of common Centre–State or inter-state interest; and **(c)** recommending better **coordination of policy and action**. Its dispute role **complements** the Supreme Court's binding legal adjudication under **Article 131**: the ISC covers both legal and non-legal disputes, but its advice is **strictly advisory and non-binding**.

After earlier sectoral uses of Article 263 (Central Councils of Health and Local Government), the V.P. Singh government established the permanent **Inter-State Council** by Presidential Order on **28 May 1990**, implementing the **Sarkaria Commission's (1988)** recommendation (which had suggested naming it **"Inter-Governmental Council"**). Its members are the **Prime Minister (Chairman)**, **Chief Ministers** of all States and UTs with legislatures, **Administrators** of UTs without legislatures, **Governors** of states under President's Rule, and PM-nominated **Union Cabinet Ministers**—originally **six** under the 1990 Order, and **nine** in the reconstitution notified on **8 November 2024**, with other ministers as permanent invitees. A **Standing Committee** (set up in **1996** and reconstituted in November 2024), chaired by the **Union Home Minister**, handles continuous consultation; the **Inter-State Council Secretariat** (1991) services both the ISC and (since 2011) the Zonal Councils.

Yet the ISC remains chronically underused. Although rules envisage three meetings annually, it has met **only 11 times since 1990**—first in October 1990 and **eleventh on 16 July 2016**—meaning **over ten years have elapsed without a full Council meeting as of November 2026**. Overshadowed by the **NITI Aayog Governing Council** and ad hoc Chief Ministers' conferences, the ISC is the forum states want revived before **delimitation**, echoing **Sarkaria** and **Punchhi (2010)** proposals for mandatory annual sittings and an independent secretariat.

> **Prelims pointer:** The **Inter-State Council (Art. 263)** is a **constitutional** (not statutory) body created by the **President** (not Parliament) in **1990** on the **Sarkaria Commission's** advice. The **PM** chairs the Council; the **Union Home Minister** chairs its **Standing Committee**. Its advice is **non-binding**.

## 4. Public acts, records and judicial proceedings (Article 261)

To stop states from functioning as legally isolated units, **Article 261**—the **"Full Faith and Credit"** clause—mandates three rules across India: **(1)** full faith and credit shall be given nationwide to public acts, records, and judicial proceedings of the Union and of every state; **(2)** **Parliament** determines by law the manner and conditions of proving them; and **(3)** final judgments or orders of **civil courts** anywhere in India are executable throughout the country according to law.

> **Prelims pointer:** **Article 261(3)** covers execution of **civil judgments only**—it does **NOT** apply to **criminal or penal laws** of one state, which another state's courts do not directly enforce.

## 5. Inter-state trade, commerce and intercourse (Part XIII: Articles 301–307)

Inspired by **Section 92 of the Australian Constitution**, **Part XIII (Articles 301–307)** protects India's economic unity. **Article 301** guarantees that trade, commerce, and intercourse throughout India shall be **free**—covering **both inter-state and intra-state** trade—subject to calibrated exceptions:

- **Parliament's limits (Arts. 302–303):** Parliament may restrict trade in the **public interest** (Art. 302). Neither Parliament nor states may prefer one state or discriminate between states—**except Parliament** (never a state) during a **scarcity of goods** (Art. 303(2)).
- **States' limits (Art. 304):** A State Legislature may **(a)** levy **non-discriminatory taxes** on goods imported from other states matching taxes on local goods, and **(b)** impose reasonable public-interest restrictions on trade—**ONLY with the previous sanction of the President** before introducing the Bill.
- **State monopolies & authority (Arts. 305, 307):** **Article 305** saves laws creating **state monopolies** (nationalisation). **Article 307** lets Parliament appoint an authority to carry out Articles 301–304—**never appointed so far**.

While a nine-judge bench in ***Jindal Stainless* (2016)** upheld non-discriminatory entry taxes, cascading state octroi and check-posts long fragmented India's market until the **101st Amendment (2016)** and **GST (2017)** dismantled border check-posts, advancing "One Nation, One Market" through the **GST Council (Article 279A)**.

> **Prelims pointer:** Under **Art. 304(b)**, a State Bill restricting trade in public interest needs **prior Presidential sanction before introduction**. Only **Parliament** may discriminate during **scarcity of goods** (Art. 303(2)), and **no Art. 307 authority has ever been constituted**.

## 6. Zonal Councils and the North-Eastern Council

### Five Zonal Councils (States Reorganisation Act, 1956)

Unlike the Inter-State Council, **Zonal Councils are STATUTORY bodies—NOT constitutional bodies**. Created under **Part III (Sections 15–22) of the States Reorganisation Act, 1956**, they flowed from **Jawaharlal Nehru's** December 1955 vision of cultivating a **"habit of cooperative working"** amid linguistic reorganisation.

| Zonal Council | Headquarters | Member States and Union Territories |
|---|---|---|
| **Northern** | New Delhi | Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Punjab, Rajasthan; UTs of Chandigarh, Delhi, Jammu & Kashmir, Ladakh |
| **Central** | Prayagraj | Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Uttar Pradesh |
| **Eastern** | Kolkata | Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha, West Bengal *(Sikkim is NOT a member)* |
| **Western** | Mumbai | Goa, Gujarat, Maharashtra; UT of Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu |
| **Southern** | Chennai | Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Telangana; UT of Puducherry *(A&N Islands & Lakshadweep: special invitees)* |

The **Union Home Minister** is the common ex-officio **Chairman** of all five councils, while member **Chief Ministers** rotate as **Vice-Chairman** for **one year** each. Other members include two ministers per state (nominated by the Governor) and two members per UT, assisted by non-voting advisers (a NITI Aayog nominee, Chief Secretaries, and Development Commissioners) and a **Standing Committee of Chief Secretaries**. Though purely **deliberative and advisory**, Zonal Councils address border issues, linguistic minorities, transport, water/power coordination, Fast Track Special Courts, and narcotics/cybercrime policing—and have become India's most active horizontal federal forum.

> **In the news:** PIB figures shared at the **28th Western Zonal Council (Panaji, 7 September 2026)** show meetings of Zonal Councils and their Standing Committees nearly tripled from **25 (2004–14)** to **71 (2014 – September 2026)**, discussing **1,893 issues** and resolving **1,445**, about three in four. The Home Minister said the councils now monitor welfare schemes as much as they settle disputes.

### The North-Eastern Council (NEC)

Established separately under the **North-Eastern Council Act, 1971** (HQ **Shillong**), the **NEC** originally covered seven states—Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, and Tripura—with **Sikkim** added as the eighth member by the **NEC (Amendment) Act, 2002**. Unlike the five Zonal Councils, it includes **both Governors and Chief Ministers** of all eight states plus three President-nominated members, with the **Union Home Minister** as ex-officio **Chairman** and the **DoNER Minister** as **Vice-Chairman** (since 2018). Designated a **statutory regional planning body** in 2002, it formulates unified development plans and periodically reviews regional security. Its **73rd Plenary Session (Shillong, 4 June 2026)** took up the recommendations of a High-Level Task Force on connectivity, investment and trade with South-East Asia.

|---|---|---|---|---|

## 7. Inter-state border disputes and the Mains way forward

Colonial boundary notifications and post-1956 linguistic reorganisation left persistent **inter-state border disputes**:

- **North-East:** Carving new states from undivided Assam created overlapping claims, though recent bilateral agreements show momentum. **Assam and Meghalaya** signed an **MoU on 29 March 2022** settling **six of 12 disputed areas**; the other six are still under talks. **Assam and Arunachal Pradesh** followed the **Namsai Declaration (July 2022)** with an **April 2023 MoU** on 123 disputed villages, now being demarcated on the ground. **Assam–Mizoram** remains in ministerial talks, while **Assam–Nagaland** is pending before the Supreme Court.
- **Maharashtra–Karnataka (Belagavi):** Following the 1956 Act's placement of Belagavi, Karwar, and Nipani in Karnataka, the **Mahajan Commission (1966–67)** awarded Belagavi to Karnataka and 264 villages to Maharashtra—accepted by Karnataka, rejected by Maharashtra, whose **2004 Article 131 suit** remains pending in the Supreme Court.
- **Punjab–Haryana:** Shared capital **Chandigarh** (untransferred despite the 1985 **Rajiv–Longowal Accord**) stays intertwined with the Ravi–Beas/SYL impasse.

### Way forward

Strengthening horizontal federalism requires four targeted steps: **(1)** reviving the **Inter-State Council** with mandatory annual meetings, an autonomous secretariat outside the Home Ministry, and structured consensus-building on post-2026 delimitation and fiscal devolution; **(2)** enacting the **ISRWD Amendment** (DRC + single permanent Standing Tribunal + open national hydrological data bank) alongside multi-state **River Basin Organisations** focused on ecological flows and demand management; **(3)** pairing the Zonal Councils' record of resolving about three issues in four with **Survey of India** digital mapping for peaceful bilateral border settlements; and **(4)** guarding the GST-enabled common market against protectionist state domicile barriers.

> **Mains angle:** Anchor GS-2 answers on a sharp empirical contrast: **(1) Constitutional design** (Arts. 261–263, 301–307) vs. **(2) Institutional asymmetry**—a **dormant constitutional ISC** (no meeting since July 2016 despite Nov 2024 reconstitution) alongside **active statutory Zonal Councils** (71 meetings and about 76% of issues resolved, up to Sept 2026); **(3) Water-tribunal paralysis** (40-yr Ravi–Beas, 22-yr Krishna-II, Feb 2026 *Pennaiyar* SC order, lapsed 2019 ISRWD Bill); and **(4) Way forward** (autonomous ISC, single permanent water tribunal + shared hydrological data, bilateral Survey of India border demarcation).
