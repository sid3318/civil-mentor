## 1. The dual role of the Governor

Part VI (**Articles 153 to 167**) establishes the State Executive: the Governor, the Chief Minister, the Council of Ministers and the Advocate General. Unlike the Union, a state has no Vice-Governor. At the apex stands the Governor, designed for a **dual role**—constitutional head of the state and vital constitutional link between the Centre and the state.

**Article 153** originally provided one Governor per state, until the **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956** added a proviso allowing the **same person to be appointed Governor of two or more states**, with emoluments shared among them as determined by the President (**Article 158(3A)**).

In the **Constituent Assembly**, the initial draft proposed an American-style directly elected Governor. On **30–31 May 1949**, the Assembly shifted to the **Canadian model** of central nomination. Jawaharlal Nehru warned that an elected Governor would create a rival centre of power alongside an elected Chief Minister, duplicate elections, and encourage a "separatist provincial tendency". B.R. Ambedkar added that a constitutional head bound by ministerial advice needed no electoral mandate.

> **Prelims pointer:** In ***Hargovind Pant v. Dr. Raghukul Tilak* (1979)**, a five-judge bench held that the Governor holds an **independent constitutional office**, NOT an employment under the Central government, and is neither subordinate nor subservient to the Centre.

## 2. Appointment, qualifications, conditions and tenure

Under **Article 155**, the Governor is **appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal**—in practice on the Union Cabinet's advice.

**Qualifications and conventions:** **Article 157** prescribes only **two** constitutional qualifications: the appointee must be a **citizen of India** and have completed **35 years** of age. Unlike the President, eligibility for legislative election is not required. Two **conventions** grew alongside—appointing an **outsider** to the state and **consulting the Chief Minister** beforehand—but neither sits in the Constitution, and both are frequently breached.

**Conditions, oath and immunities:** Under **Article 158**, a Governor cannot be a legislator (vacating any seat upon taking office) or hold an office of profit, gets a rent-free residence, and draws Parliament-fixed emoluments that cannot be diminished during the term. Under **Article 159**, the **High Court Chief Justice** (or seniormost available judge) administers the oath. **Article 361** gives personal immunity from court answerability for official acts, bars criminal proceedings or arrest in office, and requires **two months' notice** for personal civil suits—protecting the individual, not insulating unconstitutional official acts.

**Tenure and the doctrine of pleasure:** Under **Article 156**, the normal term is **five years** (continuing until a successor assumes charge), and a Governor may resign to the President or be transferred. Crucially, the Governor holds office **during the President's pleasure**, with **no stated grounds** and **no impeachment**. In ***B.P. Singhal v. Union of India* (2010)**, a five-judge bench held pleasure is **not arbitrary or unfettered**: a change of central government or ideological mismatch is invalid. Though reasons need not be supplied, arbitrary or *mala fide* removals attract limited judicial review.

> **Remember:** Article 157 lists only **two** qualifications (citizen + 35 years); outsider status and CM consultation are conventions. The **High Court Chief Justice** gives the oath (Art 159), and removal is at the **President's pleasure** without impeachment (*B.P. Singhal*, 2010).

## 3. Powers and functions of the Governor

The Governor's powers mirror the President's across executive, legislative, financial and judicial spheres, except that a Governor has **no diplomatic, military or national emergency powers**.

### Executive powers

Under **Articles 154 and 166**, all state executive action runs in the Governor's name, and the Governor frames rules for allocating government business. The Governor appoints the Chief Minister and other ministers on the Chief Minister's advice—including a mandatory **Tribal Welfare Minister** in **Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh and Odisha** (Article 164(1) proviso; the **94th Amendment Act, 2006** dropped Bihar and added Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand). The Governor also appoints the **Advocate General** (serving at the Governor's pleasure), the **State Election Commissioner**, and the **Chairman and members of the State Public Service Commission (SPSC)**, seeks information from the Chief Minister (**Article 167**), and may recommend **President's Rule** (**Article 356**), administering the state as the President's agent once imposed.

> **Prelims pointer:** Watch three traps! First, the Governor **appoints** the SPSC Chairman and members, **but only the President can remove them** (Article 317). Second, the **State Election Commissioner** (Article 243K) is removed only like a High Court judge, not at the Governor's pleasure. Third, the Governor acts as **ex-officio Chancellor** of state universities under **state statutes**, NOT under the Constitution.

### Legislative powers

An integral part of the State Legislature (**Article 168**), the Governor summons and prorogues either House, dissolves the Assembly (**Article 174**), addresses the first session after general elections and each year (**Article 176**), and sends messages (**Article 175**). The Governor nominates **one-sixth** of the Legislative Council from literature, science, art, **cooperative movement** and social service (**Article 171(5)**); the **104th Amendment Act, 2019** ended Anglo-Indian nomination to the Assembly (Article 333). The Governor decides Article 191 disqualifications on the binding opinion of the **Election Commission of India** (**Article 192**) and lays SPSC, State Finance Commission and state CAG reports before the House.

**Assent to Bills (Articles 200–201):** Under **Article 200**, the Governor has **three options**: (1) assent; (2) reserve for the President; or (3) withhold assent and—for non-Money Bills—return it "as soon as possible" for reconsideration (**first proviso**). Under the **second proviso**, reservation is **mandatory** if a Bill would endanger the **High Court's** constitutional position (Articles 31A, 31C, 254(2) and 288(2) also require Presidential assent). Once reserved, **Article 201** ends the Governor's role; even if the President returns a non-Money Bill and the legislature re-passes it within six months, the President is **not bound** to assent.

**Ordinance power (Article 213):** During recess, when immediate action is necessary, the Governor promulgates ordinances on **ministerial advice**, co-extensive with state legislative power. Ordinances lapse **six weeks** after reassembly (max life six months and six weeks) unless approved earlier; repeated re-promulgation without placing them before the legislature is a "fraud on the Constitution" (***D.C. Wadhwa*, 1987**; ***Krishna Kumar Singh*, 2017**, seven judges). Under the **Article 213(1) proviso**, the Governor **cannot issue an ordinance without Presidential instructions** if:
- a like Bill requires the President's **previous sanction** for introduction;
- the Governor would have deemed it necessary to **reserve** such a Bill; or
- a like Act would be **invalid** without Presidential assent after reservation.

### Financial and judicial powers

Financially, the Governor lays the **State Budget** (**Article 202**), recommends **Money Bills** (**Article 207**) and **Demands for Grants** (**Article 203**), controls the **Contingency Fund of the State** (**Article 267(2)**), and constitutes a **State Finance Commission** every five years (**Articles 243-I and 243-Y**).

Judicially, under **Article 161**, the Governor grants pardons, reprieves, respites, remissions, suspensions or commutations for offences against state laws, acting on binding State Cabinet advice (***A.G. Perarivalan*, 2022** barred referring Article 161 advice to the President). Unlike Article 72, the Governor **cannot pardon a death sentence**—though **Article 72(3)** preserves the power to **suspend, remit or commute** it—and has **no jurisdiction over court-martial** sentences. The Governor is consulted on High Court appointments (**Article 217**), appoints and promotes **District Judges** with the High Court (**Article 233**), and appoints other judicial officers with the High Court and SPSC (**Article 234**).

## 4. Constitutional and situational discretion: Article 163

Unlike **Article 74** (which gives the President **no express constitutional discretion**), **Article 163(1)** binds the Governor to ministerial advice *except* where required by or under the Constitution to act **in their discretion**, and **Article 163(2)** makes the Governor's decision on what falls within discretion final.

The Supreme Court has firmly curbed sweeping readings. ***Samsher Singh* (1974)** (seven judges) confirmed the Governor is normally bound by Cabinet advice. ***Nabam Rebia* (2016)** (five judges) held that Article 163(2) grants no overarching discretion and that arbitrary or *mala fide* actions remain subject to **judicial review**.

**Constitutional (express) discretion** includes: reserving Bills (and choosing among Article 200 options); recommending President's Rule under Article 356 (reviewable per *S.R. Bommai*, 1994, and *Rameshwar Prasad*, 2006); administering an adjoining Union Territory (Article 239(2)); determining mineral-royalty shares for Autonomous District Councils in **Sixth Schedule** states (**Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram**); seeking information from the Chief Minister (Article 167); and discharging **special responsibilities** under **Articles 371–371J** (after consulting ministers, acting in individual judgment)—separate development boards in Maharashtra, Gujarat and Karnataka (Art 371, 371J); law and order in **Nagaland** (Art 371A) and **Arunachal Pradesh** (Art 371H); tribal/hill areas in **Assam** (Art 371B) and **Manipur** (Art 371C); and peace and equitable advancement in **Sikkim** (Art 371F).

**Situational discretion** covers appointing a Chief Minister in a **hung Assembly** (subject to a floor test under *S.R. Bommai*, 1994), dismissing a ministry losing confidence, dissolving an Assembly after lost majority, and sanctioning a minister's prosecution against palpably biased Cabinet advice (***M.P. Special Police Establishment*, 2004**).

| Dimension | President of India | Governor of a State |
|---|---|---|
| **Selection & removal** | Indirectly elected (Art 54); removed by impeachment (Art 61) | Appointed by President (Art 155); removed at President's pleasure (Art 156) |
| **Constitutional discretion** | None expressly granted (Art 74) | Expressly granted (Art 163(1)–(2)) |
| **Bill assent** | Art 111: must assent if Parliament re-passes returned Bill | Art 200: assent, return or reserve; under Art 201, President not bound even on re-passage |
| **Ordinance power** | Art 123: no prior instructions needed | Art 213: needs President's instructions in 3 cases |
| **Pardoning power** | Art 72: Union laws, **death sentences**, **court-martial** | Art 161: state laws only; **no death-sentence pardon** (can suspend/remit/commute); **no court-martial** |
| **Emergency, diplomatic & military** | Full constitutional powers | None; can only recommend Art 356 |

## 5. Major controversies and Supreme Court jurisprudence (2016–2026)

Friction between Raj Bhavans and elected governments—especially in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Punjab, West Bengal and Telangana—has reshaped constitutional law over the past decade.

### The Article 200 bill-assent battle (2023–2025)

Indefinite gubernatorial delays over state Bills ("pocket veto") produced three landmark Supreme Court decisions:

1. ***State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor* (10 November 2023, 3-judge bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud):** Upholding the Speaker's right to reconvene a House adjourned *sine die*, the Court held that "as soon as possible" carries urgency and withholding assent under Article 200 must be read with the first proviso—requiring the Governor to remit the Bill rather than killing it silently.

> "The Governor, as an unelected Head of the State, is entrusted with certain constitutional powers. However, this power cannot be used to thwart the normal course of lawmaking by the State Legislatures." — Supreme Court of India, *State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor*, 10 November 2023

2. ***State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu* (8 April 2025, 2-judge bench of Justices J.B. Pardiwala and R. Mahadevan):** Holding Governor R.N. Ravi's reservation of 10 re-passed Bills illegal and *non est*, the bench ruled Governors generally act on ministerial advice under Article 200, prescribed **binding judicial timelines** (one to three months under Article 200; three months for the President under Article 201), and invoked **Article 142** to declare the 10 Bills **"deemed assented"**.

3. ***In Re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills by the Governor and the President of India* (Special Reference No. 1 of 2025, decided 20 November 2025):** On **13 May 2025**, President Droupadi Murmu referred **14 questions** under **Article 143(1)**. Answering the reference without disturbing the relief already given to Tamil Nadu, a **five-judge Constitution Bench** (CJI B.R. Gavai and Justices Surya Kant, Vikram Nath, P.S. Narasimha and A.S. Chandurkar) corrected key holdings of the April 2025 ruling:
   - The Governor has **three options** under Article 200 (assent, reserve, or withhold and return non-Money Bills); the first proviso restricts withholding rather than creating a fourth option, and even after re-passage the Governor may assent **or reserve** the Bill for the President.
   - In choosing among these options, the Governor exercises **constitutional discretion** and is **not bound** by ministerial aid and advice.
   - Because Articles 200–201 omit deadlines, courts **cannot impose fixed timelines** or declare **"deemed assent"** under Article 142, which breaches separation of powers.
   - The **merits** of decisions under Articles 200–201 are **not justiciable**, and Bills cannot be pre-emptively reviewed before enactment (save via Article 143). **Crucially**, however, in glaring cases of **"prolonged, unexplained, and indefinite" inaction**, courts may issue a **limited mandamus** directing the Governor to act within a reasonable time without dictating the outcome; **Article 361** grants personal immunity, not blanket cover for constitutional paralysis.

### Other recurring flashpoints

**Summoning and floor tests:** *Nabam Rebia* (2016) barred Governors from advancing Assembly sessions or setting agendas without Cabinet advice while a ministry holds majority. ***Subhash Desai* (2023)** faulted the Maharashtra Governor for calling a 2022 floor test over an internal Shiv Sena split without objective evidence of lost legislative majority.

**Hung assemblies, addresses and ministers:** Inconsistent invitations in Goa (2017), Manipur (2017), Karnataka (2018) and Maharashtra (2019) forced court-ordered televised floor tests. Governors in Tamil Nadu, Kerala and West Bengal also sparked rows by editing or skipping Cabinet-approved Article 176 addresses or attempting to dismiss or block swearing-in of ministers despite Article 164(1) tying the Governor's pleasure over ministers to the Chief Minister's advice.

**Ex-officio University Chancellor:** Because statutory university powers operate outside Article 163 advice, Vice-Chancellor appointments sparked chronic disputes in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, West Bengal and Punjab. States passed Bills replacing the Governor with the Chief Minister or an academician; Rashtrapati Bhavan returned Punjab's Bill (July 2024) and withheld assent to three West Bengal Bills (December 2025), while Tamil Nadu's amendments, which took effect through the April 2025 ruling, were challenged in the Madras High Court. The **Punchhi Commission (2010)** explicitly urged ending this statutory Chancellor burden.

> **In the news:** Introduced on **20 August 2025**, the **Constitution (130th Amendment) Bill, 2025** proposes amending Articles 75, 164 and 239AA so any PM, CM or Minister detained for **30 consecutive days** on offences carrying 5+ years' imprisonment is removed on the 31st day (by the Governor on CM's advice, or automatically the next day; a detained CM must resign or automatically ceases to hold office). Still pending before a Joint Parliamentary Committee chaired by MP Aparajita Sarangi as of **November 2026**, critics warn pre-conviction detention could destabilise elected state governments.

## 6. Reforming the Governor's office: committees and way forward

Three commissions—**Sarkaria (1988)**, **Venkatachaliah / NCRWC (2002)** and **Punchhi (2010)**—offer a clear reform blueprint.

| Dimension | Sarkaria (1988) | Venkatachaliah / NCRWC (2002) | Punchhi (2010) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Appointee** | Eminent outsider, detached from recent politics | Eminent non-partisan figure | Out of active politics for **2+ years** |
| **Selection** | Consult state CM (also VP & LS Speaker) | Committee: PM, Home Minister, LS Speaker, state CM | Formal **collegium**: PM, Home Minister, LS Speaker, state CM |
| **Tenure & removal** | Normally 5 years; remove only for compelling reasons | 5-year term; consult CM before removal/transfer | **Delete President's pleasure**; fixed 5-year term; **State Legislature impeachment** |
| **Bills & other roles** | Sparing discretion; Art 356 last resort | Reserve Bill within **6 months**; President **3 months** | Decide Bills within **6 months**; **end University Chancellor role** |

For hung assemblies, Sarkaria and Punchhi proposed a codified order of preference:

```text
               Hung State Assembly
                        │
                        ▼
   1. Largest PRE-POLL alliance (treated as one party)
                        │ (if none / fails)
                        ▼
   2. Single largest party with support of others
                        │ (if none / fails)
                        ▼
   3. Post-poll coalition where all join government
                        │ (if none / fails)
                        ▼
   4. Post-poll coalition with outside support
                        │
                        ▼
       Mandatory FLOOR TEST in the Assembly
        (S.R. Bommai, 1994 — never in Raj Bhavan)
```

> **Mains angle:** Mains 2026 asked three things in one question: the Governor's position in the federal polity, the nature of the power of assent, and whether the Governor is bound by ministerial advice in all functions. Answer the second and third from the November 2025 opinion (three options under Article 200, exercised in the Governor's discretion, with no court-set timeline but no indefinite silence either) and from Article 163 with *Samsher Singh* and *Nabam Rebia* (bound in general, with the exceptions listed in section 4). Balance **cooperative federalism** with **constitutional continuity**. Reject abolition or direct election, and address three deficits: **partisan appointment** (bipartisan collegium giving the CM a formal voice), **tenure insecurity** (codifying *B.P. Singhal* safeguards), and **uncodified discretion** (enacting the Sarkaria–Punchhi hung-assembly hierarchy, ending the statutory Chancellor role, and upholding constitutional restraint on Bill assent post-November 2025). India needs neither a rubber stamp nor a parallel Chief Minister, but a non-partisan constitutional bridge.

- **Dual role (Arts 153–167):** Constitutional head of state + Union link. **7th Amendment (1956):** same person may be Governor of 2+ states (Art 158(3A)).
- **Appointment (Art 155):** By President by warrant (Canadian model, 31 May 1949). ***Hargovind Pant* (1979):** independent constitutional office, NOT Central employment.
- **Qualifications (Art 157):** Only **two**—citizen + **35 years**; outsider status & CM consultation are conventions. Oath by **HC Chief Justice** (Art 159); **Art 361** gives personal immunity, not cover for unconstitutional acts.
- **Tenure & removal (Art 156):** 5-year term, **President's pleasure**, NO grounds, NO impeachment. ***B.P. Singhal* (2010):** not arbitrary; central government change invalid; limited judicial review.
- **Appointment traps:** Governor appoints SPSC members, **only President removes them** (Art 317); SEC removed like HC judge; **University Chancellor** role is statutory, NOT constitutional.
- **Tribal Welfare Minister (Art 164(1)):** Mandatory in **Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha** (**94th Amendment, 2006** dropped Bihar, added Chhattisgarh & Jharkhand).
- **Legislative role:** Part of Legislature (Art 168); nominates **1/6th** of Council (adds **cooperative movement**, Art 171(5)); decides Art 191 disqualifications on binding **ECI opinion** (Art 192); 104th Amendment ended Anglo-Indian nomination.
- **Bills (Arts 200–201):** Three options—assent, reserve, or withhold & return non-Money Bills. **Mandatory reservation** if HC powers endangered. Under Art 201, President NOT bound even on re-passage.
- **Ordinances (Art 213):** Needs **President's instructions** in 3 cases (Art 213(1) proviso); lapses 6 weeks post-reassembly; re-promulgation is "fraud on the Constitution" (*D.C. Wadhwa*, 1987; *Krishna Kumar Singh*, 2017).
- **Clemency (Art 161):** Bound by Cabinet advice (*Perarivalan*, 2022). Cannot **pardon** death sentence (can suspend/remit/commute, Art 72(3)); no **court-martial** power.
- **Constitutional discretion (Art 163):** Express unlike Art 74—Bill reservation, Art 356, adjoining UT (Art 239(2)), Sixth Schedule royalties (Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram), Art 167 info, **Arts 371–371J**.
- **Situational discretion:** Hung Assembly CM choice (*Bommai* floor test), dismissing ministry losing confidence, dissolving Assembly after lost majority, prosecution sanction against biased advice (*M.P. Special Police Establishment*, 2004).
- **Judicial checks:** ***Samsher Singh* (1974)** (bound by advice); ***Nabam Rebia* (2016)** (Art 163(2) not overarching; no unilateral session advance); ***Subhash Desai* (2023)** (no floor test for party splits).
- ***State of Punjab* (10 Nov 2023):** No indefinite sitting on Bills; withholding requires remitting under first proviso "as soon as possible".
- ***State of Tamil Nadu* (8 Apr 2025):** 2-judge bench invalidated reservation of 10 re-passed Bills, set 1–3 month timelines, declared **"deemed assent"** under Art 142.
- **Presidential Reference No. 1 of 2025 (20 Nov 2025, 5 judges led by CJI B.R. Gavai):** Answering 14 Art 143 questions (13 May 2025), affirmed Governor's **discretion** under Art 200 (may reserve even after re-passage), rejected **timelines** and **"deemed assent"**, held merits non-justiciable, but allowed **limited mandamus** for **"prolonged, unexplained, and indefinite" inaction**.
- **Recent flashpoints:** University Chancellor Bills returned/withheld (Punjab 2024, West Bengal 2025) and litigated in Tamil Nadu; **130th Amendment Bill, 2025** (30-day custody removal) pending with JPC (Nov 2026).
- **Reforms:** **Sarkaria (1988)**—outsider, CM consultation, 4-step hung-assembly order; **Venkatachaliah (2002)**—committee with CM; **Punchhi (2010)**—collegium, delete President's pleasure, State Legislature impeachment, **end Chancellor role**.
