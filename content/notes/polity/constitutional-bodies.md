## 1. Election Commission of India

Modern constitutional democracies no longer rely exclusively on the classical Montesquieuan triad of Legislature, Executive, and Judiciary to prevent the concentration of power. Recognising that an executive commanding a parliamentary majority could easily capture electoral machinery, politicise civil service recruitment, obscure public expenditure, or neglect vulnerable social minorities, the framers of the Indian Constitution deliberately embedded a suite of **autonomous constitutional bodies**—often described in comparative constitutional scholarship (following **Bruce Ackerman** and **Tarunabh Khaitan**) as India's **"fourth branch" or guarantor institutions**. Unlike statutory authorities created by an ordinary Act of Parliament or executive bodies established by Cabinet resolution (covered in **Chapter 22**), **constitutional bodies** derive their existence, core mandate, and primary institutional shields directly from the text of the Constitution itself; their abolition or structural alteration requires a constitutional amendment under `Article 368`.

Across the Constitution, twelve principal constitutional authorities anchor India's democratic, administrative, fiscal, social, and legal integrity: the **Election Commission of India** (`Article 324`), the **Union and State Public Service Commissions** (`Articles 315–323`), the **Comptroller and Auditor General of India** (`Articles 148–151`), the **Finance Commission** (`Article 280`) and **Goods and Services Tax Council** (`Article 279A`—both examined exhaustively in **Chapter 10**), the **National Commissions for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Backward Classes** (`Articles 338`, `338A`, and `338B`), the **Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities** (`Article 350B`), and the **Attorney General for India** (`Article 76`) and **Advocates General of the States** (`Article 165`). Together with the Supreme Court, they form the institutional bulwarks of Indian constitutionalism—and among them, none carries a more immediate mandate for democratic legitimacy than the **Election Commission of India (ECI)**.

```keyfacts
6 Years / 65 Years | Tenure & Asymmetric Removal | CEC removed like SC Judge; other ECs/Regional Commissioners removed ONLY on CEC's recommendation; no constitutional qualification or post-retirement bar
```

### Constitutional Foundation and the Rationale for a Single All-India Body

`Part XV` of the Constitution (`Articles 324 to 329`) governs elections in India, with `Article 324` serving as its operational cornerstone. `Article 324(1)` vests the **"superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of the electoral rolls for, and the conduct of, all elections to Parliament and to the Legislature of every State and of elections to the offices of President and Vice-President held under this Constitution"** in a permanent, independent **Election Commission**.

During the Constituent Assembly's early deliberations, the original draft of `Article 289` (which became `Article 324`) had contemplated a decentralised federal arrangement: one central Commission for Parliamentary elections and separate provincial Election Commissions for each State. However, disturbing reports reached the Drafting Committee indicating that certain provincial governments were manipulating electoral rolls to disenfranchise linguistic, cultural, and regional minorities residing within their borders. Convinced that universal adult suffrage—introduced in one bold stroke without property, literacy, or gender filters—could survive only if insulated from partisan provincial executives, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** moved a radical substitution on **15 June 1949** creating a unified, centrally insulated constitutional authority for both Union and State elections.

> "The whole election machinery should be in the hands of a Central Election Commission, which alone would be entitled to issue directives to returning officers, polling officers and others." — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, 15 June 1949

Established formally on **25 January 1950**—one day before the Constitution entered into force, and commemorated annually since 2011 as **National Voters' Day**—the Election Commission of India holds a distinctive federal character: it is a **common all-India constitutional body** serving both the Union and all 28 States (as well as Union Territories with Legislative Assemblies). Crucially, however, this mandate does **not** extend to grassroots local self-government elections. When the `73rd` and `74th Constitutional Amendment Acts of 1992` constitutionalised Panchayats (`Part IX`) and Municipalities (`Part IXA`), Parliament established separate, independent **State Election Commissions (SECs)** under `Articles 243K` and `243ZA`—a favourite UPSC Prelims distinction that aspirants must never conflate.

> [!prelims]
> **Classic Scope Trap — What the ECI Does and Does NOT Conduct:**
> - **Conducted by ECI (`Art. 324`):** Elections to **Lok Sabha**, **Rajya Sabha**, **State Legislative Assemblies**, **State Legislative Councils**, **President of India**, and **Vice-President of India**.
> - **NOT Conducted by ECI:** Elections to **Panchayats** (`Art. 243K`) and **Municipalities** (`Art. 243ZA`), which are conducted by independent **State Election Commissions** appointed by the respective State Governors. Similarly, internal elections to offices such as the Speaker of Lok Sabha, Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha, or Cooperative Societies are outside `Art. 324`.
> - **Common Misconception on Election Disputes:** The ECI does **not** adjudicate post-election petitions challenging the validity of an election to Parliament or State Legislatures. Under `Article 329(b)` read with the Representation of the People Act, 1951 (amended in 1966 to abolish Election Tribunals), election petitions lie before the **High Court**, while Presidential and Vice-Presidential election disputes lie exclusively before the **Supreme Court** (`Article 71`).

### Structural Evolution: From Single-Member to Permanent Three-Member Commission

A careful reading of `Article 324(2)` reveals an intentionally flexible constitutional design. The clause states that the Election Commission *"shall consist of the Chief Election Commissioner and such number of other Election Commissioners, if any, as the President may from time to time fix."* When any other Election Commissioner is appointed, `Article 324(3)` stipulates that the **Chief Election Commissioner shall act as the Chairman** of the Commission. Additionally, under `Article 324(4)`, before each general election to the Lok Sabha or State Legislative Assemblies (and before biennial elections to State Legislative Councils), the President may also appoint, **after consultation with the Election Commission**, such **Regional Commissioners** as considered necessary to assist the Commission.

Because `Article 324(2)` left the exact strength of the Commission to Presidential determination rather than fixing a rigid number in the Constitution, the Commission's internal structure underwent four distinct historical phases before settling into its permanent three-member architecture:

1. **The Single-Member Era (25 January 1950 – 15 October 1989):** For nearly four decades—starting with India's first CEC, **Sukumar Sen** (who oversaw the monumental 1951–52 and 1957 general elections)—the Commission operated strictly as a **single-member body** consisting solely of the Chief Election Commissioner.
2. **First Expansion to a Three-Member Body (16 October 1989 – 1 January 1990):** Following the **`61st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1988`** (effective **28 March 1989**), which lowered the voting age under `Article 326` from **21 years to 18 years**, the national electorate surged abruptly by tens of millions of young voters. Citing this expanded workload (though critics also alleged political friction with then-CEC R.V.S. Peri Sastri), the Rajiv Gandhi government issued a notification on **16 October 1989** appointing two Election Commissioners (**S.S. Dhanoa** and **V.S. Seigell**), converting the ECI into a three-member body for the first time.
3. **Reversion to a Single-Member Body (2 January 1990 – 30 September 1993):** Immediately after assuming office, the V.P. Singh government rescinded the 1989 notification on **1 January 1990**, abolishing the two posts of Election Commissioners and reverting the ECI to a single-member authority. When **S.S. Dhanoa** challenged the premature termination of his office, the Supreme Court in ***S.S. Dhanoa v. Union of India* (1991)** upheld the President's power to create and abolish posts of Election Commissioners under `Article 324(2)`, observing that the CEC and other ECs were not necessarily on an equal footing in every institutional aspect—a view subsequently revisited by a larger Constitution Bench.
4. **Permanent Three-Member Commission (1 October 1993 – Present):** Amid intense friction between the P.V. Narasimha Rao government and India's famously assertive tenth CEC, **T.N. Seshan**, the President promulgated an Ordinance on **1 October 1993** (later enacted as the **Election Commission (Conditions of Service of Election Commissioners and Transaction of Business) Amendment Act, 1993**, amending the parent **1991 Act**). This milestone legislation appointed two Election Commissioners (**M.S. Gill** and **G.V.G. Krishnamurty**), elevated all Election Commissioners to **complete parity with the CEC** in salary, allowances, and decision-making power, and mandated that in the event of disagreement, decisions of the Commission must be taken by **majority vote**.

#### Equal Powers, Majority Rule, and the T.N. Seshan Constitution Bench Ruling (1995)

T.N. Seshan challenged the 1993 Amendment before the Supreme Court, contending that equating the two Election Commissioners with the CEC and subjecting the CEC to majority outvoting undermined `Article 324(3)` (which makes the CEC Chairman) and `Article 324(5)` (which grants only the CEC Supreme Court judge-level removal protection). In a landmark unanimous verdict by a five-judge Constitution Bench in ***T.N. Seshan, Chief Election Commissioner of India v. Union of India* (1995)**, the Supreme Court dismissed Seshan's challenge and laid down three foundational doctrines that still govern the ECI's internal functioning:

- **Multi-Member Insulation Against Arbitrariness:** Concentrating plenary constitutional powers over India's entire electoral process in a single individual carries the inherent risk of autocracy; a multi-member Commission promotes deliberation, mutual checks, and institutional balance.
- **CEC as *Primus Inter Pares* (First Among Equals):** The designation of the CEC as "Chairman" under `Article 324(3)` signifies presiding authority to conduct meetings and transact business—it does **not** confer superior substantive voting weight, administrative supremacy, or a veto power over the other two Election Commissioners.
- **Equal Status and Majority Decision-Making:** The CEC and the two Election Commissioners possess **equal powers** and receive **equal salaries, allowances, and perquisites** (aligned with those of a Judge of the Supreme Court). All business of the Commission must, as far as possible, be transacted **unanimously**; where differences of opinion arise, the **opinion of the majority prevails**—a statutory rule originally codified in `Section 10` of the 1991 Act (as amended in 1993) and now re-enacted verbatim in **`Section 18`** of the **CEC and Other Election Commissioners Act, 2023**.

> [!case]
> ***T.N. Seshan v. Union of India* (1995, 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** Rejecting the notion of a "one-man Commission," Chief Justice A.M. Ahmadi's bench clarified that the superior removal protection enjoyed by the CEC under the first proviso to `Article 324(5)` exists strictly to shield the institutional permanence of the Commission—not to elevate the CEC into a hierarchical superior over fellow Commissioners. Crucially, the Court emphasised that while *S.S. Dhanoa* (1991) had described ECs as subordinate in a single-member context, once a multi-member Commission is constituted by statute with equal status, the CEC cannot ignore or override the majority view of the two Election Commissioners.

### The Appointment Transformation: From Executive Monopoly to Anoop Baranwal (2023) and the 2023 Act

Nowhere in Indian constitutional law has institutional reform been more fiercely contested over the past decade than in the **manner of appointing the CEC and Election Commissioners**. `Article 324(2)` states expressly that the appointment of the CEC and other Election Commissioners shall be made by the President **"subject to the provisions of any law made in that behalf by Parliament."** Read alongside the Constituent Assembly Debates of **15–16 June 1949**, this phrasing was no accidental boilerplate: members including **H.V. Kamath**, **Shibban Lal Saksena**, and **K.M. Munshi** had warned that leaving appointments to the unfettered discretion of the Prime Minister and Cabinet would allow a ruling party to pack the electoral referee with compliant loyalists. Dr. Ambedkar acknowledged the legitimacy of this concern and inserted the phrase *"subject to any law made by Parliament"* precisely so that Parliament would soon enact a statutory mechanism insulating ECI appointments from exclusive executive control.

Yet for **seventy-three years (1950–2023)**, successive governments across the entire political spectrum failed to enact any appointment statute under `Article 324(2)`. Despite repeated recommendations for a multi-party/judicial collegium from the **Tarkunde Committee (1975)**, the **Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990)**, the **Second Administrative Reforms Commission (4th Report, *Ethics in Governance*, 2007)**, and the **255th Law Commission Report (2015)**, appointments remained an opaque executive prerogative: the Law Ministry prepared a shortlist of serving or retiring civil servants, the Prime Minister selected a name, and the President signed the warrant under `Article 74(1)`.

#### The Landmark Anoop Baranwal Judgment (2 March 2023)

This 73-year legislative vacuum was decisively ruptured on **2 March 2023** when a five-judge Constitution Bench headed by **Justice K.M. Joseph** (with Justices Ajay Rastogi, Aniruddha Bose, Hrishikesh Roy, and C.T. Ravikumar) delivered its unanimous judgment in ***Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India* (2023)**. Tracing the Constituent Assembly's express intent, highlighting the dramatic shortening of CEC tenures since 2004 (most CECs appointed between 2004 and 2022 served less than two years, depriving them of the statutory six-year stability), and noting the haste surrounding the November 2022 appointment of EC Arun Goel within 24 hours of his voluntary retirement, the Court held that:

1. **Free and Fair Elections as Basic Structure:** Democracy is a cornerstone of the Basic Structure, and democracy cannot survive without a fiercely independent Election Commission insulated from the "fierce loyalty" expected by an appointing executive.
2. **Constitutional Vacuum Requires Interim Judicial Intervention:** Because Parliament had left `Article 324(2)`'s statutory mandate unfulfilled for over seven decades, the Court exercised its constitutional duty to fill the vacuum **until Parliament enacted a law**.
3. **Interim Three-Member Neutral Selection Committee:** Until Parliamentary legislation was passed, the President was directed to appoint the CEC and Election Commissioners solely on the advice of a three-member committee comprising:
   - The **Prime Minister of India**;
   - The **Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha** (or, if no recognised LoP exists, the leader of the single largest opposition party in the Lok Sabha); and
   - The **Chief Justice of India (CJI)**.

The Bench also urged Parliament to seriously consider extending **identical removal protection** (impeachment-like procedure) to the other Election Commissioners and establishing a **permanent, independent ECI Secretariat** with expenditure **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**—though it stopped short of issuing binding directions on those two fronts, leaving them to legislative wisdom.

#### The Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023

Responding directly to *Anoop Baranwal*, Parliament enacted **Act No. 49 of 2023**—the **Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023**—which received Presidential assent on **28 December 2023** and entered into force on **2 January 2024**, repealing the earlier **1991 Act**. While fulfilling the long-pending legislative mandate of `Article 324(2)` on paper, the statute triggered intense constitutional controversy by **replacing the Chief Justice of India on the Selection Committee with a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister**, thereby creating an automatic **2:1 executive majority** inside the appointment panel.

Aspirants must pay laser-sharp attention to the **critical differences between the August 2023 Bill (as introduced in Rajya Sabha) and the final December 2023 Act (as amended and passed)**—a classic source of UPSC Prelims traps where outdated coaching summaries frequently err!

> [!prelims]
> **Three Deadly Prelims Traps in the CEC Act, 2023:**
> 1. **Search Committee Chair:** It is the **Union Minister of Law and Justice** (`Sec. 6`), **NOT** the Cabinet Secretary! The Cabinet Secretary was only in the original August 2023 Bill before Parliament amended it.
> 2. **Salary Parity:** The final enacted Act (`Sec. 10`) retains **Supreme Court Judge** salary parity—it did **NOT** downgrade CEC/EC salary to Cabinet Secretary (that downgrade was withdrawn via amendment in December 2023).
> 3. **Selection Committee Scope:** Under `Sec. 8(2)`, the Selection Committee is **not confined** to the Search Committee's shortlist of five names; it can recommend *any other qualified person*. Furthermore, under `Sec. 7(2)`, an appointment is **not invalidated** by any vacancy or defect in the Selection Committee.

### Independence, Tenure, Removal, and Constitutional Flaws

To safeguard the Commission from partisan intimidation, `Article 324` builds specific constitutional protections—yet when compared alongside the Supreme Court, CAG, and UPSC, `Article 324` reveals **three striking structural omissions** that UPSC routinely tests in both Prelims MCQs and Mains analytical essays.

#### Constitutional Safeguards for Independence

1. **Security of Tenure for the CEC (`Article 324(5)`, First Proviso):** The Chief Election Commissioner cannot be removed from office except **in like manner and on the like grounds as a Judge of the Supreme Court**—that is, by an order of the President passed after an **address by each House of Parliament** supported by a **special majority** (majority of total membership of that House **and** not less than two-thirds of members present and voting) on the grounds of **proved misbehaviour or incapacity** (`Article 124(4)`). Thus, although appointed by the President, the CEC **does not hold office during the President's pleasure**.
2. **Non-Variation of Service Conditions (`Article 324(5)`, First Proviso):** The conditions of service of the Chief Election Commissioner shall **not be varied to his disadvantage** after his appointment.
3. **Partial Shield for Election Commissioners and Regional Commissioners (`Article 324(5)`, Second Proviso):** Any other Election Commissioner or a Regional Commissioner **shall not be removed from office except on the recommendation of the Chief Election Commissioner**.
4. **Power to Requisition Election Staff (`Article 324(6)`):** The President or the Governor of a State shall, when so requested by the Election Commission, make available to it such staff as may be necessary for the discharge of its electoral functions. Once drafted onto election duty, such personnel—including IAS/IPS officers, school teachers, and police—are deemed to be on **deputation to the ECI** and remain subject to its disciplinary superintendence (`Section 28A` of RPA, 1951).

#### Three Major Constitutional Flaws and Vulnerabilities

Despite the high stature of the ECI, constitutional scholars, the **255th Law Commission**, and the Supreme Court in *Anoop Baranwal* have flagged three glaring gaps in `Article 324`:

1. **No Constitutional Qualifications Prescribed:** Unlike Supreme Court Judges (`Art. 124(3)`) or the Attorney General (`Art. 76(1)`), the **Constitution itself specifies NO legal, educational, administrative, or judicial qualifications** for the CEC or Election Commissioners. While `Section 5` of the 2023 Act introduced a statutory criterion (Secretary to GoI rank), critics warn that restricting eligibility exclusively to senior civil servants narrows institutional diversity by excluding distinguished jurists, academics, and retired judges.
2. **No Constitutional Term Fixed & No Constitutional Bar on Post-Retirement Government Employment:** The **Constitution does not specify the tenure** of ECI members—the **6 years or age 65** ceiling is purely statutory (first fixed by the 1991 Act and continued in `Section 9` of the 2023 Act). More critically, unlike the CAG (`Art. 148(4)`) and UPSC Chairpersons/Members (`Art. 319`), the **Constitution does NOT debar retiring Election Commissioners or CECs from further appointment by the Union or State Governments**! Historically, former CECs and ECs have been appointed as Union Cabinet Ministers (e.g., M.S. Gill), State Governors, or Rajya Sabha nominees—creating an acute perception of post-retirement executive inducement.
3. **Asymmetric Removal Protection & Financial Dependence:** Only the **CEC** enjoys impeachment-equivalent security of tenure (`Art. 324(5)` first proviso). The **two Election Commissioners** can be removed by the President merely **"on the recommendation of the CEC"**—and as the Supreme Court observed in *T.N. Seshan* (1995) and *Anoop Baranwal* (2023), while the CEC's recommendation is a condition precedent, it is **not even strictly binding** on the President to remove, nor does it match the parliamentary safeguard protecting the CEC. Worse still, an EC awaiting elevation to CEC remains structurally vulnerable to executive favour during the critical pre-elevation window. Simultaneously, unlike the UPSC (`Art. 322`), CAG (`Art. 148(6)`), and Supreme Court (`Art. 146(3)`), the **administrative expenditure of the ECI Secretariat is VOTED by Parliament (not charged on the Consolidated Fund of India)**, leaving its budget dependent on the Union Ministry of Law and Justice.

> [!remember]
> **Astonishing Constitutional Irony for Prelims:** A **State Election Commissioner** (`Art. 243K(2)`) conducting Panchayat elections actually enjoys **stronger constitutional security of tenure** (removable only like a **High Court Judge** by the President after Parliamentary special majority!) than the **two Union Election Commissioners** (`Art. 324(5)`), who can be removed by the President merely on the **recommendation of the CEC**! Moreover, note that a **Governor appoints** the SEC, but **ONLY the President** can remove the SEC!

### Powers and Functions: Administrative, Advisory, and Quasi-Judicial

The Supreme Court in ***Mohinder Singh Gill v. Chief Election Commissioner* (1977)**—speaking through Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer—famously described `Article 324` as a **"reservoir of power"** operating in all areas left unoccupied by Parliamentary statutes (chiefly the **Representation of the People Acts of 1950 and 1951** and the **Delimitation Commission Acts**). Where Parliament has enacted a valid law under `Article 327`, the ECI must act in conformity with that statute; where legislation is silent or an unanticipated crisis threatens electoral purity, `Article 324` empowers the Commission to issue binding directions to ensure free and fair elections. Analytically, the ECI's vast functions are grouped into **three distinct categories**:

#### 1. Administrative Powers and Functions

- **Territorial Constituencies and Delimitation:** Determines territorial areas of electoral constituencies throughout the country on the basis of the **Delimitation Commission Act** enacted by Parliament (the CEC or an EC nominated by the CEC serves as an ex-officio member of the Delimitation Commission).
- **Preparation and Revision of Electoral Rolls:** Prepares, periodically revises (including summary and special intensive revisions), and maintains error-free electoral rolls under `Article 325` (one general electoral roll for every constituency; no exclusion on religion, race, caste, or sex) and `Article 326` (universal adult suffrage at 18 years), and registers all eligible voters.
- **Election Schedules and Scrutiny:** Notifies the dates and schedules of elections (formal notifications are issued by the President for Lok Sabha/Rajya Sabha and by the Governor for State Legislatures on the ECI's recommendation), appoints Returning Officers, Assistant Returning Officers, and Electoral Registration Officers, and oversees scrutiny of nomination papers.
- **Supervision of Election Machinery and Poll Conduct:** Supervises election personnel nationwide, deploys General, Police, and Expenditure Observers, administers Electronic Voting Machines (EVMs) and Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trails (VVPATs), cancels polls in the event of **rigging, booth capturing, violence, or large-scale bribery** (`Section 58A` RPA, 1951), and requisitions staff from the Centre and States under `Article 324(6)`.
- **Model Code of Conduct (MCC):** Enforces the **Model Code of Conduct** for political parties and candidates from the moment election dates are announced until completion of the poll process. Although the MCC is **not a legally enacted statute** (it originated as a voluntary consensus in Kerala in 1960 and was adopted nationally from 1971), the ECI enforces several of its prohibitions through existing penal provisions (BNS / RPA 1951 corrupt practices) and its plenary `Article 324` powers (e.g., temporary campaign bans, censures, and transfer of partisan officials).
- **Media Allocation and Election Expenditure Monitoring:** Allocates equitable broadcast/telecast time on public broadcasters (Doordarshan and Akashvani) to recognised national and state parties during elections, monitors statutory campaign expenditure ceilings of candidates (not parties—which face no statutory cap on overall party spending!), and oversees mandatory disclosure of candidates' criminal antecedents, assets, and liabilities following *ADR* (2002) and *PUCL* (2003).
- **Certifying Conduct of Elections During President's Rule (`Article 356(5)`):** Advises the President on whether elections can be held in a State under President's Rule to determine whether an extension beyond one year is constitutionally permissible.

#### 2. Advisory Powers and Functions

- **Post-Election Disqualification of MPs (`Article 103`):** Advises the **President** on all questions regarding the post-election disqualification of Members of Parliament under `Article 102(1)` (such as holding an office of profit, unsoundness of mind, undischarged insolvency, foreign citizenship, or statutory disqualifications under RPA, 1951). Crucially, under `Article 103(2)`, the **opinion of the Election Commission is BINDING on the President**, who *"shall act according to such opinion."*
- **Post-Election Disqualification of MLAs and MLCs (`Article 192`):** Advises the **Governor** on identical post-election disqualification questions relating to Members of State Legislatures under `Article 191(1)`; again, under `Article 192(2)`, the **ECI's opinion is BINDING on the Governor**.

> [!prelims]
> **Critical Disqualification Distinction (`Art. 103/192` vs `Tenth Schedule`):**
> - **Ordinary Disqualifications (`Arts. 102(1) / 191(1)` — e.g., Office of Profit, RPA conviction):** Decided by the **President / Governor** acting on the **BINDING opinion of the ECI** (`Arts. 103 & 192`).
> - **Anti-Defection Disqualifications (`Arts. 102(2) / 191(2)` & `Tenth Schedule`):** Decided solely by the **Presiding Officer of the House** (Speaker / Chairman)—**NOT the President, Governor, or ECI!** The ECI has **ZERO role** in Tenth Schedule defection adjudications.

#### 3. Quasi-Judicial Powers and Functions

- **Registration and Recognition of Political Parties (`Section 29A`, RPA 1951):** Registers political parties and accords them the status of **Recognised National Party** or **Recognised State Party** based on objective poll-performance criteria laid down in the **Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968**. Note a classic Prelims nuance: while the ECI can *register* a party under `Section 29A`, the Supreme Court in ***Indian National Congress (I) v. Institute of Social Welfare* (2002)** held that the ECI has **no general statutory power to de-register** a political party except on three narrow grounds (registration obtained by fraud/forgery, party ceasing to bear allegiance to the Constitution, or being declared unlawful by the Central Government). However, the ECI can **delist** dormant Registered Unrecognised Political Parties (RUPPs) that fail to contest elections for six consecutive years.
- **Adjudication of Party Splits and Election Symbol Disputes (`Paragraph 15`, Symbols Order 1968):** Acts as a quasi-judicial tribunal to settle disputes between rival factions claiming to be the real recognised political party and seeking its reserved election symbol (e.g., Samajwadi Party 2017, Shiv Sena 2023, NCP 2024). Upheld by the Supreme Court in ***Sadiq Ali v. Election Commission of India* (1971)**, the ECI applies the **"Test of Majority"**—examining numerical dominance in both the legislative wing and the organisational wing of the party.
- **Disqualification for Failure to Lodge Election Accounts (`Section 10A`, RPA 1951) and Removal/Reduction of Disqualification (`Section 11`):** Acts quasi-judicially to disqualify candidates for up to three years for failing to submit campaign expenditure accounts, and holds statutory power under `Section 11` to remove or reduce certain periods of statutory disqualification (except corrupt-practice disqualifications ordered by the President under `Section 8A`).
- **Appointment of Inquiry Officers:** Appoints officers to inquire into disputes concerning electoral arrangements and exercises civil court-like powers under `Section 146` of RPA, 1951 while inquiring into `Article 103` / `192` disqualification references.

### Electoral Reforms and Mains Analytical Evaluation of the ECI

For UPSC Mains GS Paper II, the Election Commission presents a compelling paradox: internationally admired as the logistical miracle-worker that administers elections for nearly **one billion registered voters** across over a million polling stations with minimal violence, yet simultaneously confronting mounting domestic scrutiny over **institutional independence, appointment neutrality, internal collegiality, electoral roll transparency, and uneven MCC enforcement**.

> [!debate]
> **The 2023 Appointment Act Controversy — Executive Accountability vs Institutional Independence:**
> - **Arguments Defending the CEC Act, 2023:** Proponents invoke the **doctrine of separation of powers**, contending that appointing executive/constitutional functionaries is not a judicial function and that seating the CJI on the Selection Committee creates a conflict of interest whenever the Supreme Court later adjudicates election disputes or challenges to the ECI's actions. They argue that `Article 324(2)` expressly entrusted the selection law to an elected **Parliament**, that including the Leader of Opposition marks an improvement over the pre-2023 unilateral executive system, and that the CJI is absent from appointment committees for statutory watchdogs like the CVC and CIC as well.
> - **Arguments Challenging the CEC Act, 2023:** Critics (including the petitions before the Constitution Bench in *Dr. Jaya Thakur*) contend that replacing the CJI with a Prime Minister-nominated Cabinet Minister hands the incumbent executive a permanent **2:1 mathematical veto**, reducing the Leader of Opposition to a ceremonial bystander and subverting the core ratio of *Anoop Baranwal*—namely, that an umpire regulating political competition cannot be chosen exclusively by the dominant contestant. Combined with `Section 8(2)` (allowing bypassing of the Search Committee's shortlist) and `Section 16` (unprecedented lifelong civil and criminal immunity), critics warn that the 2023 Act institutionalises executive dominance while eroding legal accountability.

**Priority Reform Blueprint (Synthesising Goswami 1990, 2nd ARC 2007, 255th Law Commission 2015, and *Anoop Baranwal* 2023):**
1. **Genuinely Neutral Appointment Mechanism:** Rebalance the Selection Committee so that neither the ruling executive nor the opposition holds an automatic unilateral majority (for instance, pairing the PM, LoP Lok Sabha, and either the CJI / Speaker / Chairman of Rajya Sabha, or requiring a consensus / supermajority vote alongside transparent, publicly disclosed shortlisting criteria).
2. **Equal Constitutional Removal Shield for All Commissioners:** Amend `Article 324(5)` to extend the **Supreme Court Judge-like impeachment safeguard** equally to the two Election Commissioners, eliminating their dependence on the CEC's recommendation and ensuring fearless dissent during collegial deliberations.
3. **Independent Secretariat Charged on the Consolidated Fund of India:** Establish a dedicated, permanent **Election Commission Secretariat** (modelled on `Article 98` for Parliament) whose administrative expenditure is **charged** on the CFI rather than voted through the Law Ministry's Demands for Grants.
4. **Post-Retirement Cooling-Off / Constitutional Bar:** Insert a constitutional prohibition akin to `Article 148(4)` (CAG) or `Article 319` (UPSC) barring retired CECs and ECs from accepting any post-retirement appointment under the Union or State Governments (including Governorships or party nominations) for at least a substantial cooling-off period—or permanently—to remove every incentive of executive appeasement.
5. **Statutory Backing for MCC, De-registration Powers, and Transparent Collegiality:** Confer calibrated statutory authority on the ECI to de-register shell/inactive parties and penalise serious MCC/paid-news violations with reasoned, time-bound, publicly recorded orders—including transparent minuting of `Section 18` Commission deliberations and open auditability of electoral roll revisions (such as SIR exercises) and VVPAT/EVM protocols.

## 2. Public Service Commissions: UPSC, SPSC and JSPSC

If the Election Commission safeguards the integrity of the **political executive**, the **Public Service Commissions** safeguard the integrity, impartiality, and competence of the **permanent executive**. Known across Indian constitutional jurisprudence as the **"watch-dogs of the merit system"**, the Union Public Service Commission (**UPSC**) and State Public Service Commissions (**SPSCs**) are entrenched under **Part XIV, Chapter II (`Articles 315 to 323`)** of the Constitution. Their overriding constitutional purpose—first articulated in the **Macaulay Committee Report (1854)** and institutionalised in India following the **Royal Commission on the Superior Civil Services in India (Lee Commission, 1924)**—is to insulate public recruitment, promotions, and civil service discipline from political patronage, nepotism, and the "spoils system."

Institutional continuity here runs deeper than the Constitution itself. Pursuant to the `Government of India Act, 1919` and the Lee Commission's recommendations, India's first **Public Service Commission (Central)** was formally constituted on **1 October 1926** under **Sir Ross Barker**, meaning the UPSC marked its **centenary on 1 October 2026**. The `Government of India Act, 1935` reconstituted it as the **Federal Public Service Commission (FPSC)** alongside Provincial Public Service Commissions (PPSCs) and Joint Public Service Commissions (JPSCs). On **26 January 1950**, the FPSC transitioned seamlessly under `Article 378` into the **Union Public Service Commission**, acquiring elevated constitutional independence as an autonomous guardian of meritocracy.

### Composition, Qualifications, Acting Chairmen, and Tenure

Under `Article 316(1)`, the Chairman and other members of the **UPSC** (and any **JSPSC**) are appointed by the **President of India**, while the Chairman and members of an **SPSC** are appointed by the **Governor of the State**. Crucially, the **Constitution does not specify the numerical strength** of either the UPSC or any SPSC; instead, `Article 318` delegates the determination of Commission size and staff conditions to the President (for UPSC/JSPSC) and the Governor (for SPSC). By settled practice under the **UPSC (Members) Regulations, 1969**, the UPSC ordinarily consists of a **Chairman and up to ten other members** (typically 9 to 11 in total), while State PSCs vary from 3 to 15 members depending on State size.

#### The Sole Constitutional Qualification (Article 316(1) Proviso)

Just like `Article 324` for the Election Commission, the Constitution refrains from prescribing academic degrees, judicial background, or age minimums for PSC members—with **one vital constitutional exception** inserted via the proviso to `Article 316(1)`:
- **As nearly as may be, one-half of the members of every Public Service Commission** must be persons who, on the dates of their respective appointments, **have held office for at least ten years either under the Government of India or under the Government of a State**.
- The remaining half ("non-official" members) may be drawn from any field of public eminence—academics, scientists, jurists, engineers, economists, or distinguished professionals—without any mandatory government-service requirement.

#### Acting Chairman (Article 316(1A)) and Tenure (Article 316(2))

Inserted by the **`15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963`**, `Article 316(1A)` empowers the **President** (in the case of UPSC/JSPSC) or the **Governor** (in the case of an SPSC) to appoint one of the existing members of the Commission as **Acting Chairman** in two contingencies: (a) when the office of Chairman falls vacant, or (b) when the Chairman is unable to perform his duties by reason of absence or any other cause. The Acting Chairman serves until a regular Chairman assumes office or the incumbent resumes duty.

Tenure rules under `Article 316(2)` establish an important age differential between the Union and the States:
- **UPSC Chairman and Members:** Hold office for a term of **6 years** from the date of entering office, or until attaining the age of **65 years**, whichever is earlier.
- **SPSC and JSPSC Chairman and Members:** Hold office for a term of **6 years** or until attaining the age of **62 years**, whichever is earlier. Originally, the Constitution fixed the retirement age for SPSC/JSPSC members at **60 years**; this was raised to **62 years** by the **`41st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976`** to retain experienced administrators and attract talent at the State level.
- **Resignation and Non-Reappointment:** A member may resign at any time by writing to the **President** (for UPSC/JSPSC) or to the **Governor** (for SPSC). Under `Article 316(3)`, a person who holds office as a member of a PSC is **ineligible for re-appointment to that same office** for a second term (even if he served less than six years before retiring or resigning, he cannot be given a fresh term in the exact same post).

### Constitutional Removal Procedure Under Article 317: The Asymmetric Federal Shield

No provision in `Part XIV` produces more Prelims errors than `Article 317`. Aspirants instinctively assume that because the Governor appoints an SPSC Chairman and Members, the Governor must also hold the power to remove them. **That assumption is completely false.** Under `Article 317(1)` and `317(3)`, **ONLY the President of India can remove the Chairman or any Member of ALL THREE Commissions—UPSC, SPSC, AND JSPSC.** The framers deliberately centralised the removal power in the Union President—backed by the Supreme Court—to ensure that partisan State Chief Ministers could never intimidate or purge independent SPSC members who resisted political interference in state recruitments.

#### Route 1: Removal for Misbehaviour — Mandatory Binding Supreme Court Inquiry (Article 317(1) and (4))

If the Chairman or any member of UPSC, SPSC, or JSPSC is sought to be removed on the ground of **"misbehaviour"**, the President **cannot remove him unilaterally**:
1. **Presidential Reference to Supreme Court:** The President must refer the matter to the **Supreme Court** for an inquiry under `Article 317(1)`.
2. **Interim Suspension (`Article 317(2)`):** During the pendency of the Supreme Court's inquiry (from the date of reference until the President passes final orders on receipt of the Court's report), the **President** may suspend a UPSC or JSPSC Chairman/Member, whereas the **Governor** may suspend an SPSC Chairman/Member. *(This is the ONLY disciplinary power a Governor exercises over an SPSC member—temporary suspension pending SC inquiry, NEVER removal!)*
3. **Supreme Court Inquiry and Binding Advice:** The Supreme Court conducts a formal judicial inquiry in accordance with the procedure prescribed under `Article 145` (guaranteeing full natural justice and evidence recording) and submits its report advising whether the member ought to be removed. Crucially, the **advice tendered by the Supreme Court under `Article 317(1)` is BINDING on the President**—unlike ordinary advisory opinions under `Article 143`!
4. **Constitutional Meaning of 'Misbehaviour' (`Article 317(4)`):** While the Constitution does not exhaustively define misbehaviour, `Article 317(4)` explicitly deems a Chairman or member guilty of misbehaviour if he (a) is or becomes in any way **concerned or interested in any contract or agreement** made by or on behalf of the Government of India or a State Government, or (b) **participates in any way in the profit** thereof or in any benefit or emolument arising therefrom, otherwise than as a member and in common with the other members of an incorporated company. In landmark references such as ***In Re: Reference under Article 317(1) (Dr. Ram Ashray Yadav, Chairman, Bihar PSC)* (2000)** and ***In Re: Mehar Singh Saini, Chairman, Haryana PSC* (2010)**, the Supreme Court clarified that institutional integrity, financial impropriety, nepotism, or conduct eroding public faith in the Commission also constitutes constitutional misbehaviour—though minor administrative errors made in good faith do not.

#### Route 2: Direct Removal by the President Without Supreme Court Inquiry (Article 317(3))

Under `Article 317(3)`, the President may remove the Chairman or any member of the UPSC, an SPSC, or a JSPSC **directly, without referring the matter to the Supreme Court**, on **three objective grounds**:
1. **Insolvency (`Art. 317(3)(a)`):** If he is adjudged an **insolvent** (bankrupt);
2. **Outside Paid Employment (`Art. 317(3)(b)`):** If he engages during his term of office in **any paid employment outside the duties of his office**; or
3. **Infirmity of Mind or Body (`Art. 317(3)(c)`):** If he is, in the opinion of the President, **unfit to continue in office by reason of infirmity of mind or body**.

> [!prelims]
> **Critical Removal Contrast — Do NOT Confuse `Art. 317` with `Art. 124(4)`!**
> - **Parliament has NO role** in removing the Chairman or Members of the UPSC, SPSC, or JSPSC! Unlike the CEC, CAG, or Supreme Court/High Court Judges (who are removed via a Parliamentary special majority address), PSC members are removed by the **President following a binding Supreme Court inquiry** (for misbehaviour) or directly by the President (for insolvency, outside paid employment, or mental/physical infirmity).
> - **Suspension vs Removal for SPSC:** For an SPSC member facing a Supreme Court inquiry, the **Governor** can **suspend** (`Art. 317(2)`), whereas **ONLY the President** can **remove** (`Art. 317(1)`).

### Independence Safeguards and the Strict Article 319 Post-Retirement Matrix

Beyond `Article 317` removal security, the Constitution fortifies Public Service Commissions with three formidable institutional shields:
1. **Non-Variation of Service Conditions (`Article 318` Proviso):** Although the President/Governor determines conditions of service, these **cannot be varied to a member's disadvantage** after appointment.
2. **Expenditure Charged on the Consolidated Fund (`Article 322`):** The entire administrative expenses of the UPSC—including salaries, allowances, and pensions of the Chairman, members, and the Commission's staff—are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** (and for an SPSC, on the **Consolidated Fund of the State**). Being charged expenditure, they are **non-votable** by Parliament or State Legislatures, giving PSCs financial autonomy that even the Election Commission lacks!
3. **Strict Constitutional Prohibition on Post-Retirement Employment (`Article 319`):** To prevent members from courting political favours in exchange for lucrative sinecures after retirement, `Article 319` imposes the most meticulously calibrated post-retirement re-employment matrix anywhere in the Indian Constitution.

| Outgoing Constitutional Office | Eligible for Further Employment Under GoI or Any State Govt? | Specifically Permitted Constitutional PSC Offices Under `Article 319` | Strictly Prohibited Offices |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chairman of UPSC** (`Art. 319(a)`) | **NO** — Complete, absolute bar! | **NONE.** Zero further appointment under GoI or any State Government. | Any Union/State Govt post, any SPSC post, or second term as UPSC Chairman. |
| **Member of UPSC** (`Art. 319(c)`) | **NO** — General bar under GoI/State Govt | Eligible **ONLY** for appointment as **(1) Chairman of UPSC**, OR **(2) Chairman of any SPSC**. | Cannot be reappointed as UPSC Member (`Art. 316(3)`), cannot become SPSC Member, and cannot take any other Union/State Govt job. |
| **Chairman of an SPSC** (`Art. 319(b)`) | **NO** — General bar under GoI/State Govt | Eligible **ONLY** for appointment as **(1) Chairman of UPSC**, **(2) Member of UPSC**, OR **(3) Chairman of any OTHER SPSC**. | Cannot be reappointed as Chairman of the *same* SPSC (`Art. 316(3)`), cannot become a Member of any SPSC, and cannot take any other Union/State Govt job. |
| **Member of an SPSC** (`Art. 319(d)`) | **NO** — General bar under GoI/State Govt | Eligible **ONLY** for appointment as **(1) Chairman of UPSC**, **(2) Member of UPSC**, OR **(3) Chairman of the SAME or any OTHER SPSC**. | Cannot be reappointed as Member of the *same* SPSC (`Art. 316(3)`), cannot become Member of another SPSC, and cannot take any other Union/State Govt job. |

> [!case]
> ***Hargovind Pant v. Dr. Raghukul Tilak* (1979, 5-Judge Constitution Bench) — The "Governor Exception" to Article 319:** When former Rajasthan PSC Member Dr. Raghukul Tilak was appointed Governor of Rajasthan, his appointment was challenged as barred by `Article 319(d)`. A five-judge Constitution Bench unanimously **upheld** his appointment, holding that the office of **Governor of a State** is an independent constitutional office and **NOT an "employment under the Government of India or under the Government of a State"** within the meaning of `Article 319`. Similarly, constitutional or judicial appointments not subordinate to executive control—such as a High Court/Supreme Court Judgeship or elected political office (MP/MLA/Minister)—fall outside the bar of `Article 319`.

### Functions, Limitations, and the Advisory Character of PSCs

Under `Article 320`, the UPSC (for Union and All-India Services) and SPSCs (for State Services) perform two broad sets of constitutional duties—**recruitment functions** and **advisory personnel functions**—whose scope can be enlarged by legislative enactment under `Article 321`.

#### 1. Core Constitutional Functions (Article 320(1)–(3))

- **Conducting Examinations (`Art. 320(1)`):** Conducts examinations for appointment to the services of the Union (Civil Services Examination, Indian Forest Service, Engineering Services, Combined Defence Services, NDA & NA, CAPF, Combined Medical Services, Indian Economic/Statistical Service) and the services of the States respectively.
- **Joint Recruitment Assistance (`Art. 320(2)`):** It is the constitutional duty of the **UPSC**, if requested by **any two or more States**, to assist those States in framing and operating schemes of joint recruitment for any services requiring special qualifications.
- **Mandatory Consultation on Personnel Management (`Art. 320(3)`):** The Union Government or State Government shall consult the respective Commission on:
  1. All matters relating to **methods of recruitment** to civil services and civil posts;
  2. The **principles to be followed** in making appointments to civil services and posts, in making **promotions and transfers** from one service to another, and on the **suitability of candidates** for such appointments, promotions, or transfers;
  3. **All disciplinary matters** affecting a person serving under the Government of India or a State Government in a civil capacity—including memorials or petitions relating to such matters (encompassing censure, withholding of increments or promotion, recovery of pecuniary loss, reduction to a lower stage/rank, compulsory retirement, removal, and dismissal from service);
  4. Any claim by a civil servant for **reimbursement of legal expenses** incurred in defending legal proceedings instituted against him in respect of acts done in the execution of official duty;
  5. Any claim for the award of a **pension in respect of injuries** sustained while serving under the Government in a civil capacity, and any question as to the amount of such award; and
  6. Any other matter referred to the Commission by the President or Governor.
- **Statutory Extension of Functions (`Article 321`):** **Parliament** (for UPSC) and a **State Legislature** (for SPSC) may by law confer additional functions relating to the services of the Union/State, or the personnel systems of any **local authority, body corporate, or public institution** constituted by law. Separately, under the Supreme Court's landmark police-reform directives in ***Prakash Singh v. Union of India* (2006)**, the UPSC also plays a pivotal **empanelment role**, shortlisting a panel of three senior-most IPS officers from which State Governments must select their Director General of Police (DGP).

#### 2. Crucial Constitutional Exclusions and Limitations (Article 320(3) Proviso and Article 320(4))

UPSC Prelims frequently constructs trick statements around what lies **outside** the jurisdiction of Public Service Commissions:
1. **No Consultation on Reservations or SC/ST Claims (`Article 320(4)`):** Nothing in `Article 320(3)` requires a Public Service Commission to be consulted regarding the manner in which **reservations for any backward class of citizens (`Article 16(4)`)** are made, or regarding the manner in which effect is given to the **claims of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (`Article 335`)** in appointments to services and posts!
2. **Executive Power to Exclude Posts (`Article 320(3)` Proviso & `Article 320(5)`):** The President (for Union/All-India Services) and Governor (for State Services) may make regulations specifying matters in which it shall **not be necessary** to consult the Commission (e.g., the **UPSC (Exemption from Consultation) Regulations, 1958**, updated periodically including in March 2024). Under `Article 320(5)`, all such exemption regulations must be laid before each House of Parliament (or the State Legislature) for **not less than 14 days**, and are subject to modification or repeal by the Legislature. Matters traditionally excluded include chairmanship/membership of statutory commissions and tribunals, highest diplomatic posts, Group C and Group D posts (now largely recruited by the **Staff Selection Commission**, Railway Recruitment Boards, and banking boards), and temporary/officiating appointments not exceeding one year.
3. **Purely Advisory Role — Not a Personnel Manager:** Neither the UPSC nor any SPSC classifies civil services, fixes pay scales, manages cadre training, or dictates service conditions—at the Union level, those executive functions belong to the **Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT)** under the Ministry of Personnel, Public Grievances and Pensions, which acts as the central personnel agency. Furthermore, in ***State of U.P. v. Manbodhan Lal Srivastava* (1957, 5-Judge Constitution Bench)**, the Supreme Court settled that the consultation requirement under `Article 320(3)(c)` is **directory rather than mandatory**—non-consultation or irregular consultation with the UPSC/SPSC does **not** render a government disciplinary order void or confer a judicially enforceable right on a civil servant.
4. **Accountability Mechanism for Rejecting Advice (`Article 323`):** Although PSC advice is legally non-binding, the Constitution creates a potent **parliamentary safeguard** against arbitrary rejection. Under `Article 323`, the UPSC presents an **Annual Report** to the President (and each SPSC to its Governor), who must cause a copy to be laid before Parliament (or the State Legislature) **together with a memorandum explaining, as respects the cases where the advice of the Commission was not accepted, the reasons for such non-acceptance**. At the Union level, an individual ministry **cannot** unilaterally reject UPSC advice—every proposed disagreement must be approved by the **Appointments Committee of the Cabinet (ACC)**!
5. **Institutional Overlap with CVC (1964/2003):** Since the creation of the **Central Vigilance Commission (CVC)** in 1964 (given statutory status in 2003), both UPSC and CVC advise the Union Government on disciplinary vigilance cases involving civil servants. Whenever their advice diverges, the **UPSC—being an independent constitutional authority—holds institutional edge over the statutory CVC**, though dual consultation has occasionally lengthened disciplinary timelines.

### Joint State Public Service Commission (JSPSC): Statutory Body Under Article 315(2)

A classic UPSC Prelims distinction separates the **UPSC and SPSCs** (which are directly constituted by the Constitution) from a **Joint State Public Service Commission (JSPSC)**. Under `Article 315(2)`, two or more States may agree that there shall be one Public Service Commission for that group of States; if a **resolution to that effect is passed by the House (or each House) of the Legislature of each of those States**, **Parliament may by law** provide for the appointment of a **Joint State Public Service Commission** to serve their needs.

- **Statutory, NOT Constitutional:** Because a JSPSC does not exist automatically by force of the Constitution and comes into existence **only via an Act of Parliament** passed on the request of the concerned State Legislatures, a **JSPSC is a STATUTORY body** (even though its enabling mechanism sits in `Article 315(2)`!). The classic historical example was the short-lived JSPSC constituted for **Punjab and Haryana in 1966** immediately following Punjab's reorganisation.
- **Presidential Governance:** Despite serving States, a JSPSC's Chairman and Members are **appointed by the President**, hold office for **6 years or age 62**, can be **suspended or removed ONLY by the President** (`Art. 317`), submit resignations to the **President**, and have their strength and service conditions fixed by the **President** (`Art. 318`).
- **Dual Reporting (`Article 323(2)`):** A JSPSC submits its Annual Performance Report to **EACH of the Governors of the concerned States**, and each Governor lays the report (with any non-acceptance memorandum) before his respective State Legislature.
- **Do Not Confuse JSPSC (`Art. 315(2)`) with UPSC Serving a State (`Art. 315(4)`):** Under `Article 315(4)`, the **UPSC itself** may agree to serve all or any of the needs of a single State if the **Governor of that State makes a request** and the **President approves**—no Parliamentary statute is required in that case.

## 3. Comptroller and Auditor General of India

In parliamentary democracy, the executive proposes taxes and spends public money, while Parliament authorises appropriations on behalf of the citizenry. Yet legislative financial control remains hollow unless an impartial, technically competent authority outside the executive verifies whether every rupee was spent lawfully, economically, and strictly for the purpose sanctioned by Parliament. That pivotal accountability anchor under the Indian Constitution is the **Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG)**—established under **Part V, Chapter V (`Articles 148 to 151`)**.

Head of the **Indian Audit and Accounts Department (IA&AD)** and universally celebrated as the **"Guardian of the Public Purse"**, the CAG controls the entire financial auditing system of the country at **both the Union and State levels**. Crucially, just like the Election Commission and the unified judiciary, the CAG is a **single integrated all-India constitutional office**: India has **no separate State Comptroller and Auditor Generals**. State accounts and audits are conducted by State Accountants General working directly under the CAG's superintendence. During the Constituent Assembly Debates on **30 May 1949**, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar elevated the CAG above every other non-judicial—and in functional importance even judicial—constitutional dignitary, regretting only that the draft had not placed the CAG's staff appointment powers on par with the Supreme Court:

> "Personally speaking for myself, I am of opinion that this dignitary or officer is probably the most important officer in the Constitution of India. He is the one man who is going to see that the expenses voted by Parliament are not exceeded, or varied from what has been laid down by Parliament in what is called the Appropriation Act." — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, Vol. VIII, 30 May 1949

### Appointment, Oath, Tenure, Removal, and Constitutional Safeguards

The institutional architecture of `Article 148` closely mirrors that of a **Supreme Court Judge**, ensuring that the nation's supreme auditor operates without fear of executive reprisal or expectation of post-retirement favour.

#### Appointment and Oath (Article 148(1)–(2))

- **Appointment:** Under `Article 148(1)`, the CAG is appointed by the **President of India by a warrant under his hand and seal**. As in the pre-2023 ECI regime, the Constitution prescribes **no formal qualifications** and **no selection committee** for the CAG. By executive practice—disclosed formally on oath by the Union Government in September 2026—the Cabinet Secretariat prepares a shortlist (traditionally from senior IAS or IA&AS officers), the Finance Minister approves the shortlist, the Prime Minister recommends a single name, and the President issues the warrant.
- **Oath or Affirmation (`Article 148(2)` & `Third Schedule`):** Before entering office, the CAG makes and subscribes an oath before the President (or a person appointed by him) in the form set out in the **`Third Schedule`**—promising to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution, uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India, duly and faithfully perform the duties of his office to the best of his ability, knowledge, and judgment without fear or favour, affection or ill-will, and **uphold the Constitution and the laws**. Notably, this oath formula is **word-for-word identical to the oath of Supreme Court Judges**!

#### Tenure, Resignation, and Removal (Article 148(1) Proviso and 1971 Act)

- **Tenure (Statutory, NOT Constitutional):** The Constitution leaves the CAG's term of office to Parliament. Under **`Section 4` of the Comptroller and Auditor-General's (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971**, the CAG holds office for a term of **6 years** or until attaining the age of **65 years**, whichever is earlier. He may resign at any time by writing to the President.
- **Security of Tenure & Removal Procedure (`Article 148(1)` Proviso):** Although appointed by the President, the CAG **does NOT hold office during the President's pleasure**. He can be removed from office **only in like manner and on the like grounds as a Judge of the Supreme Court** (`Article 124(4)`)—meaning an order of the President passed after an address by **both Houses of Parliament** supported by a **special majority** (majority of total membership + at least two-thirds of members present and voting) on grounds of **proved misbehaviour or incapacity**.

#### Six Pillars of Constitutional Independence (Article 148)

1. **Security of Tenure:** Impeachment-equivalent parliamentary removal threshold (`Art. 148(1)` proviso).
2. **Absolute Constitutional Bar on Post-Retirement Government Office (`Article 148(4)`):** After ceasing to hold office, the CAG is **ineligible for further office either under the Government of India or under the Government of any State**. Unlike the Election Commission (which has no constitutional post-retirement bar), the CAG faces a total constitutional embargo equivalent to the UPSC Chairman. *(Note, however, that as with `Art. 319`, courts and constitutional practice distinguish government employment from independent constitutional offices or elected legislative seats—prompting recurring ethics debates whenever retired CAGs have subsequently become State Governors, MPs, or heads of statutory/banking advisory bodies.)*
3. **Salary Parity and Non-Variation Shield (`Article 148(3)` & `Second Schedule`):** The CAG's salary and service conditions are determined by Parliament by law—fixed at parity with a **Judge of the Supreme Court**—and neither his salary nor his rights regarding leave, pension, or retirement age can be **varied to his disadvantage** after appointment.
4. **Consultative Control Over Audit Staff (`Article 148(5)`):** Subject to the Constitution and Parliamentary laws, the conditions of service of persons serving in the **Indian Audit and Accounts Department** and the administrative powers of the CAG are prescribed by the President **only after consultation with the CAG**.
5. **Charged Expenditure (`Article 148(6)`):** All administrative expenses of the CAG's office—including all salaries, allowances, and pensions payable to the CAG and IA&AD personnel—are **charged upon the Consolidated Fund of India** and are therefore **non-votable** by Parliament.
6. **Complete Insulation from Ministerial Representation:** **No Minister can represent the CAG in Parliament** (in either House), and no Minister can be called upon to assume responsibility for any action or audit finding of the CAG. The CAG is an independent constitutional authority accountable **solely to Parliament**—never a subordinate department of the Ministry of Finance.

### Duties, Powers, and Audit Jurisdiction: Articles 149–151 and the 1971 Act

`Article 149` empowers Parliament to prescribe the duties and powers of the CAG in relation to the accounts of the Union, the States, and any other authority or body. Exercising this mandate, Parliament enacted the **Comptroller and Auditor-General's (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971** (significantly amended in **1976** to separate Union accounting from auditing). Alongside `Articles 150`, `151`, and `279`, this statute constructs a sweeping public-finance oversight architecture:

| Constitutional / Statutory Anchor | Functional Domain | Exact Scope, Mechanics & Prelims Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| **`Art. 149` & `Sec. 13` (1971 Act)** | **Consolidated, Contingency & Public Accounts** | Audits **ALL expenditure** from the Consolidated Fund of India, Consolidated Fund of each State, and Consolidated Fund of each **UT with a Legislative Assembly** (Delhi, Puducherry, J&K); audits all transactions of **Contingency Funds** and **Public Accounts** of both Union and States. |
| **`Sec. 16` (1971 Act)** | **Audit of Receipts** | Audits **all receipts** (tax and non-tax revenues) payable into the Consolidated Fund of India and of each State/UT with Assembly—checking revenue assessment, collection, and leakage (e.g., direct tax, customs, GST compliance). |
| **`Sec. 17` (1971 Act)** | **Stores, Stock & Departmental Commercial Accounts** | Audits all trading, manufacturing, profit and loss accounts, balance sheets, subsidiary accounts, and stores/stock records maintained by any Union or State government department. |
| **`Secs. 14 & 15` (1971 Act)** | **Bodies & Authorities Substantially Financed by Grants/Loans** | Audits receipts and expenditure of any body/authority **substantially financed** from Union or State revenues (grant/loan in a financial year **≥ ₹25 lakh AND ≥ 75% of total expenditure**, OR **≥ ₹1 crore** unconditionally under `Sec. 14(2)`), and audits specific-purpose grants/loans under `Sec. 15`. |
| **`Sec. 19` (1971 Act) & Companies Act, 2013** | **Government Companies & Statutory Corporations** | Audits all **Government companies** (appoints statutory auditors and conducts supplementary/test audit) and statutory corporations where governing laws mandate CAG audit (see **Three-Tier Public Corporation Classification** below!). |
| **`Sec. 20` (1971 Act)** | **Audit on Presidential / Gubernatorial Request** | Audits accounts of **any other body or authority** (such as **Panchayati Raj Institutions, Municipalities**, or private-public joint ventures) when **requested by the President or a State Governor** on terms agreed with CAG. |
| **`Art. 150`** | **Form of Union & State Accounts** | Advises the President on the **form** in which the accounts of the Union and of the States shall be kept (President prescribes the form **on the advice of the CAG**). |
| **`Art. 279`** | **Net Proceeds Tax Certification** | Ascertains and certifies the **"net proceeds"** of any tax or duty (gross proceeds minus cost of collection) for Union–State tax devolution—and **`Art. 279(1)` declares the CAG's certificate FINAL!** |
| **`Art. 151`** | **Submission of Three Audit Reports** | Submits **three audit reports** (Appropriation Accounts, Finance Accounts, Public Undertakings) to the **President** (for Union) and **Governor** (for each State), who lay them before Parliament / State Legislatures. |

#### The 1976 Separation of Accounts from Audit — Critical Federal Asymmetry

Before 1976, the CAG performed a **dual role** at both the Union and State levels: compiling and maintaining government accounts *and* auditing those same accounts—an obvious institutional conflict of interest because an auditor should not audit accounts compiled by his own department. By amending the 1971 Act in **1976**, the Union Government **separated accounts from audit at the Central level**, transferring Union accounting functions to the newly established **Controller General of Accounts (CGA)** under the Department of Expenditure, Ministry of Finance (supported by departmental Chief Controllers of Accounts).

> [!prelims]
> **Two High-Frequency UPSC Prelims Traps on CAG Functions:**
> 1. **Union vs State Accounting (`1976` Amendment Trap):** The 1976 separation applied **ONLY to the Central Government**! At the **State level**, the CAG **STILL compiles and maintains the accounts** of State Governments (through State Accountants General) alongside auditing them (except where a few States like Goa have separate arrangements for specific accounts).
> 2. **Three-Tier Audit Classification of Public Corporations (M. Laxmikanth Classic):**
>    - **Tier 1 — Totally & Directly Audited by CAG:** Corporations whose statutes vest sole audit in CAG (e.g., **Damodar Valley Corporation**, **Oil and Natural Gas Commission (ONGC)** historically before corporatisation, **Air India / Indian Airlines** historically).
>    - **Tier 2 — Audited by Private Auditors Appointed by Govt in Consultation with CAG + CAG Supplementary Audit:** Corporations where CAG retains supplementary/test audit power (e.g., **Central Warehousing Corporation**, **Industrial Finance Corporation**).
>    - **Tier 3 — Totally OUTSIDE CAG Audit (Private Auditors Only):** Commercial/financial institutions audited exclusively by private chartered accountants who submit reports directly to Parliament without CAG involvement—most famously the **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)**, **State Bank of India (SBI)**, **Life Insurance Corporation (LIC)**, and **Food Corporation of India (FCI)**!
> 3. **Secret Service Expenditure Limitation:** The CAG **cannot** call for vouchers or operational particulars of **secret service expenditure** (e.g., intelligence agencies like R&AW or IB); he must accept a formal certificate from the competent administrative authority certifying that the expenditure was duly incurred under its authority.

#### Three Dimensions of Audit: Regulatory, Propriety, and Performance

Over seven decades, the CAG's methodology has evolved well beyond clerical voucher-checking into a multi-layered public-accountability discipline:
1. **Legal and Regulatory (Compliance) Audit (Obligatory):** Verifies that funds were legally available for the applicable service, properly sanctioned by competent authority, and spent strictly in accordance with the Appropriation Act, financial rules, and constitutional provisions.
2. **Propriety Audit (Discretionary):** Examines the **"wisdom, faithfulness, and economy"** of government expenditure—flagging wasteful spending, imprudent contract modifications, and irregular concessions even when the expenditure formally complied with technical rules. While administrative theorist **Paul H. Appleby** famously criticised CAG auditing in 1956 as an overly negative colonial relic that paralysed civil service initiative (suggesting the CAG be stripped of audit functions), Indian parliamentary practice decisively rejected Appleby's thesis, recognising propriety audit as indispensable against executive leakages.
3. **Performance (Value-for-Money) Audit:** Evaluates large welfare schemes, infrastructure projects, revenue concessions, and public-private partnerships (PPPs) on the international **3Es framework—Economy, Efficiency, and Effectiveness**. High-profile CAG performance audits—ranging from **2G Spectrum (2010)** and **Coal Block Allocations (2012)** to **Dwarka Expressway / Bharatmala**, **Ayushman Bharat–PMJAY**, and **GST compliance**—demonstrated the CAG's power to surface systemic governance failures, even as they sparked intense debates over whether the CAG should quantify "presumptive losses" or critique executive policy choices.

#### The Three Reports (Article 151) and the CAG–PAC Partnership

Under `Article 151(1)` and `151(2)`, the CAG submits **three distinct audit reports** to the President (for the Union) and to the Governor (for each State), who cause them to be laid before Parliament and the respective State Legislatures:
1. **Audit Report on Appropriation Accounts:** Compares actual government expenditure against the expenditure formally sanctioned by Parliament/State Legislature in the Appropriation Act (exposing savings, excesses requiring regularisation, and unauthorised re-appropriations).
2. **Audit Report on Finance Accounts:** Presents a comprehensive statement of the annual receipts, disbursements, debt position, and fiscal balance of the Union or State Government.
3. **Audit Report on Public Undertakings:** Reviews the operational efficiency, financial health, and governance compliance of Central and State Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs).

Once laid on the table of the House, these reports stand automatically referred to Parliament's premier financial watchdog—the **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)** (for Appropriation and Finance Accounts) and the **Committee on Public Undertakings (CoPU)** (for PSU audits). Because the CAG assists the PAC in examining departmental Secretaries, framing technical questions, vetting Action Taken Notes, and ensuring executive compliance, constitutional convention celebrates the CAG as the **"guide, friend, and philosopher" of the Public Accounts Committee**.

```flow
# From Public Expenditure to Parliamentary Accountability: The CAG–PAC Audit Cycle
Parliamentary Appropriation (Arts. 114 & 266) | Parliament votes Demands for Grants & passes Appropriation Act; Union Executive draws funds via CGA WITHOUT prior CAG clearance
Executive Spending & Revenue Collection | Ministries, States, UTs with Assemblies, PSUs & grant-aided bodies execute budgets across the financial year
Ex-Post CAG Audit (Arts. 149–150 & 1971 Act) | Regulatory Audit (Legality) || Propriety Audit (Economy & Wisdom) || Performance Audit (3Es: Outcomes)
Submission of 3 Audit Reports (Art. 151) | CAG submits Appropriation, Finance & Public Undertakings Reports to President / Governor → laid before Parliament / State Legislature
Parliamentary Scrutiny (PAC & CoPU) | CAG acts as 'guide, friend & philosopher' to PAC; Secretaries summoned; Ministries file mandatory Action Taken Reports (ATRs)
```

### Indian CAG vs UK CAG: Why India's CAG Is an Auditor, Not a Comptroller

One of the sharpest conceptual contrasts in Indian Polity—repeatedly tested in both Prelims and Mains—lies in the mismatch between the CAG's **constitutional title** (*Comptroller and Auditor General*) and his **actual legal authority**. In India, the CAG performs **strictly the role of an *ex-post* Auditor General**; he exercises **ZERO "Comptroller" function**!

```compare
# Comptroller and Auditor General of India vs Comptroller and Auditor General of the United Kingdom
CAG of India (Arts. 148–151) | CAG of the United Kingdom
Actual Role :: Purely an EX-POST Auditor General — NO Comptroller role over release of public funds! | Truly BOTH Comptroller AND Auditor General
Control Over Exchequer Withdrawals :: Zero prior control — Ministries/CGA draw money from Consolidated Fund without CAG's prior approval; CAG audits ONLY AFTER expenditure is incurred | Strict prior control — NO money can be issued from the British Consolidated Fund (Exchequer) without the CAG's prior credit approval
Relationship with Parliament :: Independent constitutional authority — NOT a Member of Parliament and NOT an officer of Parliament; accountable to Parliament via Art. 151 reports | Statutory Officer of the House of Commons (heads the National Audit Office — NAO)
Appointment Process :: Appointed solely by President on executive advice (PM/Finance Minister/Cabinet Secy shortlist — currently challenged in SC 2024–2026) | Appointed by the Crown on an address moved by the PM in the House of Commons with the formal agreement of the Chair of the Public Accounts Committee (PAC)!
Federal Mandate :: Single integrated auditor for BOTH Union Government AND all 28 State Governments (and UTs with Assemblies) | Unitary parliamentary audit (separate devolved audit bodies for Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland)
```

### Contemporary Issues (2024–2026) and Mains Reform Agenda

Across **2024–2026**, the office of the CAG has moved to the forefront of India's constitutional accountability discourse—triggered by three converging debates: **executive monopoly over CAG appointments**, **audit scope over modern public-private spending**, and **timely tabling and follow-up of audit reports**.

> [!news]
> **Current CAG Leadership & Pending Supreme Court Collegium PILs (2024–2026):**
> - **Current 15th CAG (as of November 2026):** **K. Sanjay Murthy** (1989-batch IAS officer, former Secretary, Department of Higher Education) was sworn in by President Droupadi Murmu as the **Comptroller and Auditor General of India on 21 November 2024**, succeeding **Girish Chandra Murmu**.
> - **Live Supreme Court Challenge to Unilateral Executive Appointments (2024–2026):** Three tagged PILs—***Anupam Kulshreshtha v. Union of India*** (filed by retired Deputy CAGs and IA&AS veterans; notice issued January 2024), **Centre for Public Interest Litigation (CPIL)** (notice issued **17 March 2025**), and **Lok Prahari** (August 2025)—challenge the Centre's exclusive, opaque selection process under `Article 148(1)` as violating `Article 14` and the Constituent Assembly's vision, urging a **non-partisan selection collegium** (such as PM + LoP + CJI, or Speaker + LoP + PAC Chair + CJI).
> - **Union Government's September 2026 Affidavit:** In a formal counter-affidavit filed on **17 September 2026** in *Anupam Kulshreshtha*, the Union Government disclosed its internal executive sequence (Cabinet Secretariat shortlist → Finance Minister approval → Prime Minister recommendation → Presidential warrant) and defended it as a **"settled constitutional convention"**. Petitioners countered that the constitutional phrase *"by warrant under his hand and seal"* was consciously chosen by the framers to equate the CAG's independence with Supreme Court Judges, making executive self-selection of its own auditor inherently conflicted.

> [!debate]
> **CAG's Expanding Mandate vs Policy Overreach — Finding the Constitutional Balance:**
> - **Concerns Over Audit Dilution & Delayed Tabling:** Governance analysts and petitioners before the Supreme Court have flagged sharp dips in the annual number of Union CAG audit reports tabled in recent parliamentary sessions, prolonged delays by several State Governments in placing CAG reports before Assemblies (sometimes exceeding a year despite `Art. 151`'s clear constitutional duty), exclusion of **off-budget vehicles, Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs), and several PPP concessions** from automatic audit, and weak capacity in auditing over 2.5 lakh **Panchayats and Municipalities** despite massive Finance Commission devolutions.
> - **Concerns Over "Policy Paralysis" & Presumptive Loss Estimates:** Conversely, defenders of executive policy space warn (echoing former Finance Ministers and the *2G/Coal* era debate) that aggressive quantification of hypothetical "notional losses" without accounting for socio-economic welfare objectives can trigger risk aversion among honest civil servants, chilling bold public infrastructure decisions.
> - **Balanced Mains Synthesis (Vinod Rai Proposals, 2nd ARC & International INTOSAI Standards):** Modernise the **1971 CAG Act** to (1) codify a **multi-stakeholder Selection Committee** (incorporating the PM, LoP, and PAC Chair on the UK model); (2) mandate **time-bound tabling** of CAG reports in Parliament and State Legislatures (within 7–14 days of receipt during a session); (3) bring **all PPPs, SPVs, statutory regulators, and local-body grants** transparently within statutory audit jurisdiction (reinforced by the Supreme Court's 2014 ruling in ***Association of Unified Telecom Service Providers of India v. Union of India*** upholding CAG audit of private telecom revenue-sharing accounts); (4) empower CAG with real-time **AI/big-data continuous auditing** rather than merely lagging post-mortems; and (5) institutionalise legally enforceable timelines for departmental **Action Taken Reports (ATRs)** monitored jointly by CAG and PAC.

## 4. National Commissions for SCs, STs and Backward Classes, and Linguistic Minorities

Unlike purely procedural watchdogs such as the ECI or CAG, the fourth family of Indian constitutional bodies embodies the **transformative social-justice core** of the Constitution—translating the Preamble's promise of *Social, Economic, and Political Justice* and the equality guarantees of `Articles 15, 16, 17, 29, 30, 46, 330–342A, and 350A` into living administrative oversight. Under **Part XVI** (*Special Provisions Relating to Certain Classes*) and **Part XVII** (*Official Language*), four distinct constitutional authorities safeguard India's historically disadvantaged castes, indigenous tribes, socially and educationally backward classes, and linguistic minorities:
1. **National Commission for Scheduled Castes (NCSC)** — `Article 338`;
2. **National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST)** — `Article 338A`;
3. **National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC)** — `Article 338B`; and
4. **Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities)** — `Article 350B`.

### Historical and Constitutional Evolution (1950–2026)

Understanding how a **single Special Officer** in 1950 evolved over seven decades into **three distinct five-member constitutional commissions** plus a separate linguistic-minority office is indispensable for UPSC Prelims—where Amendment numbers (`7th`, `65th`, `89th`, `102nd`, `105th`) and statutory-to-constitutional transitions are perennial favourite questions.


#### Why the Bifurcation (89th Amendment) and Elevation (102nd and 105th Amendments) Mattered

- **Bifurcation of NCSC and NCST (`89th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003`, effective 19 February 2004):** Geographically and culturally, Scheduled Tribes differ fundamentally from Scheduled Castes. Whereas SCs face social untouchability and economic marginalisation dispersed across general villages and cities, STs largely inhabit distinct ecological, forest, and hilly tracts (`Fifth` and `Sixth Schedules`) confronting land alienation, displacement by mining/dams, forest-rights conflicts, and cultural erosion. Following the creation of a dedicated **Ministry of Tribal Affairs in 1999**, Parliament enacted the `89th Amendment` to carve out an independent **NCST (`Article 338A`)** while retaining **NCSC (`Article 338`)**.
- **Constitutionalisation of NCBC (`102nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2018`, assent 11 August 2018):** Under the repealed `NCBC Act, 1993`, the statutory NCBC was a narrow technical body competent only to examine inclusion/exclusion petitions for the Central OBC list—all OBC grievance redressal and safeguard monitoring remained anomalously vested in the **NCSC** under `Article 338(10)`! The `102nd Amendment` repealed the 1993 Act, inserted **`Article 338B`** granting NCBC full constitutional parity (including complaint investigation, civil court powers, and policy consultation), inserted **`Article 342A`** governing Presidential notification of SEBCs (mirroring `Arts. 341` & `342` for SCs/STs), added **`Article 366(26C)`** defining SEBCs, and removed OBC references from `Article 338(10)` so NCSC could focus exclusively on SCs and Anglo-Indians.
- **Restoration of Federal SEBC Powers (`105th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2021`, assent 18 August 2021):** In ***Dr. Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v. Chief Minister, Maharashtra* (5 May 2021 — Maratha Reservation case)**, a five-judge Constitution Bench not only struck down Maharashtra's Maratha quota for breaching the 50% *Indra Sawhney* ceiling without extraordinary grounds, but also ruled by a **3:2 majority** that `Article 342A` (inserted by the `102nd Amendment`) empowered **only the President** to notify SEBCs—stripping State Governments of their historic federal authority to identify and maintain State-specific OBC lists! With unanimous bipartisan support across Parliament, the **`105th Amendment Act, 2021`** amended `Articles 338B(9)`, `342A(1)–(3)`, and `366(26C)` to **explicitly restore every State and Union Territory's sovereign power to prepare and maintain its own State/UT SEBC list** for its own purposes, independent of the Central SEBC List.

### Composition, Tenure, Functions, and Powers of NCSC, NCST, and NCBC

Architecturally, `Articles 338`, `338A`, and `338B` are drafted as near-mirror twins—allowing aspirants to master all three commissions simultaneously while isolating their exact differences.

#### Composition, Appointment, and Service Conditions

- **Five-Member Structure:** Each of the three commissions—**NCSC**, **NCST**, and **NCBC**—consists of a **Chairperson**, a **Vice-Chairperson**, and **three other Members** (`Clause 3` of `Arts. 338, 338A, 338B`).
- **Appointment (`Clause 4`):** All five members are appointed by the **President by warrant under his hand and seal**.
- **Conditions of Service and Tenure (`Clause 3`):** Critically—a classic Prelims trap—the **Constitution does NOT fix the tenure, qualifications, or service conditions** of the Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson, or Members of NCSC, NCST, or NCBC! Instead, `Clause 3` delegates these entirely to **Rules made by the President**. Under the Presidential Rules currently in force:
  - Members hold office for a term of **3 years** from the date of assuming charge;
  - A person is eligible for appointment for **not more than two terms**;
  - In NCSC and NCST, the Chairperson holds the rank of a **Union Cabinet Minister**, the Vice-Chairperson that of a **Minister of State**, and the three Members that of a **Secretary to the Government of India** (for NCBC, the 2018 notification fixed the Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson, and Members in the rank and pay of **Secretary to GoI**—note also that under the NCSC/NCST/NCBC Rules, representation requirements specify community belonging and women representation norms).
- **Ex-Officio Seat on NHRC:** Under the `Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993` (as amended in **2019**), the Chairpersons of **NCSC, NCST, and NCBC** all serve as **ex-officio (deemed) Members of the National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)**!

#### Core Constitutional Functions (Clause 5 of Articles 338, 338A and 338B)

Every Commission performs six identical constitutional duties for its respective community (SCs, STs, or SEBCs):
1. **Investigation and Monitoring:** To investigate and monitor all matters relating to the **constitutional and other legal safeguards** provided for the community and evaluate their working;
2. **Inquiry into Specific Complaints:** To inquire into **specific complaints** with respect to the deprivation of rights and safeguards of the community;
3. **Participation in Socio-Economic Planning:** To participate and advise on the **planning process of socio-economic development** of the community and to evaluate the progress of their development under the Union and any State;
4. **Reporting to the President:** To present to the President, **annually and at such other times as the Commission may deem fit**, reports upon the working of those safeguards;
5. **Recommendations for Effective Implementation:** To make recommendations as to the **measures that should be taken by the Union or any State** for the effective implementation of those safeguards and other measures for protection, welfare, and socio-economic development; and
6. **Presidentially Assigned Functions:** To discharge such other functions in relation to the protection, welfare, development, and advancement of the community as the **President may, subject to any law made by Parliament, by rule specify**.

#### Distinct Community-Specific Mandates (Article 338(10) and NCST 2005 Notification)

- **NCSC's Continuing Anglo-Indian Mandate (`Article 338(10)`):** Under `Article 338(10)` (as modified by the `102nd Amendment`), references to Scheduled Castes in `Article 338` continue to include the **Anglo-Indian community**. Thus, even after the **`104th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019`** ended the two nominated Anglo-Indian seats in Lok Sabha (`Art. 331`) and State Assemblies (`Art. 333`), the **NCSC remains constitutionally obligated** to investigate, monitor, and report on safeguards for Anglo-Indians!
- **NCST's Special Tribal Mandate (Presidential Notification, 2005):** Exercising `Article 338A(5)(f)`, the President specified six vital tribal-specific duties for the NCST:
  1. Conferring **ownership rights in respect of Minor Forest Produce (MFP)** on STs living in forest areas;
  2. Safeguarding rights of tribal communities over **mineral resources, water resources**, etc., as per law;
  3. Improving livelihood strategies and sustainable tribal development;
  4. Enhancing efficacy of **relief and rehabilitation measures** for tribal groups displaced by development projects;
  5. Preventing **alienation of tribal people from land** and effectively rehabilitating those already alienated; and
  6. Promoting maximum tribal cooperation in protecting forests, encouraging social forestry, ensuring full implementation of the **PESA Act, 1996**, and reducing/eliminating the practice of **shifting cultivation (jhum)**.

#### Civil Court Powers, Reporting Accountability, and Mandatory Consultation

- **Powers of a Civil Court (`Clause 8`):** While investigating any matter under `Clause 5(a)` or inquiring into any complaint under `Clause 5(b)`, the NCSC, NCST, and NCBC each possess all the powers of a **Civil Court trying a suit**, specifically:
  - Summoning and enforcing the attendance of any person from any part of India and examining him on oath;
  - Requiring the discovery and production of any document;
  - Receiving evidence on affidavits;
  - Requisitioning any public record or copy thereof from any court or office;
  - Issuing commissions for the examination of witnesses and documents; and
  - Any other matter which the President may by rule determine.
- **Parliamentary & State Legislative Laying of Reports (`Clauses 6 & 7`):** The President causes every report to be laid before **each House of Parliament** along with a **memorandum explaining the action taken or proposed to be taken** on recommendations relating to the Union **and the reasons for the non-acceptance, if any**, of such recommendations. Where a report (or any part of it) relates to a **State Government**, a copy is forwarded to the **Governor of that State**, who lays it before the **State Legislature** accompanied by a similar Action Taken Memorandum.
- **Mandatory Policy Consultation (`Clause 9`) — Watch the `105th Amendment` Proviso!** Under `Clause 9` of `Arts. 338, 338A, and 338B`, the Union and every State Government **shall consult** the respective Commission on **all major policy matters** affecting SCs, STs, or SEBCs. **Crucial Prelims Exception:** The **`105th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2021`** added a proviso to **`Article 338B(9)`** stating expressly that **nothing in `Clause 9` applies for the purposes of `Article 342A(3)`**—meaning State Governments and UTs are **NOT required to consult the NCBC** when preparing and maintaining their own separate State/UT SEBC lists!

> [!prelims]
> **Two Classic Judicial & Constitutional Traps on NCSC, NCST, and NCBC:**
> 1. **Civil Court Powers ≠ Civil Court Adjudication!** In ***All India Indian Overseas Bank SC and ST Employees' Welfare Association v. Union of India* (1996)**, the Supreme Court categorically held that `Article 338(8)` grants the Commission procedural powers of a civil court *solely for investigating and procuring evidence*—it does **NOT** make the Commission a court or tribunal, and it **cannot pass binding orders, stay promotions, or issue permanent injunctions**!
> 2. **Tenure Is NOT in the Constitution:** Neither `Art. 338`, `Art. 338A`, nor `Art. 338B` specifies a 3-year tenure or qualifications—the **3-year tenure** is fixed entirely by **President's Rules**, not the Constitution!

### Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (Article 350B, Part XVII)

Unlike SCs, STs, and OBCs—who are protected under `Part XVI`—**linguistic minorities** derive their constitutional watchdog from **Part XVII (`Official Language`)**, Chapter IV (*Special Directives*), under **`Article 350B`**.

#### Constitutional Origin and Rationale (7th Amendment Act, 1956)

Originally, the **Constitution of 1950 contained NO provision** for a Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities. However, when the **States Reorganisation Commission (Fazl Ali Commission, 1953–55)** recommended reorganising Indian States predominantly along linguistic lines, it simultaneously warned that every newly formed linguistic State would inevitably contain substantial pockets of citizens speaking minority languages (for example, Marathi speakers in Belagavi/Karnataka, Telugu speakers in Tamil Nadu, Bengali speakers in Assam, or Urdu speakers across northern States). To prevent majoritarian cultural assimilation and protect national unity, the Fazl Ali Commission recommended creating a dedicated constitutional safeguard mechanism.

Acting on this recommendation, Parliament enacted the **`Seventh Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956`** (effective 1 November 1956), which inserted two twin provisions into `Part XVII`:
- **`Article 350A`:** Directs every State and local authority to endeavour to provide adequate facilities for **instruction in the mother-tongue at the primary stage of education** to children belonging to linguistic minority groups, and empowers the President to issue necessary directions to any State; and
- **`Article 350B`:** Mandates the appointment of a **Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities** by the **President of India**.

#### Constitutional Mandate, Structural Omissions, and Organisational Architecture

Under `Article 350B(2)`, it is the constitutional duty of the Special Officer to **investigate all matters relating to the safeguards provided for linguistic minorities under the Constitution** and to **report to the President** upon those matters at such intervals as the President may direct. The President must cause all such reports to be **laid before each House of Parliament** and **sent to the Governments of the States concerned**.

- **Constitutional Silence on Service Conditions:** Just like the Election Commission (in the constitutional text), the Constitution **does NOT specify the qualifications, tenure, salaries, allowances, service conditions, or procedure for removal** of the Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities!
- **Creation and Designation:** Pursuant to `Article 350B`, the office was created in **July 1957** and designated as the **Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities in India (CLM)**.
- **Nodal Ministry:** At the Central level, the CLM functions under the **Ministry of Minority Affairs** (earlier under the Ministry of Social Justice & Empowerment / Home Affairs) and submits its annual reports to the President through the **Union Minister of Minority Affairs**.
- **Headquarters and Regional Offices (Know the Dual Textbook Fact!):** Created initially at New Delhi in July 1957, the CLM headquarters was shifted for several decades to **Allahabad (Prayagraj), Uttar Pradesh** (which is why classic editions of M. Laxmikanth cite Allahabad/Prayagraj as the headquarters). Officially, per the Ministry of Minority Affairs, the **headquarters was shifted back to New Delhi with effect from 1 June 2015**, supported by **three Regional Offices** at **Belagavi (Karnataka)**, **Chennai (Tamil Nadu)**, and **Kolkata (West Bengal)** (with Prayagraj retained in zonal listings), each headed by an Assistant Commissioner. At headquarters, the Commissioner is assisted by a Deputy Commissioner and Assistant Commissioner, and maintains operational liaison with all States and UTs through designated **State Nodal Officers**.
- **Scope of Constitutional & Agreed Safeguards Monitored:**
  1. **Constitutional Safeguards:** `Article 29` (right to conserve distinct language, script, or culture), `Article 30` (right of linguistic minorities to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice), `Article 347` (Presidential direction for official recognition of a language spoken by a substantial proportion of a State's population), `Article 350` (right to submit grievance representations in any language used in the Union or State), and `Article 350A` (mother-tongue primary education).
  2. **Nationally Agreed Scheme of Safeguards (1956 Provincial Education Ministers' Conference & 1961 Chief Ministers' Conference):** Includes the **Three-Language Formula**, provision of mother-tongue primary teachers where at least **40 pupils in a school or 10 pupils in a class** request it, translation of important laws/rules/notices in districts/tehsils where a linguistic minority constitutes **15–20% of the population**, and declaration of an additional official language where a linguistic minority exceeds **60% of a district's population**.

> [!remember]
> **Who Is a "Linguistic Minority"? (Supreme Court & CLM Rule):** The Constitution **does NOT define** the term "linguistic minority." However, both the Supreme Court (***DAV College v. State of Punjab*, 1971** and ***T.M.A. Pai Foundation*, 2002**) and the CLM define a linguistic minority **strictly on a State-wise basis**—any group residing in a State or part thereof having a distinct spoken language (whether or not it has a separate script, and whether or not it is listed in the Eighth Schedule!) that is **NOT the principal official/majority language of that State**. Thus, Hindi speakers are a linguistic minority in Tamil Nadu or Kerala, while Tamil speakers are a linguistic minority in Uttar Pradesh or Maharashtra!

### Institutional Gaps and Mains Reform Agenda for Social & Linguistic Commissions

For UPSC GS Paper II (Polity & Social Justice), these four constitutional commissions illustrate a recurrent institutional paradox: **strong constitutional stature paired with weak enforcement teeth and frequent administrative neglect**.

> [!mains]
> **Four Structural Bottlenecks & Way Forward for NCSC, NCST, NCBC, and CLM:**
> 1. **Recommendatory Nature & Non-Binding Action Taken Reports:** Because the Commissions cannot issue enforceable directives (*IOB SC/ST Employees' Assn.*, 1996), departments routinely reject or delay recommendations without penalty; moreover, Action Taken Memoranda are frequently tabled years late in Parliament and State Legislatures.
> 2. **Prolonged Vacancies & Executive-Led Appointments:** Unlike NHRC or Lokpal (which have statutory multi-party selection panels), the Chairperson and Members of NCSC, NCST, and NCBC are appointed solely by the Union executive under Presidential Rules, with key Vice-Chairperson and Member posts—and the post of **Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities**—frequently left vacant for extended stretches (highlighted in 2025–2026 parliamentary and media reviews).
> 3. **Policy Consultation Friction:** As starkly illustrated when former NCST Chairperson Harsh Chouhan resigned in June 2023 citing non-consultation by the Environment Ministry on the **Forest Conservation Rules, 2022** (which impacted Gram Sabha consent under the Forest Rights Act, 2006), ministries sometimes bypass the mandatory `Clause 9` policy-consultation obligation.
> 4. **Acute Weakness of the CLM (`Art. 350B`):** Unlike NCSC, NCST, and NCBC, the Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities is a **single-officer body lacking even Civil Court powers**, fixed constitutional tenure, or independent field staff—leaving `Article 350A` mother-tongue schooling largely under-implemented across India.
> - **Reform Blueprint:** Establish transparent **bipartisan Selection Panels** and fixed statutory terms; enforce **mandatory timelines** for tabling Annual Reports and ATRs (with dedicated parliamentary committee scrutiny); make **`Clause 9` pre-legislative consultation** a mandatory Cabinet-note compliance check; upgrade the **CLM (`Art. 350B`)** into a multi-member statutory/constitutional body equipped with Civil Court investigative powers and active digital grievance tracking; and digitise real-time atrocity, scholarship, and land-alienation compliance dashboards across all States.

## 5. Law Officers: Attorney General, Solicitor General and Advocate General

Unlike the adversarial watchdogs examined in Sections 1 through 4—whose constitutional duty is to check, audit, or monitor the executive—the **Law Officers of the Union and the States** perform a subtly different constitutional role: serving as the **chief legal advisers and principal courtroom advocates** of the Union and State Governments while simultaneously owing an overriding ethical duty to uphold constitutional legality as officers of the court. Under **Part V (`Articles 76, 88, and 105(4)`)** and **Part VI (`Articles 165, 177, and 194(4)`)**, the Constitution establishes two mirror constitutional offices—the **Attorney General for India** at the Union level and the **Advocate General** in every State—assisted at the Centre by non-constitutional law officers, namely the **Solicitor General of India** and **Additional Solicitors General**.

### Attorney General for India (Articles 76, 88 and 105(4))

Recognised as the **highest law officer in the country**, the **Attorney General for India** stands at the apex of the Union's legal representation. India's first Attorney General, **M.C. Setalvad** (1950–1963), famously set the enduring benchmark of institutional independence for the office—maintaining that the Attorney General is not a partisan mouthpiece of the ruling party, but a constitutional jurist obligated to tender candid, legally sound advice to the Government of India and assist the Supreme Court dispassionately.

#### Appointment, Qualifications, Tenure, and Remuneration (Article 76(1) and (4))

- **Appointment (`Article 76(1)`):** The Attorney General is appointed by the **President of India** (acting on the aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers under `Article 74(1)`).
- **Constitutional Qualifications (`Article 76(1)`):** He must be a person **qualified to be appointed a Judge of the Supreme Court** (`Article 124(3)`). Consequently, he must be a **citizen of India** and satisfy **any one** of three alternative criteria:
  1. Have been a **Judge of a High Court** (or of two or more High Courts in succession) for **at least five years**; **OR**
  2. Have been an **Advocate of a High Court** (or of two or more High Courts in succession) for **at least ten years**; **OR**
  3. Be, in the opinion of the President, a **distinguished jurist**.
- **Term of Office and Removal (`Article 76(4)`):** In sharp contrast to the CEC, UPSC Chairman, or CAG, the **Constitution does NOT fix any term of office** for the Attorney General, nor does it prescribe **any procedure or grounds for his removal**. Under `Article 76(4)`, the Attorney General **holds office during the pleasure of the President**. He may be removed by the President at any time without parliamentary address or judicial inquiry, and he may quit office at any time by submitting his resignation to the President. By unbroken constitutional convention, the Attorney General resigns whenever the Union Council of Ministers resigns or is replaced, since he is appointed on its advice.
- **Remuneration (`Article 76(4)`):** The Constitution does **not** fix the remuneration of the Attorney General; he receives such retainer fee and daily appearance fees as the **President may determine** (governed by the **Law Officers (Conditions of Service) Rules, 1987**, as amended periodically).

#### Constitutional and Assigned Duties (Article 76(2))

Under `Article 76(2)`, the Attorney General performs three core constitutional functions:
1. **Legal Advice to the Union:** To give advice to the Government of India upon such legal matters as may from time to time be referred to him by the President;
2. **Assigned Legal Duties:** To perform such other duties of a legal character as may from time to time be assigned to him by the President; and
3. **Constitutional and Statutory Functions:** To discharge the functions conferred on him by or under the Constitution or any other law for the time being in force—most prominently, deciding whether to grant mandatory statutory consent under **`Section 15(1)(b)` of the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** before a private citizen can move a criminal contempt petition before the Supreme Court!

Pursuant to executive notification issued by the President under `Article 76(2)`, three specific litigation mandates are formally assigned to the Attorney General:
- To **appear on behalf of the Government of India** in all cases in the **Supreme Court** in which the Government of India is concerned;
- To **represent the Government of India** in any **Presidential reference** made to the Supreme Court under **`Article 143`** (Advisory Jurisdiction); and
- To **appear (when required by the Government of India) in any High Court** in any case in which the Government of India is concerned.

#### Extraordinary Courtroom and Parliamentary Rights (Articles 76(3), 88 and 105(4))

Even though the Attorney General is neither a Judge nor an elected Member of Parliament, the Constitution clothes him with three exceptional privileges necessary to bridge the executive, legislature, and judiciary:
1. **Nationwide Right of Audience (`Article 76(3)`):** In the performance of his official duties, the Attorney General has the **right of audience in ALL courts in the territory of India**—from the Supreme Court down to every High Court and subordinate tribunal.
2. **Right to Speak and Participate in Parliament (`Article 88`):** Alongside Union Ministers, the Attorney General has the constitutional right to **speak in, and otherwise take part in the proceedings of, either House of Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha), any Joint Sitting of the Houses (`Article 108`), and any Committee of Parliament of which he may be named a member**—**WITHOUT, however, being entitled to vote**!
3. **Full Parliamentary Privileges (`Article 105(4)`):** Whenever exercising his rights under `Article 88`, the Attorney General enjoys **all the privileges and immunities** available to a Member of Parliament (including complete freedom of speech inside the House and immunity from legal proceedings for anything said in Parliament or its committees).

#### Professional Limitations and Status Under Private Practice and RTI

A favourite UPSC Prelims trap concerns the employment status of the Attorney General: the Attorney General is **NOT a full-time counsel for the Government**, does **NOT fall in the category of a Government Servant**, and is **NOT debarred from private legal practice**! To prevent conflicts of interest while allowing continued bar practice, the **Law Officers (Conditions of Service) Rules, 1987** impose **five strict professional restrictions**:
1. He shall **not advise or hold a brief against the Government of India**;
2. He shall **not advise or hold a brief in cases in which he is called upon to advise or appear for the Government of India**;
3. He shall **not defend accused persons in criminal prosecutions without the permission of the Government of India**;
4. He shall **not accept appointment as a director in any company or corporation without the permission of the Government of India**; and
5. He shall **not advise any Ministry or Department of the Government of India, or any statutory organisation or PSU**, unless the proposal or reference is received through the **Ministry of Law and Justice, Department of Legal Affairs**.

> [!example]
> **India's Attorney General vs Britain's Attorney General — Constitutional Separation from the Cabinet:**
> In the **United Kingdom**, the Attorney General is a **political Minister** and member of the Government (frequently attending Cabinet meetings and sitting as a voting MP or Peer). By contrast, India deliberately rejected the British political model: India's Attorney General is **NOT a member of the Union Cabinet**, cannot vote in Parliament, and is kept institutionally distinct from the **Union Minister of Law and Justice** (who handles political and administrative portfolio duties inside the Cabinet). On legal transparency, note also that while a Single Judge of the Delhi High Court held in 2015 that the AG's office was a "public authority" under the RTI Act, 2005, a **Division Bench of the Delhi High Court in February 2017 reversed that ruling**, holding that the Attorney General is **NOT a "public authority"** under `Section 2(h)` of the RTI Act given his constitutional fiduciary lawyer-client relationship with the Union Government (though certain administrative records routed through the Law Ministry remain accessible).

### Solicitor General of India and Additional Solicitors General

Because the legal workload of the Government of India across the Supreme Court, 25 High Courts, and specialised national tribunals is vastly greater than any single advocate can handle, the Attorney General is assisted by the **Solicitor General of India (SGI)**—the **second-highest law officer** of the Union—and several **Additional Solicitors General (ASGs)** assigned to the Supreme Court and High Courts.

> [!prelims]
> **The Single Most Common Law-Officer Trap in UPSC Prelims:**
> - **`Article 76` mentions ONLY the Attorney General for India!** Neither the **Solicitor General of India** nor the **Additional Solicitors General** are mentioned anywhere in the Constitution!
> - Consequently, the Solicitor General and Additional Solicitors General are **NON-CONSTITUTIONAL (Executive / Rule-based) offices** created under the **Law Officers (Conditions of Service) Rules, 1987** and appointed by the **President** upon approval of the **Appointments Committee of the Cabinet (ACC)** (ordinarily for a three-year term or until further orders).
> - **Crucial Operational Consequence:** Unlike the Attorney General, the **Solicitor General and Additional SGs do NOT possess the constitutional right under `Article 88`** to speak or take part in the proceedings of Parliament or its Committees, nor do they enjoy `Article 105(4)` MP privileges!

### Advocate General of the State (Articles 165, 177 and 194(4))

Corresponding directly to the Attorney General at the Union level, **`Article 165`** in **Part VI** establishes the office of the **Advocate General for each State**—the **highest law officer in the State**.

- **Appointment and Qualifications (`Article 165(1)`):** Appointed by the **Governor of the State**, he must be a person **qualified to be appointed a Judge of a High Court** (`Article 217(2)`). Specifically, he must be a **citizen of India** and must have either:
  1. Held a **judicial office in the territory of India for at least ten years**; **OR**
  2. Been an **advocate of a High Court** (or of two or more such courts in succession) for **at least ten years**.
- **Term, Removal, and Remuneration (`Article 165(3)`):** Identical to the Attorney General at the Centre, the Advocate General has **NO fixed constitutional term** and **NO constitutional removal procedure**—he **holds office during the pleasure of the Governor**, receives such remuneration as the **Governor may determine**, may resign to the Governor at any time, and conventionally resigns when the State Council of Ministers resigns or changes.
- **Duties and Functions (`Article 165(2)`):** Advises the State Government on legal matters referred by the Governor, performs other legal duties assigned by the Governor, represents the State Government in the High Court and Supreme Court, and discharges statutory duties (including granting consent for criminal contempt proceedings before the State High Court under `Section 15` of the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971).
- **Rights and Privileges (`Articles 165, 177 & 194(4)`):** In performing his official duties, the Advocate General is entitled to appear before **any court of law within the State**. Under **`Article 177`**, he has the right to **speak and take part in the proceedings of both Houses of the State Legislature** (or the Legislative Assembly in unicameral States) and **any committee of the State Legislature of which he may be named a member**, **WITHOUT a right to vote**. Under **`Article 194(4)`**, he enjoys **all privileges and immunities** available to a Member of the State Legislature. Like the AG, he is not a full-time government servant and is permitted private practice subject to strict bars against appearing or advising against the State Government.

> [!prelims]
> **Subtle Qualification Trap — Attorney General vs Advocate General (`Art. 76` vs `Art. 165`):**
> Because the **Attorney General** must meet **Supreme Court Judge qualifications (`Art. 124(3)`)**, a **"distinguished jurist"** (such as an eminent legal academic who never practised at the bar or sat on the bench) IS eligible to become Attorney General! By contrast, because the **Advocate General** must meet **High Court Judge qualifications (`Art. 217(2)`)**, and `Article 217(2)` contains **NO "distinguished jurist" clause**, a legal academic with neither 10 years of judicial office nor 10 years of High Court advocacy **CANNOT** be appointed Advocate General of a State! Furthermore, note the **judicial experience differential**: **5 years as HC Judge** qualifies for AG (SC Judge standard), whereas **10 years of judicial office** is required for Advocate General (HC Judge standard).

### Ethical Independence of Law Officers and UPSC PYQ Anatomy (2011–2025)

Because the Attorney General (`Article 76`) and the Advocate General (`Article 165`) hold office at the pleasure of the President and Governor respectively, constitutional scholars have long examined how India's law officers balance **loyalty to the elected client** against **fidelity to the Constitution and the Court**. Unlike civil servants bound strictly by departmental hierarchy, an Attorney General or Advocate General stands in a dual constitutional posture: he advises the executive honestly when proposed legislation or executive action risks breaching `Part III` Fundamental Rights or the Basic Structure, yet once a valid policy decision is taken by the Government, he defends it vigorously in court—unless doing so violates conscience or settled constitutional ethics, in which case the honourable course is to recuse or resign.

#### Historical Precedents and Judicial Benchmarks for Law Officers

- **The Setalvad Benchmark of Candid Counsel:** India's first Attorney General, **M.C. Setalvad**, established two enduring conventions during his thirteen-year tenure: first, that the Attorney General must remain outside partisan Cabinet politics so his legal advice carries impartial moral weight; and second, that he must resist executive pressure to act as an advocate for unconstitutional shortcuts. Similarly, when the Law Commission of India (14th Report, 1958) reviewed the office, it rejected proposals to merge the Attorney General's post with that of the Union Law Minister (an idea briefly floated in 1962–63 and swiftly abandoned after intense parliamentary and bar opposition), preserving India's decisive break from the British ministerial model.
- **Gatekeeping Role in Criminal Contempt (`Section 15`, Contempt of Courts Act, 1971):** One of the most visible modern demonstrations of an Attorney General's institutional independence occurs under `Section 15(1)(b)` of the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971, which requires the **written consent of the Attorney General** (or the Solicitor General if AG is unavailable/recused; and the **Advocate General** at the High Court level) before a private citizen can move a criminal contempt petition against public commentary. Former AG **K.K. Venugopal** and incumbent AG **R. Venkataramani** have repeatedly exercised this quasi-judicial gatekeeping power independently—often **refusing consent** in cases involving robust criticism of judicial verdicts or public institutions so as to protect `Article 19(1)(a)` free speech and shield the Supreme Court from frivolous contempt litigation.
- **Transparency in Engaging State Law Officers (*Brijeshwar Singh Chahal*, 2016):** While `Article 165` vests the Governor with pleasure-based authority to appoint the Advocate General, State Governments routinely appoint hundreds of Additional Advocates General, Standing Counsel, and Government Pleaders. In ***State of Punjab v. Brijeshwar Singh Chahal* (2016)**, the Supreme Court held that public law offices cannot be distributed as political spoils: appointments of government counsel must satisfy `Article 14` standards of fairness, transparency, and merit-based consultation with the Advocate General and Chief Justice of the High Court.
- **Why "Pleasure" Tenure Remains Justified for Law Officers (Unlike ECI/CAG/UPSC):** Aspirants occasionally ask in Mains whether the AG and Advocate General should be given fixed tenures and impeachment protection like the CAG or CEC. Constitutional consensus answers **no**: the relationship between a Government and its chief legal counsel rests fundamentally on **mutual fiduciary trust**—an incoming Council of Ministers cannot be forced to take confidential litigation strategy from an advocate appointed by its political predecessor. Hence, the constitutional convention that the AG and Advocate General resign upon a change of government ensures both democratic coherence and professional candour.

### Unified GS-2 Mains Synthesis: Insulating India's Fourth-Branch Constitutional Watchdogs

When UPSC Mains poses a sweeping analytical question on constitutional institutions—whether probing the ECI's appointment controversy, the CAG's pending collegium petitions, State PSC paper leaks, or vacancies in social-justice commissions—top-scoring answers avoid treating each institution in isolation. Instead, they synthesise the **four cross-cutting structural vulnerabilities** affecting India's "fourth branch" of constitutional governance and propose a coherent **institutional insulation blueprint**:

1. **The Appointment Deficit (Executive Self-Selection):** Remarkably, the original text of the Constitution specified **no independent collegium** for appointing the ECI (`Art. 324`), CAG (`Art. 148`), UPSC/SPSC (`Art. 316`), or NCSC/NCST/NCBC (`Arts. 338–338B`). While judicial intervention in *Anoop Baranwal* (2023) forced legislative action for the ECI, the resulting **CEC Act, 2023** (now before a Constitution Bench following *Dr. Jaya Thakur*, September 2026) preserved a 2:1 executive majority, and the CAG's unilateral executive selection remains challenged in *Anupam Kulshreshtha* and *CPIL* (2024–2026). **A watchdog chosen solely by the executive it must police suffers from structural perception bias from day one.**
2. **Asymmetric Security of Tenure and Legal Immunity Imbalances:** While the CAG, CEC, and UPSC/SPSC Chairmen enjoy robust constitutional removal shields, **Election Commissioners** (`Art. 324(5)` second proviso), **NCSC/NCST/NCBC members** (removable under Presidential Rules), and the **CLM** (`Art. 350B`) lack equivalent constitutional tenure guarantees. Conversely, statutory over-corrections like `Section 16` of the CEC Act, 2023 (blanket lifelong civil/criminal immunity—under Supreme Court notice in October 2026) risk severing constitutional independence from basic rule-of-law accountability.
3. **Financial and Secretarial Dependence:** Only the **UPSC/SPSCs (`Art. 322`)** and **CAG (`Art. 148(6)`)** enjoy administrative expenditure **charged** on the Consolidated Fund. Leaving the **ECI**, **NCSC/NCST/NCBC**, and **CLM** dependent on voted ministerial budgets and cadre deputations compromises their operational autonomy.
4. **The "Revolving Door" and Delayed Parliamentary Follow-Up:** The absence of post-retirement cooling-off bars for the ECI (and the *Hargovind Pant* loophole permitting retired PSC/CAG heads to accept Governorships or political offices), combined with delayed tabling of CAG, UPSC, NCSC, NCST, NCBC, and CLM reports and toothless non-binding recommendations, blunts the ultimate sanction of parliamentary accountability.

    - **`Sec. 6` (Search Committee):** Headed by **Union Minister of Law & Justice** (+ 2 Secretary-rank members) preparing panel of 5 names *(TRAP: Cabinet Secretary was only in original Aug 2023 Bill — changed to Law Minister in final Act!)*.
    - **`Sec. 7` (Selection Committee):** **PM (Chair) + LoP Lok Sabha** (or largest opposition party leader) **+ Union Cabinet Minister nominated by PM** *(replaced CJI → 2:1 executive majority!)*; `Sec. 7(2)` saves appointment despite vacancy/defect; **`Sec. 8(2)`** allows picking **"any other person"** outside Search panel!
    - **`Sec. 9` & `Sec. 10`:** Term **6 yrs / 65 yrs** (statutory, non-renewable; combined EC+CEC capped at 6 yrs); **Salary equal to SC Judge** *(TRAP: Cabinet Secy downgrade in Aug 2023 Bill was WITHDRAWN in Dec 2023!)*.
    - **`Sec. 16`:** Blanket lifelong civil/criminal immunity for official acts (added Dec 2023; **SC CJI Surya Kant Bench issued notice on ADR challenge on 13 Oct 2026**). **`Sec. 18`:** Unanimous as far as possible, else **majority vote**.

- **Removal & 3 Constitutional Flaws (`Art. 324(5)`):** CEC removed like **SC Judge** (Parl. special majority); other **ECs & Regional Commissioners** removed by President **ONLY on CEC's recommendation** *(weaker than State ECs under `Art. 243K(2)` who are removed like HC Judges!)*. **3 Flaws:** (1) No constitutional qualification; (2) No constitutional tenure or **post-retirement Govt employment bar**; (3) Asymmetric EC removal & **non-charged (voted) Secretariat budget**.
- **Powers (3 Types):** **Administrative** (delimitation, rolls `Arts. 325–326`, poll schedules, EVMs/VVPATs, MCC [non-statutory], `Art. 324(6)` staff requisition, `Art. 356(5)` certification; ***Mohinder Singh Gill*, 1977** = plenary "reservoir of power"); **Advisory** (**BINDING** advice to President under **`Art. 103`** & Governor under **`Art. 192`** on ordinary MP/MLA disqualifications — **ZERO role in Tenth Schedule anti-defection!**); **Quasi-Judicial** (party registration `Sec. 29A` RPA 1951 [no general de-registration power — *INC v. Institute of Social Welfare*, 2002], split/symbol disputes under Para 15 Symbols Order 1968 via **Test of Majority** — *Sadiq Ali*, 1971).

## 2. Public Service Commissions: UPSC, SPSC and JSPSC

- **Constitutional Framework (`Part XIV, Arts. 315–323`):** **"Watch-dogs of the merit system"**. Lee Commission (1924) → 1st Central PSC established **1 Oct 1926** (**Centenary: 1 Oct 2026!**) → FPSC (1935) → UPSC (1950). **UPSC & SPSC = Constitutional (`Art. 315(1)`)**; **JSPSC (`Art. 315(2)`) = STATUTORY** (created by **Act of Parliament** on resolution of **2+ State Legislatures** — e.g., Punjab–Haryana 1966; distinguish from `Art. 315(4)` where UPSC serves a State on Governor's request + President's approval!).
- **Composition, Qualification & Tenure (`Arts. 316 & 318`):** Strength NOT fixed in Constitution (President fixes UPSC/JSPSC; Governor fixes SPSC). **ONLY constitutional qualification (`Art. 316(1)` proviso):** **Half (50%) must have 10+ years service under GoI or a State Govt!** Acting Chairman (`Art. 316(1A)` — `15th Amend 1963`). Tenure: **UPSC = 6 yrs / 65 yrs**; **SPSC & JSPSC = 6 yrs / 62 yrs** (`41st Amend 1976` raised 60→62). No reappointment to same office (`Art. 316(3)`).
- **Removal (`Art. 317`) — #1 Prelims Trap:** **ONLY the PRESIDENT can remove** Chair/Members of **ALL THREE — UPSC, SPSC AND JSPSC!** (**Governor can ONLY suspend an SPSC member pending SC inquiry (`Art. 317(2)`), NEVER remove!** Parliament has **NO** role!).
  - **Misbehaviour (`Art. 317(1) & (4)`):** Mandatory reference to **Supreme Court**; SC inquiry advice is **BINDING** on President! Interest in govt contract/profit = deemed misbehaviour.
  - **Direct Presidential Removal WITHOUT SC Inquiry (`Art. 317(3)`):** (a) Adjudged **insolvent**; (b) **Outside paid employment** during term; (c) **Infirmity of mind or body** in President's opinion.
- **Independence & `Article 319` Post-Retirement Matrix:** Conditions cannot be varied to disadvantage (`Art. 318`); ALL expenses **charged** on CFI (UPSC) / CFS (SPSC) (`Art. 322`).
  - **UPSC Chair:** ZERO further Govt office (`319(a)`).
  - **UPSC Member:** ONLY UPSC Chair or any SPSC Chair (`319(c)`).
  - **SPSC Chair:** ONLY UPSC Chair/Member or **other** SPSC Chair (`319(b)`).
  - **SPSC Member:** ONLY UPSC Chair/Member or **same/other** SPSC Chair (`319(d)`).
  - ***Hargovind Pant v. Raghukul Tilak* (1979, 5-Judge):** **Governor of a State** is NOT "employment under GoI/State", so former PSC members CAN be appointed Governor!
- **Functions & Limits (`Arts. 320, 321, 323`):** Conducts exams; aids 2+ States in joint recruitment (`320(2)`); consulted on recruitment, promotions, transfers, disciplinary matters, legal defence costs & injury pensions (`320(3)`). **EXCLUDED (`Art. 320(4)`):** Reservations for BCs (`Art. 16(4)`) & SC/ST claims (`Art. 335`)! Exemption Regulations (`Art. 320(5)`) laid before Legislature for **14 days**. Purely **advisory / directory** (***Manbodhan Lal Srivastava*, 1957**); Annual Reports (`Art. 323`) laid with **explanatory memorandum for non-acceptance** (JSPSC submits to **EACH concerned Governor**!; ACC alone can overrule UPSC at Centre; UPSC holds edge over statutory CVC).
- **2024–2026 Updates:** Current UPSC Chair: **Dr. Ajay Kumar** (assumed charge **15 May 2025**, succeeding Preeti Sudan). Aug 2024 **Lateral Entry** ad withdrawn over reservation debate; post-Puja Khedkar **Aadhaar authentication** (Aug 2024), revamped portal (May 2025), **AI facial authentication** across 5.5 lakh candidates & **1st-ever Provisional Answer Key** for CS (P) 2026 (**27 May 2026**); **Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024** (eff. 21 Jun 2024) + **Amendment Bill, 2026** (Jul 2026).

## 3. Comptroller and Auditor General of India

- **Constitutional Standing (`Part V, Arts. 148–151`):** Head of **IA&AD**; **"Guardian of the Public Purse"**; Ambedkar (30 May 1949): *"probably the most important officer in the Constitution of India."* **Single integrated office** auditing **BOTH Union & all States** (NO separate State CAGs!).
- **Appointment, Oath, Tenure & Independence (`Art. 148`):** Appointed by **President by warrant under hand & seal**; Oath in **`Third Schedule`** (identical to SC Judge); Tenure **6 yrs / 65 yrs** *(statutory — `CAG DPC Act, 1971`)**; Removed **ONLY like SC Judge** (Parl. special majority); Salary equal to SC Judge, non-variable (`148(3)`); **Absolute constitutional bar** on any further office under Union/State Govt (`Art. 148(4)`); IA&AD rules after CAG consultation (`148(5)`); ALL expenses **charged on CFI** (`148(6)`); **NO Minister represents CAG in Parliament**.
- **Duties & Audit Scope (`Arts. 149–151, 279` & `1971 Act`):** Audits Consolidated/Contingency/Public Accounts of Union, all States & UTs with Assemblies (Delhi, Puducherry, J&K); receipts (`Sec. 16`); substantially grant-financed bodies (`Sec. 14`: ≥ ₹25 lakh & ≥ 75% expenditure, OR ≥ ₹1 cr); Govt companies (`Sec. 19`); requested audits (`Sec. 20` — PRIs/ULBs). **Form of accounts (`Art. 150`):** President prescribes **on CAG's advice**. **Net Proceeds tax certificate (`Art. 279`):** CAG's certificate is **FINAL!**
  - **1976 Separation Trap:** Accounts separated from audit at **Centre ONLY** (shifted to **CGA** under Finance Ministry); CAG **STILL compiles & maintains State accounts**!
  - **3-Tier Public Corporation Audit:** Tier 1 (Total CAG — DVC); Tier 2 (Private + CAG supplementary — CWC); Tier 3 (**Totally OUTSIDE CAG** — **RBI, SBI, LIC, FCI**!). **Secret Service Expenditure:** CAG accepts administrative certificate (cannot demand vouchers).
  - **3 Audit Types:** Regulatory (legality), Propriety (wisdom/economy — rejected Paul Appleby's 1956 critique), Performance (**3Es**: Economy, Efficiency, Effectiveness). **3 Reports (`Art. 151`):** Appropriation Accounts, Finance Accounts, Public Undertakings → submitted to President/Governor → examined by **PAC** (CAG = **"guide, friend & philosopher"**) & CoPU.
- **Indian CAG vs UK CAG:** India = **purely *ex-post* Auditor** (NO prior control over Consolidated Fund issue; NOT an officer of Parliament); UK = **BOTH Comptroller AND Auditor** (prior credit control over Exchequer; Officer of House of Commons appointed with PAC Chair's agreement).
- **2024–2026 Updates:** Current **15th CAG: K. Sanjay Murthy** (sworn in **21 Nov 2024**, succeeding G.C. Murmu). Tagged SC PILs (*Anupam Kulshreshtha*, *CPIL* [notice 17 Mar 2025], *Lok Prahari*) challenge executive-only CAG selection seeking a neutral collegium; Union's **17 Sep 2026 affidavit** defended Cabinet Secy→FM→PM practice as "settled constitutional convention."

## 4. National Commissions for SCs, STs and Backward Classes, and Linguistic Minorities

- **Amendment Evolution Chronology:** Original `Art. 338` (1950 single Special Officer) → **`7th Amend 1956`** (`Art. 350A` & **`Art. 350B`** Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities on Fazl Ali SRC advice) → 1978/1987 non-statutory multi-member bodies → **`65th Amend 1990`** (eff. 1992: multi-member Constitutional NCSCST) → **`NCBC Act, 1993`** (statutory post-*Indra Sawhney* 1992) → **`89th Amend 2003`** (eff. 19 Feb 2004: bifurcated into **NCSC `Art. 338`** & **NCST `Art. 338A`**) → **`102nd Amend 2018`** (Constitutional **NCBC `Art. 338B`** + `Art. 342A` + `Art. 366(26C)`) → ***Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil* (May 2021)** (3:2 Maratha ruling stripped State SEBC list power) → **`105th Amend 2021`** (restored State/UT power to maintain separate State SEBC lists under `Art. 342A(3)`!).
- **Common Structure of NCSC, NCST & NCBC:** **5 members** (**Chair + Vice-Chair + 3 Members**) appointed by **President by warrant**; **3-year tenure** (max 2 terms) fixed by **President's Rules (NOT Constitution!)**; Chairpersons are **ex-officio Members of NHRC**.
- **Powers, Limits & Unique Mandates:** Investigate safeguards, inquire into complaints, advise on socio-economic planning, submit Annual/Special Reports to President → laid before Parliament & concerned State Legislatures with **Action Taken Memoranda**. Enjoy **Civil Court powers** *only during investigation* (`Clause 8` — **cannot issue binding injunctions/stays**, ***All India IOB SC/ST Employees' Assn.*, 1996**!). Mandatory consultation on major policy matters (`Clause 9` — **EXCEPT** under `105th Amend` proviso to `Art. 338B(9)`, States/UTs need **NOT** consult NCBC for their own State SEBC lists!).
  - **NCSC (`Art. 338(10)`):** Still safeguards **Anglo-Indians** (even after `104th Amend 2019` ended nominated Anglo-Indian seats!). **NCST (2005 Notification):** MFP ownership, PESA, Forest Rights, tribal land alienation, mineral/water rights, curbing jhum.
  - **Current Chairs (Nov 2026):** **NCSC — Kishor Makwana**; **NCST — Antar Singh Arya**; **NCBC — Sadhvi Niranjan Jyoti** (assumed charge **18 Mar 2026**, succeeding Hansraj Gangaram Ahir).
- **Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (`Part XVII, Art. 350B`):** Created July 1957 as **Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities (CLM)** under **Ministry of Minority Affairs**. Constitution specifies **NO qualifications, tenure, salary, or removal**; NO Civil Court powers. **HQ:** Historically **Allahabad (Prayagraj)** (traditional Laxmikanth), officially shifted to **New Delhi w.e.f. 1 June 2015**; Regional Offices at **Belagavi, Chennai & Kolkata** (plus Prayagraj zonal presence). Monitors `Arts. 29, 30, 347, 350, 350A` & Nationally Agreed Safeguards (40/10 pupils mother-tongue rule; 15–20% translation; 60% additional official language). "Linguistic minority" determined strictly **State-wise** (*DAV College*, 1971; *T.M.A. Pai*, 2002).

## 5. Law Officers: Attorney General, Solicitor General and Advocate General

- **Attorney General for India (`Part V, Art. 76`):** Highest law officer (1st: M.C. Setalvad; **16th/Current Nov 2026: R. Venkataramani**, reappointed w.e.f. 1 Oct 2025 till **30 Sep 2027**). Qualified to be **SC Judge** (Citizen + 5 yrs HC Judge OR 10 yrs HC Advocate OR **Distinguished Jurist**). Holds office during **President's pleasure** (NO fixed term/removal/remuneration in Constitution; resigns with CoM).
  - **Rights:** Right of audience in **ALL Indian courts (`Art. 76(3)`)**; speak & participate in **both Houses, Joint Sittings & Committees WITHOUT voting (`Art. 88`)**; full **MP privileges (`Art. 105(4)`)**. Grants criminal contempt consent (`Sec. 15` Contempt of Courts Act, 1971).
  - **Limitations:** **NOT a Government servant**; **NOT a Cabinet member** (unlike UK AG); allowed **private legal practice**; barred from advising/appearing against GoI, defending criminal accused or taking company directorship without GoI consent; **NOT a "public authority" under RTI** (Delhi HC DB, 2017).
- **Solicitor General & Additional SGs (Non-Constitutional Trap!):** **NOT mentioned in `Art. 76`!** Created under **Law Officers (Conditions of Service) Rules, 1987** to assist AG; **NO `Art. 88` right to participate in Parliament!** Current SGI (Nov 2026): **Tushar Mehta** (reappointed till **30 Jun 2029**).
- **Advocate General of the State (`Part VI, Art. 165`):** Highest State law officer appointed by **Governor**; qualified to be **HC Judge** (10 yrs Judicial Office OR 10 yrs HC Advocate — **NO "Distinguished Jurist" clause!**). **Governor's pleasure**; right of audience in all courts within the State; **`Art. 177`** participation in State Legislature & Committees **WITHOUT vote** + **`Art. 194(4)`** MLA privileges.
