## 1. Constitutional foundation of parliamentary financial control

Parliamentary democracy rests on the power of the purse—"no taxation without representation"—enshrined in **Articles 112 to 117** and **Articles 265 to 267**. Two constitutional rules guarantee that the executive can neither raise nor spend public money without legislative sanction:

- **Article 265:** *No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law*—never by mere executive notification.
- **Article 266(3):** *No money can be withdrawn from the Consolidated Fund of India except under an Appropriation Act* passed by Parliament.

Under **Article 112**, the President causes to be laid before both Houses every financial year (1 April to 31 March) the **"Annual Financial Statement" (AFS)** of estimated Union receipts and expenditure. Notably, **the word "Budget" never appears in the Constitution**. Under **Article 112(2)**, the statement distinguishes **revenue expenditure** from capital expenditure, and separates **charged** expenditure from **voted** expenditure.

### The 2017 budgetary reforms

Effective from **FY 2017–18**, three procedural reforms reshaped the Union Budget:

1. **Advancement to 1 February:** Moving presentation forward from the last working day of February allows enactment before **31 March**, eliminating routine Votes on Account in non-election years.
2. **Merger of the Railway Budget:** Acting on the **Bibek Debroy Committee (2015)**, the government merged the Railway Budget into the Union Budget, ending a 92-year separation introduced in **1924** on the **Acworth Committee (1921)** recommendation.
3. **Abolition of Plan vs Non-Plan distinction:** Replaced by a clean **Revenue vs Capital** classification after NITI Aayog succeeded the Planning Commission.

Alongside the AFS, the Finance Minister tables the **Finance Bill**, **Demands for Grants**, **Appropriation Bill**, **Expenditure Budget**, **Receipt Budget**, and statutory statements mandated by the **FRBM Act, 2003** (Macro-Economic Framework and Medium-Term Fiscal Policy-cum-Fiscal Policy Strategy statements; macroeconomic fiscal concepts sit in the Economics notes).

> **Prelims pointer:** Remember three classic traps: (1) the Constitution uses **"Annual Financial Statement" (Art 112)**, never "Budget"; (2) **Art 265** covers *taxes*, whereas statutory *fees* involving *quid pro quo* may be varied by delegated rules; (3) **Acworth Committee (1921/1924)** separated the Railway Budget, while **Bibek Debroy Committee (2015/2017)** merged it back.

## 2. Charged versus voted expenditure (Article 112(3))

To safeguard institutional independence and India's sovereign debt commitments, the Constitution splits Consolidated Fund outlays into two streams:

- **Charged expenditure (non-votable):** Insulated from parliamentary voting (**Article 113(1)**), **but CAN be discussed** in either House.
- **Voted expenditure:** Submitted as **Demands for Grants** exclusively to the **Lok Sabha**, which may assent, refuse, or reduce any demand (**Article 113(2)**).

Under **Article 112(3)** (and allied provisions), expenditure **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** comprises:
1. Emoluments, allowances and office expenditure of the **President**;
2. Salaries and allowances of the **Chairman and Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha** and the **Speaker and Deputy Speaker of Lok Sabha**;
3. **Union debt charges** (interest, sinking fund, redemption, and loan-raising costs);
4. Salaries, allowances and **pensions of Supreme Court judges**;
5. **Pensions of High Court judges** (and Federal Court judges);
6. Salary, allowances and pension of the **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)**;
7. Administrative expenses (including staff salaries and pensions) of the **Supreme Court (Art 146)**, **CAG's office (Art 148)**, and **UPSC (Art 322)**;
8. Sums required to satisfy any **judgment, decree or award** of any court or arbitral tribunal;
9. Any other expenditure declared charged by the Constitution or parliamentary law (e.g., statutory grants-in-aid to states under Art 275).

> **Remember:** Classic UPSC trap! **High Court judges' salaries and allowances** are charged on the **State Consolidated Fund** (**Art 202(3)(d)**), whereas their **PENSIONS** are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India** (**Art 112(3)(d)(iii)**), since HC judges are transferable across states. Also never assume Charged expenditure is undebatable—Parliament **can discuss it**, just not vote on it.

## 3. Six stages of budget enactment and other parliamentary grants

Budget passage balances executive initiative (only the government may propose taxes or spending) with parliamentary control across six tightly sequenced stages.

```text
  [1] PRESENTATION (1 Feb: FM speech in LS; laid in RS)
                          │
                          ▼
  [2] GENERAL DISCUSSION (Both Houses, 3-4 days; no vote)
                          │
                          ▼
  [3] SCRUTINY BY 24 DRSCs (3-4 week recess; reports)
                          │
                          ▼
  [4] VOTING ON DEMANDS FOR GRANTS (Lok Sabha ONLY!)
      → Cut Motions (Policy/Economy/Token) → Guillotine
                          │
                          ▼
  [5] APPROPRIATION BILL (Art 114: authorises CFI draw)
                          │
                          ▼
  [6] FINANCE BILL (Art 117: taxes; enacted ≤ 75 days)
```

1. **Presentation:** The Finance Minister delivers the Budget Speech in the Lok Sabha (11:00 AM, 1 February) and lays the papers before the Rajya Sabha immediately after. **No discussion occurs on presentation day.**
2. **General Discussion:** Lasting 3–4 days in **both Houses**, MPs debate broad fiscal priorities without cut motions or voting; the Finance Minister replies at the close.
3. **Scrutiny by 24 DRSCs:** Parliament takes a **3–4 week recess** during which the 24 Departmentally Related Standing Committees examine ministry-wise Demands for Grants in detail and table recommendatory reports in both Houses.
4. **Voting on Demands for Grants (Article 113):** **Exclusive to Lok Sabha** (Rajya Sabha may discuss ministry workings, never vote). Under **Article 113(3)**, every demand requires the **President's recommendation**. Lok Sabha can assent, refuse, or reduce—never increase—a demand. Members may move three **Cut Motions** (passage of any cut motion signals loss of confidence and forces resignation):
   - **Policy Cut:** Reduce demand to **₹1**—disapproves underlying policy and invites an alternative policy.
   - **Economy Cut:** Reduce demand by a **specified amount**—flags wasteful spending to enforce economy.
   - **Token Cut:** Reduce demand by **₹100**—ventilates a **specific grievance** within Union responsibility.
   On the final allotted day, the Speaker applies the **Guillotine**, putting all remaining undiscussed Demands for Grants to vote together without debate.
5. **Appropriation Bill (Article 114):** Even voted grants cannot leave the CFI without an **Appropriation Act** (**Art 266(3)**), bundling both **voted grants** and **charged sums**. Under **Article 114(2)**, **no amendment** may alter the amount or destination of any grant. As a Money Bill, Rajya Sabha must return it within **14 days**.
6. **Finance Bill (Article 117):** Enacts the year's tax proposals. Under the **Provisional Collection of Taxes Act, 1931**, it **must be enacted (with Presidential assent) within 75 days of introduction**. Treated as a Money Bill (when confined to Art 110 matters), amendments may **reduce or abolish a tax**, **NOT impose or increase one** without Presidential recommendation (**Art 117(1)**).

> **In the news:** **Routine guillotining persists.** **PRS Legislative Research** notes that over the decade 2015–16 to 2024–25, **over two-thirds** of voted Union expenditure was guillotined yearly—hitting **100%** in 2018–19 and 2023–24, and **89%** in 2024–25. In **Budget Session 2026** (Jan–Apr 2026), **77% of the Budget was guillotined**: of five ministries listed in Lok Sabha (Railways, Agriculture, Home Affairs, External Affairs, Power), only **Agriculture and Railways** were actually discussed.

### Other parliamentary grants (Articles 115–116)

| Grant | Article | Meaning & Prelims Key |
|---|---|---|
| **Vote on Account** | **Art 116(1)(a)** | Advance grant pending full Budget passage (usually **1/6th** / ~2 months); now used mainly in **election years** alongside an **Interim Budget** |
| **Supplementary Grant** | **Art 115(1)(a)** | Extra funds when the authorised grant for an existing service proves **insufficient** in the current year |
| **Additional Grant** | **Art 115(1)(a)** | Funds needed during the current year for a **new service** not contemplated in that year's Budget |
| **Excess Grant** | **Art 115(1)(b)** | Regularises money **already spent beyond** the voted limit; voted *after* year-end—**must be vetted by the PAC before Lok Sabha votes** |
| **Vote of Credit** | **Art 116(1)(b)** | **"Blank cheque"** for an unexpected demand whose indefinite character (e.g., war/disaster) precludes detailed specification |
| **Exceptional Grant** | **Art 116(1)(c)** | Granted for a **special purpose** forming no part of the current service of any financial year |
| **Token Grant** | Convention / Art 115 | Nominal **₹1** grant when a **new service** is financed purely via **reappropriation** (shifting savings), securing Lok Sabha approval |

> **Prelims pointer:** Keep the rupee traps straight: **Policy Cut = ₹1**, **Token Cut = ₹100**, and **Token Grant = ₹1** (reappropriation for a *new service*). Also note: **Vote on Account** authorises *expenditure only*, whereas an **Interim Budget** presents both receipts and expenditure (deferring major tax/policy overhauls to the incoming government by convention).

## 4. The three public funds of India (Articles 266–267)

Union moneys sit in three constitutional funds—each mirrored at the State level under **Articles 266 and 267(2)**:

| Feature | Consolidated Fund of India (CFI) | Public Account of India | Contingency Fund of India |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Article & basis** | **Article 266(1)** | **Article 266(2)** | **Article 267(1)** (created by **Contingency Fund of India Act, 1950**) |
| **Receipts** | All Union tax/non-tax revenues, borrowings (treasury bills, loans), loan recoveries | All *other* public money: provident funds, small savings (PPF, Sukanya Samriddhi), judicial/departmental deposits, remittances | Fixed **imprest** drawn from CFI—raised from ₹500 crore (2005) to **₹30,000 crore** via **Sec 127, Finance Act, 2021** |
| **Nature** | Government's own funds | **Banker/trustee** funds repayable to depositors | Emergency advance pool for **unforeseen urgent expenditure** |
| **Parliamentary control** | **Prior Appropriation Act mandatory** (Art 266(3)) | **NO parliamentary appropriation**—operated by **executive action** | **Post facto** parliamentary authorisation required, followed by CFI replenishment |
| **Custodian** | Government of India | Union Executive | Placed at **President's disposal**; held by **Secretary, Dept. of Economic Affairs** (40% directly at disposal of **Expenditure Secretary** under 2021 amended Rules) |

> **Prelims pointer:** **Article 267(1)** only *empowers Parliament* to establish the Contingency Fund by law (done via the **1950 Act**). Only the **CFI** requires prior parliamentary appropriation; the **Public Account** needs none.

## 5. Parliamentary committees: architecture, financial panels and DRSCs

A full House of hundreds of MPs operating under television cameras and strict party whips lacks the time and technical calm to dissect complex Bills or audit departmental spending. That work belongs to **Parliamentary Committees**—"mini-parliaments" mentioned indirectly in **Article 105** (privileges) and **Article 118** (House rules), and detailed in Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha Rules of Procedure.

> "Congress in session is Congress on public exhibition, whilst Congress in its committee-rooms is Congress at work." — Woodrow Wilson, *Congressional Government*, 1885

A body qualifies as a **Parliamentary Committee** only if it meets **four tests**: (1) appointed/elected by the House or nominated by the Speaker/Chairman; (2) works under the Speaker/Chairman's direction; (3) presents its report to the House or presiding officer; and (4) draws its secretariat from the **Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha Secretariat**.

> **Prelims pointer:** **Consultative Committees are NOT Parliamentary Committees!** Constituted by the **Ministry of Parliamentary Affairs**, attached to specific ministries, **chaired by the Union Minister**, and comprising **10 to 30 MPs** voluntarily, they serve as informal policy-dialogue forums and dissolve with each Lok Sabha—failing the four-fold test.

                PARLIAMENTARY COMMITTEES

### The three Financial Committees

All three elected financial watchdogs share a **1-year term** (1 May–30 April), election via **proportional representation by single transferable vote (PR-STV)**, and **strict exclusion of Ministers** (a member becoming a minister ceases to belong).

| Dimension | Public Accounts Committee (PAC) | Estimates Committee | Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Origin** | **1921** (GoI Act, 1919)—oldest panel | **1921** (Standing Financial Committee); reconstituted **1950** (**John Mathai**) | **1964** (**Krishna Menon Committee**) |
| **Composition** | **22 (15 LS + 7 RS)** | **30—ALL Lok Sabha** (**NO RS members!**; largest panel; raised from 25 in 1956) | **22 (15 LS + 7 RS)** (raised from 15 in 1974) |
| **Chairperson** | LS Speaker appoints; **Opposition MP since 1967** (**K.C. Venugopal**, INC, 2024–27) | LS Speaker appoints; **ruling-party MP** (**Dr. Sanjay Jaiswal**, BJP, 2024–27) | LS Speaker appoints from **LS only** (**Baijayant Panda**, BJP, 2024–27) |
| **Mandate** | Examines **CAG audit reports** (appropriation/finance accounts), checks waste/legality, vets **Excess Grants** | **"Continuous economy committee"**; suggests efficiencies, administrative reform & **alternative policies** | Audits **PSU** reports & CAG PSU audits against **sound business & commercial practices** |
| **Limitation** | ***Post-mortem*** & advisory; cannot question wide government policy | Examines estimates *after* voting; no CAG technical assistance | Excludes broad policy & **day-to-day PSU administration** |

The **CAG** acts as the PAC's **"friend, philosopher and guide"**. Note the sharp UPSC contrast: while the PAC conducts a *post-mortem* audit and cannot question broad policy, the **Estimates Committee CAN suggest alternative policies** to achieve economy.

### The 24 Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs)

Created in **April 1993** (**17 panels**) and expanded in **July 2004** to **24 DRSCs**, these committees cover every Union ministry:

- **Control split:** **16 DRSCs** function under the **Lok Sabha Speaker** (e.g., Finance, Defence, External Affairs, Railways, Agriculture), while **8 DRSCs** work under the **Rajya Sabha Chairman** (**Commerce; Home Affairs; Education, Women, Children, Youth and Sports; Industry; Science & Technology, Environment, Forests and Climate Change; Transport, Tourism and Culture; Health and Family Welfare; and Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice**).
- **Composition:** **31 members each (21 LS + 10 RS)**, **nominated** by the Speaker and Chairman (**NOT elected**—key difference from Financial Committees!). Term is **1 year**; **no ministers** may be members.
- **Four functions:** Examine ministry **Demands for Grants** during the Budget recess (no cut motions), scrutinise **Bills** referred by presiding officers, review ministries' **Annual Reports**, and assess **long-term national policy documents**. They avoid day-to-day administration; reports are persuasive rather than binding, tracked through ministerial **Action Taken Reports (ATRs)**.

| Standing Committee | Strength (LS / RS) | Key Role & Prelims Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| **Business Advisory (BAC)** | 15 LS / 11 RS | Allocates House time; **Speaker (LS) / Chairman (RS) chairs** |
| **Rules Committee** | 15 LS / 16 RS | Reviews House procedure rules; **Speaker / Chairman chairs** |
| **Private Members' Bills & Resolutions** | **15 LS ONLY** | Classifies/allots time to Private Members' Bills; **Deputy Speaker chairs** (BAC handles this in RS) |
| **Absence of Members** | **15 LS ONLY** | Examines MP leave applications (re Art 101(4) 60-day rule); no RS counterpart |
| **Privileges / Petitions / Ethics** | 15 LS / 10 RS each | Semi-judicial privilege probes; public petitions; MP Code of Conduct (Ethics: RS **1997**, LS **2000**) |
| **Government Assurances** | 15 LS / 10 RS | Tracks floor promises by ministers (**Indian innovation, 1953**) |
| **Subordinate Legislation** | 15 LS / 15 RS | Checks delegated rules/regulations against parent Acts |
| **Welfare of SC & ST / OBCs / Women** | **30 (20 LS + 10 RS)** each | Joint panels (SC/ST chair 2026–27: **Faggan Singh Kulaste**; Women created **1997**) |
| **Joint Committee on Offices of Profit** | **15 (10 LS + 5 RS)** | Advises which offices trigger Art 102(1)(a) disqualification |

### Ad hoc Committees: Select Committees and JPCs

**Ad hoc committees** dissolve once their mandate finishes and comprise **Inquiry Committees** (e.g., **Joint Parliamentary Committees [JPCs]** probing controversies—starting with India's first JPC on **Bofors, 1987** [B. Shankaranand], followed by **Harshad Mehta 1992**, **Ketan Parekh 2001**, **Soft-Drink Pesticides 2003**, **2G Spectrum 2011**, and **VVIP Chopper 2013**) and **Advisory Committees** (**Select Committee** of one House or **Joint Committee / JPC** of both Houses, typically in a 2:1 LS:RS ratio, scrutinising Bills clause by clause—such as the Income-Tax Bill 2025 Select Committee). JPCs can summon officials and experts (summoning a Minister needs the Speaker's consent); reports are non-binding.

> **In the news:** **Revival of legislative JPCs (2024–2026).** Three high-profile Bills went to JPCs in the 18th Lok Sabha: (1) **Waqf (Amendment) Bill, 2024** (31-member JPC chaired by **Jagdambika Pal**; report submitted **30 January 2025** amid Opposition dissent notes; enacted as the **Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025** on 5 April 2025); (2) **Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill, 2024 ("One Nation, One Election")** & companion UT Bill (JPC chaired by **P.P. Chaudhary**, expanded to 41 members; extended on **29 July 2026** until the last week of **Winter Session 2026**); and (3) **Constitution (130th Amendment) Bill, 2025** (30-day custody removal of PM/CM/Ministers; 31-member JPC chaired by **Aparajita Sarangi**, extended till **Winter Session 2026**). The **FCRA (Amendment) Bill, 2026** was also referred to a Joint Committee in Monsoon Session 2026.

### Parliamentary Forums

Started on **12 August 2005** (Speaker Somnath Chatterjee), **eight Parliamentary Forums** exist today: Water Conservation and Management, Youth, Children, Population and Public Health, Global Warming and Climate Change, Disaster Management, Artisans and Craftspeople, and Millennium Development Goals. Each has up to **31 members (21 LS + 10 RS)** excluding ex-officio heads, builds MP awareness through expert briefings, and **does NOT submit reports**.

> **Prelims pointer:** The **Lok Sabha Speaker** is ex-officio **President of seven Parliamentary Forums**—the **only exception** is **Population and Public Health**, where the **Rajya Sabha Chairman** is President and the LS Speaker is Co-President!

## 7. Mains analysis: are India's committees fulfilling their promise?

Operating behind closed doors, **free from televised grandstanding and party whips**, committees enable MPs across party lines to summon regulators, economists and civil society—substantially strengthening landmark laws like **GST**, the **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016)**, and the **Consumer Protection Act (2019)**. Yet **PRS Legislative Research** data highlights six structural cracks:

1. **Declining Bill referrals:** Discretionary referral caused committee scrutiny of Bills to drop from **60% (14th LS)** and **71% (15th LS)** to **25% (16th LS)** and just **16% (17th LS)**, ticking up only modestly to **21% in the 18th LS up to Monsoon Session 2026** (where nine of eleven Bills passed Lok Sabha with no MP other than the minister speaking).
2. **Blanket guillotining:** Passing **89% (2024–25)** and **77% (2026–27)** of the Union Budget without floor debate disconnects DRSC Demand scrutiny from Lok Sabha votes.
3. **One-year tenure:** Annual reconstitution prevents MPs from building lasting domain specialisation, unlike multi-year UK/US committees.
4. **Weak research staff & low attendance:** Attendance often hovers around 40–50%, and committees lack dedicated fiscal/legal researchers comparable to the **US Congressional Budget Office (CBO)**.
5. **Non-binding reports & quiet ATRs:** Ministries need not accept recommendations, and Action Taken Reports rarely receive floor debate.
6. **Partisan spillovers:** Walkouts and sharp dissent notes in recent JPCs risk importing floor polarisation into committee rooms.

### Balanced way forward

Revitalising parliamentary financial control and committee oversight requires five reforms: (1) **mandatory DRSC referral** for all major Bills unless written urgency is recorded (**NCRWC, 2002**); (2) extending DRSC tenure to **2–2.5 years** with public attendance records; (3) creating an independent **Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO)** for neutral fiscal forecasts and Bill costings; (4) reserving guaranteed floor time each session to debate top-spending ministries' Demands and key PAC/DRSC **Action Taken Reports**; and (5) enforcing mandatory **pre-legislative public consultation** so citizen input feeds directly into committee work.

> **Mains angle:** Ground GS-2 answers in verified **PRS metrics** (Bill referrals dropping from 71% in 15th LS to 16% in 17th LS and 21% in 18th LS till Monsoon 2026; 77% of Budget 2026 guillotined), cite Woodrow Wilson on "mini-parliaments", and close with concrete institutional fixes—mandatory referral, 2-year tenures, a PBO, and ATR floor debates.

- **Financial control foundation:** **Art 265** (no tax without law); **Art 266(3)** (no CFI withdrawal without Appropriation Act); **Art 112** (**Annual Financial Statement**—word **"Budget"** never appears in the Constitution).
- **2017 reforms:** Presentation advanced to **1 Feb**; **Railway Budget merged** (**Bibek Debroy, 2015**, ending **1924 Acworth** split); **Plan vs Non-Plan** replaced by Revenue vs Capital.
- **Charged vs Voted (Arts 112(3), 113):** **Charged** is **non-votable** but **CAN be discussed**; **Voted** is voted **only by Lok Sabha** on President's recommendation (Art 113(3)).
- **High Court judges' trap:** HC judges' **salaries** sit on **State CFI (Art 202(3)(d))**, while their **PENSIONS** sit on **Union CFI (Art 112(3)(d)(iii))**. Also on Union CFI: President, LS/RS presiding officers, SC judges, CAG, UPSC/SC/CAG office costs, Union debt, court decrees.
- **Six Budget stages:** (1) Presentation (no same-day debate) → (2) General Discussion (both Houses, no vote) → (3) **24 DRSCs** (3–4 wk recess) → (4) Demands voting (**LS only**; Cut Motions → Guillotine) → (5) **Appropriation Bill (Art 114)** → (6) **Finance Bill (Art 117)**.
- **Cut Motions (LS only; passage = resignation):** **Policy Cut** (to **₹1**, policy disapproval); **Economy Cut** (specific amount); **Token Cut** (by **₹100**, specific grievance).
- **Guillotine (PRS):** Undiscussed Demands voted together on final day; **89%** guillotined in 2024–25 and **77% in Budget Session 2026** (only Agriculture & Railways of 5 listed ministries debated).
- **Appropriation vs Finance Bill:** Appropriation Bill (Art 114) bars amendments altering amount/destination; Finance Bill (Art 117) must be enacted in **75 days** (**Provisional Collection of Taxes Act, 1931**)—amendments may reduce/abolish, not raise, taxes without Presidential recommendation.

- **Parliamentary vs Consultative Committees:** Parliamentary committees pass 4 tests (House/Chair-constituted, Chair-directed, report to House/Chair, LS/RS Secretariat). **Consultative Committees** (Min. of Parliamentary Affairs, Minister chairs, 10–30 MPs) are **NOT** parliamentary committees.

- **Estimates Committee:** Reconstituted **1950** (**John Mathai**); **30—ALL Lok Sabha (NO RS!; largest panel)**; PR-STV, 1-yr term, no ministers, ruling-party chair (**Dr. Sanjay Jaiswal**, 2024–27); "continuous economy committee" that **can suggest alternative policies**.

- **Ad hoc Committees & 2024–26 JPCs:** **Select Committee** (1 House) vs **JPC** (both Houses; 1st: **Bofors 1987**).
