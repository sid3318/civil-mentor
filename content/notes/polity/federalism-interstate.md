## 1. Nature of Indian Federalism

Political scientists classify modern nation-states into **unitary** and **federal** models on the basis of how sovereign authority is distributed between the national government and regional governments. In a unitary state—such as the United Kingdom, France, Japan, China, Italy, or Norway—all supreme constitutional power is concentrated in a single national government; regional or local bodies may exist for administrative convenience, yet they derive their authority purely from statutes of the national legislature and can be curtailed or abolished at will. In a federal state—such as the United States, Switzerland, Australia, Canada, Russia, Brazil, or India—the Constitution itself divides sovereign powers between a national government (variously termed the Union, Federal, or Central Government) and regional governments (States, Provinces, Cantons, or Republics), so that each tier operates independently within its constitutionally demarcated sphere.

The term **federation** is derived from the Latin word ***foedus***, meaning treaty, compact, or agreement. Historically, a federation was conceived as a new political union forged by a voluntary compact between previously independent states wishing to pool defence, diplomacy, and common commerce while retaining domestic self-rule. When the Constituent Assembly met in New Delhi between December 1946 and November 1949, however, India faced a vastly different historical predicament: how to bind a continental polity of immense linguistic, religious, tribal, and economic diversity into a single democratic republic immediately after the trauma of Partition and amid the integration of over 550 princely states. The framers therefore engineered a remarkably resilient constitutional hybrid—a **federal system with a strong unitary bias**, capable of functioning as a decentralised federal democracy in ordinary times and transforming seamlessly into a cohesive unitary state during existential crises.

### Federal vs Unitary Models and Coming-Together vs Holding-Together Federations

Understanding where India sits on the global spectrum of constitutional design requires looking beyond the simplistic binary of "federal versus unitary". Political scientist **Alfred Stepan** drew an influential distinction between two fundamentally different pathways through which federations come into existence: **"coming-together" federations** and **"holding-together" federations**.

In a **coming-together federation**, previously independent or sovereign political units voluntarily unite to enhance collective security and economic scale—the classic archetype being the **United States of America (1787)**, the world's oldest modern federation, alongside **Switzerland** and **Australia**. Because independent units surrender only enumerated powers to the new national centre, coming-together federations typically display **symmetric representation** (every American state receives exactly two Senate seats regardless of population), retain **residuary powers** with the constituent states, guard state borders as constitutionally inviolable, and restrict the central government from intervening in provincial governance.

By contrast, a **holding-together federation** emerges when a large, culturally diverse, previously unitary polity or colonial empire devolves constitutional powers to constituent regions precisely in order to hold the country together democratically—exemplified by **India**, **Canada**, **Belgium**, and **Spain**. Because preventing fragmentation and managing deep socio-economic disparities are overriding priorities, holding-together federations characteristically feature a **strong Centre**, **Union residuary powers**, **asymmetric accommodations** for historically distinct regions, and emergency safeguards to preserve territorial integrity.

#### Why India Adopted the Canadian Model Over the American Model

While the framers drew structural inspiration from the **Government of India Act, 1935** (particularly its three-fold legislative distribution and emergency framework), they consciously patterned India's federal model on **Canada** rather than the United States. Four decisive features reflect this Canadian lineage:

1. **Mode of Formation (Disintegration / Devolution rather than Integration):** Like Canada under the British North America Act of 1867, the Indian federation was created by converting a centralised British Indian state into autonomous provinces (alongside integrating princely states), rather than by sovereign states contracting into a union.
2. **Preference for the Term "Union":** Both the Canadian Constitution and `Article 1` of the Indian Constitution prefer the term **"Union"** over "Federation" to underscore permanent national indissolubility.
3. **Strong Centralising Tendency and Union Residuary Powers:** Just as Canada vests residuary legislative powers in the Dominion Parliament rather than the provinces, `Article 248` read with `Entry 97` of the Union List vests all unenumerated powers—including residuary taxation—exclusively in Parliament.
4. **Centrally Appointed Governors:** Following the Canadian practice of centrally appointed Lieutenant-Governors of Provinces (rather than popularly elected State Governors as in the United States), `Article 155` empowers the President to appoint State Governors, creating a constitutional link between New Delhi and every state capital.

# Unitary Polity vs Coming-Together Federation vs Holding-Together Union
Unitary Polity (UK / France) | Coming-Together Federation (USA) | Holding-Together Union (India)

### Why the Word Federation Is Absent: Article 1 and Dr Ambedkar's Defence

A foundational textual fact of Indian constitutional law is that the word **"federation"** appears **nowhere** in the Constitution of India. Although the Union Constitution Committee had initially drafted the phrase *"Federation of States"*, the Drafting Committee deliberately replaced it in `Article 1(1)` with the solemn declaration: ***"India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States."***

Introducing the Draft Constitution in the Constituent Assembly on **4 November 1948**, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar explained why the Drafting Committee insisted on the word **"Union"** even while designing an unmistakably federal polity. He advanced **two clear constitutional reasons**:

1. **The Indian Federation is NOT the result of an agreement among States:** Unlike the thirteen American colonies that met at Philadelphia in 1787 as sovereign entities to sign a federal compact, the provinces of British India were administrative units created by colonial devolution, and the princely states acceded and merged into a single republican sovereignty.
2. **No State has the right to secede from the Union:** Because the Indian Union was not forged by a revocable contract between sovereign states, no constituent state possesses any constitutional or moral right to break away. The federation is called a **Union** precisely because it is **indestructible**.

> "The Drafting Committee wanted to make it clear that though India was to be a Federation, the Federation was not the result of an agreement by the States to join in a Federation and that the Federation not being the result of an agreement no State has the right to secede from it. The Federation is a Union because it is indestructible. Though the country and the people may be divided into different States for convenience of administration the country is one integral whole, its people a single people living under a single imperium derived from a single source." — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, Vol. VII, 4 November 1948

Crucially, because `Article 3` authorises Parliament by ordinary legislation (simple majority) to form new states, unite territories, diminish areas, or alter state boundaries and names without requiring the consent of the affected State Legislatures, constitutional scholars famously describe India as an **"indestructible Union of destructible States"**. This stands in direct contrast to Chief Justice Salmon P. Chase's celebrated dictum in ***Texas v. White* (1869)** describing the American polity as *"an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States."*

#### Ambedkar's Defence of Constitutional Elasticity

During the Constituent Assembly Debates, critics—including K. Santhanam, P.T. Chacko, and Mahavir Tyagi—charged that the Draft Constitution was excessively centralised and reduced the states to glorified municipalities. Dr. Ambedkar mounted a spirited, nuanced rebuttal on **4 November 1948** and again in his closing address on **25 November 1949**. He observed that classical federations like the United States and Australia suffered from two structural defects: extreme **rigidity** and excessive **legalism**, forcing courts to stretch constitutional clauses (such as the US "implied powers" and "commerce" doctrines) to cope with modern economic and wartime crises.

India's Constitution avoided that tight mould by building **structural elasticity** directly into the text: in normal times it operates as a genuine federal system with coordinate spheres of authority, while during national emergencies (`Part XVIII`) it converts smoothly into a unitary system without requiring a single formal constitutional amendment. Furthermore, Ambedkar rejected the claim that states were mere agents of the Centre, emphasising that the chief mark of federalism—a **Dual Polity** whose legislative and executive authority is partitioned directly by the Constitution rather than delegated by the Centre—remained firmly intact.

> "All federal systems including the American are placed in a tight mould of federalism. No matter what the circumstances, it cannot change its form and shape. It can never be unitary. On the other hand the Draft Constitution can be both unitary as well as federal according to the requirements of time and circumstances. In normal times, it is framed to work as a federal system. But in times of war it is so designed as to make it work as though it was a unitary system." — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, Vol. VII, 4 November 1948

### Seven Federal Features of the Indian Constitution

Despite its centralising safeguards, the Indian Constitution incorporates all **seven classic structural attributes** of a genuine federal polity:

1. **Dual Polity:** The Constitution establishes a dual polity consisting of the **Union** at the Centre and the **States** at the periphery. Each tier is endowed with sovereign legislative and executive powers derived **directly from the Constitution**—not granted as revocable delegations by the Central Government. The Union handles matters of national importance (defence, foreign affairs, currency, communications), while the States govern matters of regional and local concern (public order, police, agriculture, public health, local self-government). *(Note: The **73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts, 1992** innovatively added a constitutional **third tier**—Panchayats and Municipalities—making India a unique multi-tiered federation.)*
2. **Written Constitution:** A federal system cannot function smoothly without a clear written compact defining jurisdictional boundaries so that neither tier encroaches upon the other. India possesses not merely a written Constitution, but the **lengthiest written Constitution in the world** (originally comprising a Preamble, 395 Articles across 22 Parts, and 8 Schedules; presently around 470 Articles across 25 Parts and 12 Schedules). It codifies in meticulous detail the organisation, structure, powers, and mutual obligations of both the Union and State Governments, minimising ambiguities.
3. **Division of Powers:** `Article 246` read with the **Seventh Schedule** divides legislative subjects systematically across three lists:
   - **List I — Union List:** Originally **97** subjects (subsequently expanded via lettered entries to 100, standing at **98 active entries** post-101st Amendment Act, 2016), over which Parliament holds exclusive legislative competence.
   - **List II — State List:** Originally **66** subjects (standing at **59 active entries** post-42nd and 101st Amendments), over which State Legislatures hold exclusive competence in normal times.
   - **List III — Concurrent List:** Originally **47** subjects (now **52 entries**, after the 42nd Amendment shifted five subjects from the State List), over which both Parliament and State Legislatures can legislate, with Parliamentary law prevailing in the event of irreconcilable repugnancy (`Article 254`).
   - Additionally, `Article 246A` (inserted by the 101st Amendment, 2016) grants simultaneous concurrent power over **Goods and Services Tax (GST)**, while `Article 248` assigns **residuary powers** to the Centre.
4. **Supremacy of the Constitution:** The Constitution is the supreme (*grundnorm*) law of the land. Every legislative enactment, executive order, or judicial pronouncement of both the Union and the States must strictly conform to constitutional provisions. If either Parliament or a State Legislature transgresses its assigned list or violates constitutional limitations, the Supreme Court and High Courts are empowered to strike down the ultra vires law through **Judicial Review** (`Articles 13, 32, 131, 226`).
5. **Rigid Constitution:** Because the division of powers and constitutional supremacy would collapse if the Centre could unilaterally alter federal rules by simple majority, the Constitution is **rigid** with respect to its federal structure. Under the proviso to **`Article 368(2)`**, any constitutional amendment affecting federal provisions—namely the election of the President (`Arts. 54–55`), extent of Union and State executive power (`Arts. 73, 162`), the Supreme Court and High Courts, distribution of legislative powers and the **Seventh Schedule**, Goods and Services Tax Council (`Art. 279A`), representation of States in Parliament, or `Article 368` itself—requires **BOTH** a **special majority of Parliament** (majority of total membership of each House plus two-thirds of members present and voting) **AND ratification by not less than half of the State Legislatures** by simple majority.
6. **Independent Judiciary:** Every federation requires an impartial constitutional umpire to settle Centre–State and inter-State disputes and safeguard constitutional supremacy. The Constitution establishes an **independent Supreme Court** headed by the Chief Justice of India, insulated from executive pressure through security of tenure (`Article 124(4)` impeachment), fixed service conditions, salaries charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, and post-retirement practice restrictions. Under **`Article 131`**, the Supreme Court exercises **exclusive original jurisdiction** to adjudicate federal legal disputes between the Centre and one or more States, or between two or more States.
7. **Bicameralism:** The Constitution establishes a bicameral Union Parliament consisting of an **Upper House (Rajya Sabha — Council of States)** and a **Lower House (Lok Sabha — House of the People)**. While the Lok Sabha represents the people of India directly as a single electorate, the Rajya Sabha represents the **States and Union Territories** of the Indian federation. Even though the Rajya Sabha is less powerful than the US Senate in financial matters, it acts as a crucial institutional guardian of federal interests—possessing two exclusive federal powers: authorising Parliament to legislate on a State List subject in the national interest (`Article 249`, by two-thirds present and voting) and authorising the creation of a new All-India Service (`Article 312`, by two-thirds present and voting).

### Fifteen-Plus Unitary and Non-Federal Features

Alongside these seven federal pillars, the Indian Constitution embeds an extraordinary array of **unitary or non-federal features** that deliberately tilt the balance of authority toward the Union. Mastering all of them is essential for both Prelims elimination and Mains constitutional critique:

1. **Strong Centre:** The distribution of powers under the Seventh Schedule heavily favours the Union. The **Union List** contains not only the largest number of subjects (98 active vs 59 in the State List) but also the most fiscally buoyant and strategically vital domains (defence, atomic energy, currency, banking, foreign affairs, railways, income and corporate taxes). On the **Concurrent List**, central law overrides state law in case of conflict (`Article 254(1)`). Crucially, **residuary powers** (`Article 248`) belong solely to the Union Parliament—whereas in the United States and Australia, residuary powers vest in the States.
2. **States Are Not Indestructible:** Unlike classic federations where constituent units enjoy guaranteed territorial permanence, **`Article 3`** empowers Parliament to form new states, merge or split existing ones, or alter their areas, boundaries, and names unilaterally by a **simple majority**. While the President must refer the Bill to the concerned State Legislature for expressing its views within a stipulated period, those views are **not binding** on either the President or Parliament (*Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay*, 1960).
3. **Single Constitution:** Ordinarily, federations permit states to adopt their own separate constitutions within the national framework (as all 50 US states do). In India, a **single Constitution** frames the governmental machinery for both the Centre and every State; no State holds the power to frame its own separate constitution. *(The lone historical exception of Jammu and Kashmir's separate state constitution ceased to operate following the August 2019 Presidential Orders upheld in **In Re: Article 370**, December 2023.)*
4. **Flexibility of the Constitution:** Outside the specific federal clauses protected by the `Article 368(2)` proviso, the bulk of the Indian Constitution can be amended **unilaterally by Parliament**—either by simple majority (outside `Article 368`) or by special majority without state ratification. Moreover, the power to **initiate** a constitutional amendment lies exclusively with Parliament; unlike the US Constitution (Article V), Indian State Legislatures cannot initiate constitutional amendments.
5. **No Equality of State Representation in the Rajya Sabha:** The **Fourth Schedule** allocates Rajya Sabha seats roughly in proportion to **population**, ranging from **1 seat each** for smaller states (Goa, Sikkim, Nagaland, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Tripura, Arunachal Pradesh) up to **31 seats** for Uttar Pradesh. By contrast, the United States Senate grants every state—whether California or Wyoming—exactly **2 seats** regardless of population, and the Australian Senate allocates **12 seats** equally to each original state.
6. **Emergency Provisions (`Part XVIII`, Articles 352–360):** India's Emergency provisions represent the single most potent unitary lever in the Constitution. Under **National Emergency (`Article 352`)**, Parliament gains concurrent legislative power over the entire State List (`Article 250`), the Union can issue binding executive directions to States on *any* matter (`Article 353`), and revenue distribution can be altered (`Article 354`). Under **President's Rule (`Article 356`)**, the Centre assumes direct executive and legislative control of a State. Under **Financial Emergency (`Article 360`)**, the Centre can enforce financial propriety canons and require State Money Bills to be reserved for Presidential assent. Remarkably, this federal-to-unitary metamorphosis occurs **without any formal constitutional amendment**.
7. **Single Citizenship (`Part II`, Articles 5–11):** Rejecting the dual citizenship model of the United States, Switzerland, and Australia (where every individual is simultaneously a citizen of the federation and of their home state), India provides **single Indian citizenship** modelled on Canada and Britain. Every Indian enjoys identical constitutional rights across the length and breadth of the country regardless of the state of birth or domicile.
8. **Integrated Judiciary:** Unlike the United States—which maintains two parallel judicial systems (federal courts enforcing federal laws and state courts enforcing state laws)—India operates a **single, unified, integrated judicial pyramid** with the Supreme Court at the apex, High Courts in the middle, and subordinate courts beneath. This unified hierarchy enforces **both Central and State laws** seamlessly.
9. **All-India Services (`Article 312`):** In classic federations, the Centre and States maintain completely separate bureaucracies. India uniquely retains **All-India Services**—the **Indian Administrative Service (IAS)**, **Indian Police Service (IPS)**, and **Indian Forest Service (IFoS, constituted 1966)**—which are common to both tiers. Recruited and trained by the Union, their officers occupy top administrative posts in the States under the **immediate operational control** of State Governments, yet **ultimate disciplinary control** (including dismissal or removal under `Article 311`) rests solely with the Central Government.
10. **Integrated Audit Machinery (`Article 148`):** The **Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG)** audits the public accounts of **both the Union Government and every State Government**. Yet the CAG is appointed solely by the President (on Union advice) and removed only by Parliament—States have zero voice in the appointment or removal of the official who scrutinises their treasuries. (By contrast, the US Comptroller General audits only federal accounts, never state accounts.)
11. **Parliament's Authority Over the State List:** Even outside an Emergency, the State List is not hermetically sealed. Parliament can legislate directly on State List subjects across **five extraordinary gateways**: (a) **`Article 249`** (Rajya Sabha resolution passed by two-thirds present and voting in the national interest); (b) **`Article 250`** (during National Emergency); (c) **`Article 252`** (on requesting resolutions of two or more States—after which only Parliament can amend or repeal the law); (d) **`Article 253`** (implementing international treaties, agreements, or conventions without requiring State consent); and (e) **`Article 356`** (during President's Rule).
12. **Appointment and Dual Role of the Governor (`Articles 155–156`):** Unlike American State Governors—who are directly elected by state voters—Indian State Governors are **appointed by the President** and hold office **during the President's pleasure**. Serving simultaneously as the constitutional head of the State and the vital **agent of the Centre**, the Governor reports state political crises (`Article 356`), administers discretionary powers (`Article 163`), and monitors constitutional compliance.
13. **Integrated Election Machinery (`Article 324`):** The **Election Commission of India (ECI)** conducts elections not only to Parliament and the offices of President and Vice-President, but also to **every State Legislature**. Constituted entirely by the President without State involvement, it contrasts sharply with the United States, where federal and state elections are administered separately by state authorities. *(Note: State Election Commissions under `Articles 243K` and `243ZA` supervise only local Panchayat and Municipal elections.)*
14. **Veto Over State Bills (`Articles 200–201` & `304(b)`):** Under `Article 200`, the Governor is empowered to **reserve** certain Bills passed by a State Legislature for the consideration of the President (mandatory if a Bill endangers the constitutional position of the High Court). Under `Article 201`, the President may withhold assent not merely in the first instance, but **even if the State Legislature re-passes the Bill** after reconsideration—investing the Union executive with an **absolute veto** over reserved State legislation. In addition, certain State Bills (such as those imposing trade restrictions under `Article 304(b)`) require **prior Presidential sanction** even before introduction!
15. **Financial Dependence, Union Executive Directions & Coercive Sanction (`Articles 256, 257, 365` & Part XII):** India's fiscal architecture builds a sharp **Vertical Fiscal Imbalance**: the Centre collects the most elastic revenue streams while States bear over 60% of developmental and social expenditure, creating heavy reliance on Finance Commission transfers (`Art. 270`), statutory grants (`Art. 275`), discretionary Centrally Sponsored Scheme grants (`Art. 282`), and Central consent for state borrowing (`Art. 293(3)`). Administratively, `Articles 256` and `257` empower the Union to issue binding directions to States (compliance with parliamentary laws, protection of railways and national communications), backed by **`Article 365`**—which stipulates that a State's failure to obey Union directions empowers the President to hold that constitutional machinery has failed, thereby triggering **President's Rule (`Article 356`)**.
16. **Union Control Over High Court Appointments & State PSC Removals (`Articles 217, 222, 317`):** Judges of State High Courts are appointed and transferred by the President (following the Supreme Court collegium process), and Parliament can establish a common High Court for two or more States (`Article 231`). Similarly, while the Chairman and members of a **State Public Service Commission (SPSC)** are appointed by the Governor, they can be **removed ONLY by the President** (after Supreme Court inquiry under `Article 317`); and Parliament can create a **Joint State Public Service Commission (JSPSC)** on state request (`Article 315(2)`), whose members are both appointed and removed by the President.
17. **Central Deployment of Armed Forces in States (`Article 355` & Entry 2A, List I):** Even though "Public Order" and "Police" belong to the State List (Entries 1 and 2), `Article 355` imposes a constitutional duty on the Union to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance, and the **42nd Amendment Act, 1976** inserted **`Entry 2A` in the Union List**, enabling the deployment of Central Armed Police Forces (CAPFs) in any State in aid of civil power, subject to Union control.

### Scholarly Characterisations and Critical Evaluations

Because India refuses to conform neatly to the 1787 Philadelphia archetype, eminent constitutional jurists and political scientists have offered celebrated—and vigorously contested—characterisations of the Indian federal system. Matching each scholar accurately to their exact phrase and analytical reasoning is a staple of both UPSC Prelims and Mains GS-2 answers:

- **Prof. K.C. Wheare — *"Quasi-federal"*:** Writing from the benchmark of classical American dual federalism (*Modern Constitutions*, 1951), Wheare argued that the Indian Constitution establishes a system of government which is at most **quasi-federal**, describing the Indian Union as *"a unitary state with subsidiary federal features rather than a federal state with subsidiary unitary features."* He cited `Article 3`, central appointment of Governors, reservation of State Bills, and Emergency powers as decisive deviations from the strict "federal principle" (where general and regional governments are each coordinate and wholly independent).
- **Granville Austin — *"Cooperative federalism"*:** Rejecting Wheare's rigid formalism in his classic *The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation* (1966), Austin hailed India as the world's premier exemplar of **cooperative federalism**. He observed that although the Constitution created a strong Central Government to steer nation-building and social revolution, it did not make State Governments weak or reduce them to mere administrative agencies; rather, it produced an interdependent partnership where national policies rely continuously on state cooperation and political consensus.
- **W.H. Morris-Jones — *"Bargaining federalism"*:** Analysing the living political dynamics between New Delhi and state capitals (*The Government and Politics of India*, 1964), Morris-Jones coined the term **bargaining federalism**. Neither tier enjoys unchallenged supremacy in practice; instead, Centre–State relations operate as an ongoing arena of hard political, fiscal, and electoral negotiation—a dynamic that intensified dramatically after single-party dominance ended in 1967 and during the coalition era (1989–2014 and post-2024).
- **Sir Ivor Jennings — *"Federation with a strong centralising tendency"*:** In *Some Characteristics of the Indian Constitution* (1953), Jennings accepted India's unmistakable federal framework while noting that its framers infused a deliberate centralising bias to counter fissiparous forces, communal divisions, and developmental bottlenecks.
- **Paul H. Appleby — *"Extremely federal"*:** In a striking reversal of Wheare's critique, American public administration scholar Paul Appleby—invited by Nehru's government in 1953 to evaluate Indian administration—described India as **extremely federal**. Looking at *administrative reality* rather than bare constitutional clauses, Appleby pointed out that unlike the United States Federal Government (which runs massive direct field bureaucracies across all states), the Indian Union Government possesses minimal field administration of its own and depends almost completely on **State Governments and district bureaucracies** to implement agricultural, health, educational, taxation, and welfare programmes!
- **C.H. Alexandrowicz — *Sui generis* ("a federation of its own kind"):** Rejecting Wheare's label as misleading and Eurocentric, Alexandrowicz argued in *Constitutional Developments in India* (1957) that India is a genuine federation ***sui generis***—a unique constitutional creation tailored to post-colonial Asian realities rather than a defective copy of Western models.
- **K. Santhanam — Extra-Constitutional Financial & Planning Centralisation:** While serving as Chairman of the Second Finance Commission, Santhanam identified **two operational factors**—beyond formal constitutional articles—that reinforced central unitary dominance during the early decades: (1) the overwhelming **financial superiority of the Centre** and consequent State dependence on central grants, and (2) the emergence of the powerful extra-constitutional **Planning Commission**, which effectively controlled the entire developmental blueprint of the States through discretionary plan assistance.
- **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's Constitutional Rebuttal:** Pre-empting quasi-federal critiques inside the Constituent Assembly itself, Ambedkar maintained that India's Constitution is unequivocally a **Federal Constitution** because it establishes a **Dual Polity** with constitutionally guaranteed, coordinate State spheres—stressing that States are *not* leagues or subordinate departments of the Centre and that central overriding powers are strictly confined to exceptional emergencies.

### Judicial Evolution of Indian Federalism

The Supreme Court's interpretation of India's federal character did not remain static; it evolved across six decades alongside India's political shift from one-party Congress dominance to multi-party coalition federalism. Early Constitution Benches leaned sharply toward centralist formalism, whereas post-1990 jurisprudence decisively elevated federalism into an inviolable constitutional bedrock.

#### Early Centralist Phase: West Bengal (1962) and Rajasthan (1977)

In ***State of West Bengal v. Union of India* (1962)**, a 6-Judge Bench (by a 5–1 majority led by Chief Justice B.P. Sinha) upheld Parliament's Coal Bearing Areas (Acquisition and Development) Act, 1957, empowering the Union to acquire coal-bearing land owned directly by the State of West Bengal. Examining the unitary biases of the Constitution, the majority declared that the Indian Constitution was **not truly federal** in the classical American sense, asserting that political sovereignty did not reside separately in the States. Yet the judgment is equally remembered for **Justice K. Subba Rao's prophetic lone dissent**, which insisted that India is genuinely federal, that States are supreme within their allotted List II sphere, and that the Centre cannot confiscate State property without constitutional warrant.

Fifteen years later, in ***State of Rajasthan v. Union of India* (1977)**, a 7-Judge Bench upheld the Janata Government's dissolution of nine Congress-ruled State Assemblies under `Article 356` after Congress's rout in the 1977 Lok Sabha elections. Chief Justice M.H. Beg famously remarked that Indian federalism was **"watered down"** to meet the needs of national development and unity, characterising the Constitution as more unitary than federal—*"amphibian"* in structure, shifting easily between federal and unitary postures.

#### The Watershed Turn: S.R. Bommai (1994) and Federalism as Basic Structure

The decisive doctrinal reversal arrived with the landmark **9-Judge Constitution Bench** decision in ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (11 March 1994)**. Scrutinising the misuse of `Article 356` across Karnataka, Meghalaya, Nagaland, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Himachal Pradesh, the Supreme Court declared unequivocally that **federalism is a Basic Feature (Basic Structure) of the Constitution of India**.

Writing a deeply influential opinion, **Justice B.P. Jeevan Reddy** dismantled the myth that centralising safeguards diminish States into administrative subordinates. He clarified that the Constitution makes States **independent constitutional entities**—**not mere appendages or satellites of the Centre**—and that within the sphere allotted to them under the Seventh Schedule, **States are supreme**. The fact that greater powers are conferred on the Union during emergencies or on national subjects does not dilute the constitutional sovereignty of States in normal times; consequently, the Centre must exercise overriding powers like `Article 356` with extreme constitutional restraint, subject to rigorous judicial review.

#### Modern Doctrinal Refinement: Kuldip Nayar (2006), NCT of Delhi (2018 & 2023), and In Re: Article 370 (2023)

Subsequent Constitution Benches built a nuanced, pragmatic federal jurisprudence atop *Bommai*:

- ***Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India* (2006, 5-Judge Bench):** Upholding the 2003 amendment to the Representation of the People Act, 1951 (which abolished the domicile requirement for Rajya Sabha candidates and introduced open-ballot voting to curb cross-voting), the Court reiterated that federalism is indeed Basic Structure, yet clarified that **India's federal model is unique** and does not mandate strict American-style territorial domicile for upper-house representatives.
- ***Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India* (4 July 2018, 5-Judge Bench) & May 2023 (5-Judge Bench):** Interpreting `Article 239AA`, the Supreme Court articulated the twin doctrines of **"collaborative federalism"** and **"pragmatic federalism"**, anchored in **constitutional trust** and **constitutional morality**. The Court held that federal actors (Union, States, and elected UT Ministries) must engage in continuous dialogue and cooperative accommodation rather than adversarial obstruction—later applying this principle in May 2023 to affirm the elected Delhi Government's executive control over civil services (outside Police, Public Order, and Land), a ruling subsequently modified by Parliament's GNCTD Amendment Act, 2023.
- ***In Re: Article 370 of the Constitution* (11 December 2023, 5-Judge Unanimous Bench):** While upholding the August 2019 abrogation of `Article 370` on the ground that it was a **temporary, transitional provision** and Jammu & Kashmir retained no separate internal sovereignty after signing the Instrument of Accession, the Constitution Bench strongly affirmed that **asymmetric federalism** is a thriving, permanent hallmark of India's constitutional design—visibly embodied across `Articles 371` to `371J` and the Fifth and Sixth Schedules.
- **2024–2025 Fiscal & Executive Federalism Milestones:** Two 9-Judge Benches in 2024—***Mineral Area Development Authority (MADA) v. SAIL*** (25 July 2024, upholding States' independent mineral taxing power under Entries 49 & 50, List II) and ***State of Uttar Pradesh v. Lalta Prasad Vaish*** (23 October 2024, affirming State competence over industrial alcohol under Entry 8, List II)—vigorously protected State legislative turf against expansive Union List interpretations. Similarly, the 5-Judge Constitution Bench opinion in ***In re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills*** (**20 November 2025**, `2025 INSC 1333`) balanced institutional separation (rejecting rigid court-imposed assent deadlines or Article 142 "deemed assent") with federal accountability (denying Governors an indefinite "pocket veto" over elected State Bills).

## 2. Cooperative, Competitive & Asymmetric Federalism

While legal textbooks often freeze federalism into a static contest between Union power and State autonomy, the real governance of a 1.4-billion-strong republic runs on a far richer, three-dimensional framework. Contemporary Indian federalism operates simultaneously through **cooperative federalism** (vertical and horizontal partnership toward shared national goals), **competitive federalism** (healthy horizontal emulation among states for capital, fiscal strength, and public-service delivery), and **asymmetric federalism** (differentiated constitutional and fiscal tailoring to accommodate India's unmatched ethnic, geographical, and developmental diversity). Understanding how these three engines reinforce—and occasionally collide with—one another is the hallmark of high-scoring UPSC Mains GS-2 answers.

### Cooperative Federalism: Philosophy, Institutional Pillars and Critiques

Classic nineteenth-century federalism was likened by political scientist Morton Grodzins to a **"layer cake"**—where federal and state governments operated inside watertight, mutually exclusive jurisdictional layers. Twentieth- and twenty-first-century governance, however, demands **"marble-cake" (cooperative) federalism**, in which national and regional tiers intertwine continuously to solve complex challenges—public health, climate change, infrastructure corridors, internal security, and macroeconomic stability—that respect no state borders.

As Granville Austin observed, cooperative federalism is woven into the Indian Constitution's DNA. It functions along two complementary axes: **vertical cooperation** (between New Delhi and the States) and **horizontal cooperation** (among States themselves). Five institutional pillars anchor cooperative federalism in India today:

1. **NITI Aayog and the "Team India" Paradigm (January 2015):** Replacing the sixty-five-year-old **Planning Commission**—which had long attracted criticism from states and commissions alike for imposing top-down, one-size-fits-all Five-Year Plans via discretionary plan transfers—the Union established **NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India)** on **1 January 2015** as a policy think tank grounded in **"Team India"** cooperative governance. Its apex **Governing Council** brings together the Prime Minister, all State Chief Ministers, Chief Ministers of UTs with legislatures (Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu & Kashmir), and Lieutenant Governors/Administrators of other UTs on a single deliberative table. NITI Aayog also provides for time-bound **Regional Councils** to resolve inter-state issues and constitutes **Sub-Groups of Chief Ministers** (on CSS rationalisation, Swachh Bharat, Skill Development, and Digital Payments) so that state executives co-author national policy frameworks.
2. **The GST Council (`Article 279A`, 101st Amendment Act, 2016):** Widely celebrated as India's boldest institutional experiment in **"pooled sovereignty"**, the GST Council merged fragmented Union and State indirect-tax silos into a unified common market launched on **1 July 2017**. With 33 members (Union Finance Minister as Chairperson, Union MoS Finance, and 31 State/UT Finance Ministers) deciding by a **three-fourths weighted majority** (Centre holding 1/3rd weight; all States/UTs collectively holding 2/3rd weight), neither the Union nor the States can impose decisions unilaterally—establishing a structural **mutual veto**. Crucially, across over fifty-seven meetings—including the landmark **56th meeting (3 September 2025)** approving **"GST 2.0"** two-rate rationalisation (5% merit and 18% standard, plus 40% demerit rate, exempting individual life/health insurance) and the **57th meeting (8 October 2026)** recommending removal of GST arrest provisions—virtually every major reform has been forged by **consensus** rather than divisive division voting.
   - Notably, in ***Union of India v. M/s Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd.*** (**19 May 2022**), the Supreme Court held that GST Council recommendations carry **persuasive value** and are **not legally binding** on Parliament or State Legislatures under `Article 246A`. Rejecting the notion that cooperative federalism means blind compliance with the Centre, the Court explicitly lauded **"uncooperative federalism"**—constitutional dialogue, dissent, and bargaining by States—as a healthy safeguard of democratic pluralism!
3. **Constitutional & Statutory Coordination Forums:** The **Inter-State Council (`Article 263`)**, the five statutory **Zonal Councils (1956 Act)**, and the **North-Eastern Council (1971 Act)** provide formal vertical and horizontal coordination platforms (examined thoroughly in Section 5).
4. **Rationalisation of Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS):** Historically, over 130 tied Centrally Sponsored Schemes routed through `Article 282` constrained state budgetary autonomy. Accepting the recommendations of the **NITI Aayog Sub-Group of Chief Ministers on Rationalisation of CSS (2015)** (chaired by then Madhya Pradesh Chief Minister Shivraj Singh Chouhan), the Union consolidated 66 schemes into **28 umbrella schemes** organised into three tiers:
   - **Core of the Core Schemes (6 schemes):** Focused on social protection and inclusion (e.g., MGNREGA, National Social Assistance Programme, umbrella schemes for SCs, STs, OBCs, and Minorities), retaining higher pre-existing Union funding shares.
   - **Core Schemes:** Funded on a standardised **60:40** Centre–State ratio for General States and **90:10** for the **8 North-Eastern and 3 Himalayan States** (100% Union-funded for UTs without legislatures).
   - **Optional Schemes:** Funded on a **50:50** ratio for General States (**80:20** for NE/Himalayan States), leaving states free to opt in or stay out.
   - A **25% flexi-fund window** (30% for NE/Himalayan States) was simultaneously introduced to allow states to adapt schemes to local priorities.
5. **Rule-Based Fiscal Devolution (`Article 280`):** Following the **14th Finance Commission's (2015–20)** watershed increase of States' vertical share in the divisible pool from **32% to 42%**, both the **15th Finance Commission (2021–26)** and the **16th Finance Commission (2026–31, tabled 1 February 2026)** maintained untied vertical devolution at **41%** (adjusting 1 percentage point solely for the Union Territories of J&K and Ladakh), shifting the fiscal centre of gravity away from discretionary Planning Commission handouts toward rule-based constitutional transfers.

> "The 'recommendations' of the GST Council are the product of a collaborative dialogue involving the Union and States. They are recommendatory in nature. To regard them as binding edicts would disrupt fiscal federalism, where both the Union and the States are conferred equal power to legislate on GST... Indian federalism is a dialogue between cooperative and uncooperative federalism where the federal units are at liberty to use different means of persuasion ranging from collaboration to contestation." — Justice D.Y. Chandrachud, *Union of India v. M/s Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd.*, 19 May 2022

#### Limits of Cooperative Federalism and "Coercive / Centralising Federalism" Critiques

Despite these impressive institutional milestones, constitutional scholars (including Louise Tillin, Yamini Aiyar, and Suhas Palshikar) caution that India's cooperative federalism increasingly exhibits **centralising or "coercive" tendencies** in practice:

- **Asymmetry of Fiscal Power:** While NITI Aayog promotes dialogue, it lacks any independent fund-allocating mandate, whereas the Union Finance Ministry exercises tight control over tied `Article 282` CSS releases, Single Nodal Agency (SNA) treasury rules, mandatory central branding of co-funded schemes, and `Article 293(3)` state borrowing ceilings.
- **Erosion of the Divisible Pool:** Rapidly expanding Union reliance on **non-shareable cesses and `Article 271` surcharges** compressed the divisible pool from roughly **89%** of Union Gross Tax Revenue during the 13th FC period to around **78%** during the 15th FC period—meaning States' effective share of Gross Tax Revenue fell closer to ~32% despite the headline 41% devolution figure!
- **Dormancy of Constitutional Consensus Bodies:** The **Inter-State Council** has not convened a single full sitting since **16 July 2016** (over a decade of dormancy as of November 2026), and opposition-ruled states frequently boycott or protest NITI Aayog Governing Council meetings citing unilateral agendas.
- **Unilateral Legislation in Overlapping Domains:** Enactment of far-reaching statutes impacting State List subjects—such as the repealed 2020 Farm Laws (agriculture/markets) or the **MMDR Amendment Act, 2026** (inserting Section 9D to restrict state mineral levies shortly after the 9-Judge Bench verdict in *MADA v. SAIL*)—without structured prior consensus fuels charges of **"executive federalism"** overpowering genuine partnership.

### Competitive Federalism: Engines, Performance Rankings and Divergence Risks

Whereas cooperative federalism emphasises vertical and horizontal *solidarity*, **competitive federalism** harnesses healthy horizontal *rivalry* among States—and between States and the Centre—to spur economic reforms, fiscal discipline, and superior citizen outcomes. Economists trace the conceptual logic to **Charles Tiebout's "voting with one's feet" hypothesis** and **Justice Louis Brandeis's** famous description of constituent states as **"laboratories of democracy"**, where innovative governance models tested in one state (such as Gujarat's solar-power parks, Tamil Nadu's midday meals, Kerala's decentralised primary healthcare, Odisha's cyclone disaster management, or Telangana's TS-iPASS industrial clearance) are emulated nationwide.

In India, competitive federalism was virtually dormant during the pre-1991 era of centralised industrial licensing ("Licence-Permit Raj"). The **1991 Economic Reforms** dismantled industrial licensing and opened foreign direct investment (FDI), transforming State Chief Ministers into active investment ambassadors competing directly for private capital, manufacturing hubs, and IT corridors. Since 2015, the Union Government and NITI Aayog have institutionalised competitive federalism through **transparent, outcome-based comparative benchmarking**:

- **Business Reforms Action Plan (BRAP) / State Ease of Doing Business (DPIIT):** Periodically assesses States and UTs on hundreds of regulatory reform parameters—single-window digital clearances, online land records, labour-law compliance, construction permits, and real-time business-user feedback—categorising performers into *Top Achievers*, *Achievers*, *Aspirers*, and *Emerging Business Ecosystems*.
- **Export Preparedness Index (EPI — NITI Aayog):** Benchmarks states across four pillars—Policy, Business Ecosystem, Export Ecosystem, and Export Performance—grouping coastal, landlocked, Himalayan, and UT economies separately to ensure fair peer comparison.
- **SDG India Index (NITI Aayog):** Tracks state and UT performance across 16 Sustainable Development Goals on a 0–100 composite scale (*Achievers*, *Front Runners*, *Performers*, *Aspirants*), embedding competitive accountability into social-sector indicators like maternal health, clean water, gender equality, and climate action.
- **Good Governance Index (GGI — DARPG):** Evaluates governance outcomes across 10 sectors (agriculture, commerce, human resource development, public health, infrastructure, judicial/public security, environment, citizen-centric governance) across peer groups (Group A, Group B, NE/Hill States, UTs).
- **Aspirational Districts Programme (ADP, 2018) & Aspirational Blocks Programme (ABP, 2023):** Extends competitive federalism down to the **sub-state level** across **112 backward districts** and **500 backward blocks**. Real-time monthly **Delta Rankings** measure incremental month-on-month improvement across health, nutrition, education, agriculture, financial inclusion, and basic infrastructure—triggering peer emulation among District Collectors and Block Development Officers.
- **Fiscal Health Index (FHI — NITI Aayog):** Extending benchmarking to state treasuries, NITI Aayog launched the inaugural **FHI 2025** (January 2025) and published **FHI 2026 (March 2026)** evaluating FY 2023–24 fiscal quality across 18 major states on five sub-indices (Quality of Expenditure, Revenue Mobilisation, Fiscal Prudence, Debt Index, and Debt Sustainability). In FHI 2026, **Odisha (73.1)**, **Goa (54.7)**, and **Jharkhand (50.5)** emerged as top *Achievers*, while **Punjab, Andhra Pradesh, West Bengal, and Kerala** occupied the bottom tier under persistent revenue deficits and elevated debt ratios.
- **State Investment Summits & 16th Finance Commission Efficiency Signal:** Flagship conclaves—*Vibrant Gujarat*, *Invest Karnataka*, *Tamil Nadu Global Investors Meet*, *UP Global Investors Summit*, *Advantage Assam*—turn state capitals into global capital marketplaces. Reinforcing this efficiency paradigm, the **16th Finance Commission (tabled 1 February 2026)** introduced a landmark **10% horizontal devolution weight for "Contribution to GDP"** (measured via the square root of each state's GSDP share, replacing the 2.5% tax-effort metric), directly rewarding growth-generating states within India's fiscal transfer architecture.

> [!example]
> **Sub-State Competitive Federalism in Action — Aspirational Districts & FHI 2026:**
> Instead of penalising historically lagging regions for low absolute baselines, the **Aspirational Districts Programme (112 districts)** ranks districts on *incremental delta progress*—helping previously underserved districts such as Gumla (Jharkhand), Chandauli (UP), and Nuh (Haryana) achieve rapid gains in institutional deliveries, micro-irrigation, and bank-account penetration. Conversely, **NITI Aayog's FHI 2026 (March 2026)** demonstrates how transparent benchmarking exposes structural fiscal stress—highlighting that mineral-rich **Odisha** maintained sustained revenue surpluses and high capital-outlay shares while wealthier industrial states like **Punjab** and **Kerala** faced mounting debt-to-GSDP stress from rigid committed expenditure.

#### Structural Risks of Unchecked Competitive Federalism

While healthy competition stimulates innovation, economists and federal scholars highlight **four acute hazards** when competition is divorced from cooperative equalisation:

1. **Widening North–South and Coastal–Hinterland Divergence:** Indian states do not begin the competitive race from an equal starting line. Richer, coastal, and historically industrialised states (Maharashtra, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Telangana) possess ports, deep urban clusters, superior human capital, and high own-tax bases, enabling them to attract the lion's share of FDI and high-tech manufacturing. Landlocked, densely populated, or hilly hinterland states (Bihar, Jharkhand, eastern Uttar Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, North-East) risk falling into a structural poverty trap as divergence in per capita GSDP widens (with India's richest states now boasting per capita incomes over five to six times higher than the poorest).
2. **"Race to the Bottom" in Regulation and Taxation:** Eager to lure investors away from neighbouring states, governments may offer unsustainable land giveaways, competitive power subsidies, or diluted environmental and labour protections—eroding fiscal revenue and worker welfare nationwide.
3. **Competitive Populism and Unfunded Freebies:** Electoral rivalry across state borders can turn into a fiscal spiral of unconditional cash transfers, free electricity, and farm loan waivers, crowding out capital expenditure on schools, hospitals, and infrastructure (a concern flagged sharply by the RBI and the 16th Finance Commission).
4. **Metric Gaming and Regional Parochialism:** Chasing index scores can incentivise superficial paperwork reforms rather than genuine ground-level ease, while local job-protectionism (e.g., statutory private-sector domicile quotas for "sons of the soil") directly fractures India's unified labour market.

Sustainable Indian federalism therefore demands **collaborative-competitive equilibrium**: fostering dynamic competition for efficiency and governance outcomes while deploying cooperative equalisation (Finance Commission transfers, special infrastructure packages, and capacity support) so that lagging states are lifted onto a level playing field.

```compare
# Cooperative Federalism vs Competitive Federalism
Cooperative Federalism | Competitive Federalism
Core Philosophy :: Partnership, consensus, and mutual interdependence ('Team India') to achieve shared national goals | Healthy inter-state rivalry and peer emulation ('laboratories of democracy') to maximise efficiency and investment
Direction of Interaction :: Primarily vertical (Centre ↔ States) complemented by horizontal consensus (State ↔ State) | Primarily horizontal (State vs State) alongside Centre–State benchmarking and sub-state district rivalry

Ideal Constitutional Balance :: Must respect state legislative dignity (*Mohit Minerals* 2022) and rely on untied rule-based transfers rather than tied conditionalities | Must be cushioned by cooperative equalisation (Finance Commission equity weights + infrastructure catch-up for lagging regions)
```

### Asymmetric Federalism: Constitutional Architecture and Special Category States

In a **symmetric federation** (such as the United States or Australia), every constituent state enjoys formally identical constitutional powers, institutional structure, and representation. In an **asymmetric federation**, different constituent units possess **differentiated powers, safeguards, or fiscal arrangements** tailored to their distinct historical, cultural, linguistic, tribal, or geographical circumstances.

Political theorist **Charles Tarlton** distinguishes two forms of asymmetry, both vibrantly present in India: ***de facto* asymmetry** (arising naturally from vast differences in state size, population, geography, and economic output—compare Uttar Pradesh's 230+ million population with Sikkim's ~7 lakh) and ***de jure* (constitutional) asymmetry** (deliberately written into the Constitution to accommodate ethnic identity, customary laws, tribal land protection, and backward-region development within an indivisible Union). Far from weakening national unity, India's asymmetric federalism has been the constitutional glue that transformed separatist anxieties in the North-East and regional grievances across the Deccan into peaceful democratic integration.

#### Five Constitutional Pillars of Indian Asymmetric Federalism

1. **Part XXI — Special Provisions for Twelve States (`Articles 371` to `371J`):** Titled *"Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions"*, Part XXI encodes bespoke constitutional safeguards for **12 Indian States**—most inserted via constitutional amendments accompanying statehood accords or regional development agreements:

| Article | State(s) Covered | Inserted By | Core Asymmetric Safeguards & Constitutional Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **`Art. 371`** | **Maharashtra & Gujarat** | 7th Amendment (1956) | President may authorise Governors to establish separate **Development Boards** for Vidarbha, Marathwada & rest of Maharashtra, and for Saurashtra, Kutch & rest of Gujarat, ensuring equitable fund allocation, technical education, and state employment. |
| **`Art. 371A`** | **Nagaland** | 13th Amendment (1962) | **Acts of Parliament** on Naga religious/social practices, Naga customary law & procedure, civil/criminal justice under customary law, and **ownership/transfer of land and its resources** do **NOT apply** to Nagaland unless the **State Assembly resolves** so; Tuensang regional council & Governor's special law-and-order responsibility. |
| **`Art. 371B`** | **Assam** | 22nd Amendment (1969) | President may provide for a special **Committee of the Assam Legislative Assembly** comprising MLAs elected from the **Tribal Areas** of the State and other specified members. |
| **`Art. 371C`** | **Manipur** | 27th Amendment (1971) | President may constitute a **Hill Areas Committee** of the Manipur Legislative Assembly; Governor submits annual reports to the President on Hill Areas administration; Union may issue directions to the State. |
| **`Art. 371D` & `371E`** | **Andhra Pradesh & Telangana** | 32nd Amendment (1973) *(extended to TS, 2014)* | `Art. 371D`: President may order equitable opportunities in **public employment and education** across regions (local cadres) and establish an Administrative Tribunal. `Art. 371E`: Parliament may establish a **Central University** in Andhra Pradesh. |
| **`Art. 371F`** | **Sikkim** | 36th Amendment (1975) | Minimum **30-member Assembly**; 1 Lok Sabha seat; Parliament may reserve seats for various sections (e.g., Bhutia–Lepcha, Sangha) to protect their rights; **Governor's special responsibility** for peace and equitable social-economic advancement; protection of pre-merger Sikkim laws. |
| **`Art. 371G`** | **Mizoram** | 53rd Amendment (1986) | Mirrors Nagaland's cultural shield: **Parliamentary Acts** on Mizo religious/social practices, customary law, administration of customary justice, and **land ownership/transfer** do **NOT apply** unless the **Mizoram Assembly resolves** so; minimum **40 Assembly seats**. |
| **`Art. 371H`** | **Arunachal Pradesh** | 55th Amendment (1986) | **Governor holds special responsibility** for **law and order** (exercises individual judgment after consulting Council of Ministers, subject to Presidential directions); minimum **30 Assembly seats**. |
| **`Art. 371I`** | **Goa** | 56th Amendment (1987) | Goa Legislative Assembly shall consist of **not less than 30 members** (exception to `Article 170`'s normal 60-member floor). |
| **`Art. 371J`** | **Karnataka** | 98th Amendment (2012) | Empowers the President to grant the Karnataka Governor special responsibility to establish a separate **Development Board for Hyderabad–Karnataka (Kalyana–Karnataka)** region, ensuring equitable fund allocation and local reservation in education and state jobs. |

2. **Fifth Schedule and Sixth Schedule (`Article 244` — Scheduled & Tribal Areas):**
   - **Fifth Schedule (`Article 244(1)`):** Governs the administration of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in **10 States** (*Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, and Rajasthan*). It establishes a **Tribes Advisory Council (TAC)** (up to 20 members, three-fourths ST MLAs) and empowers the **Governor** to direct that any Act of Parliament or State Legislature shall not apply (or apply with modifications) to a Scheduled Area, and to make regulations restricting tribal land transfer and moneylending.
   - **Sixth Schedule (`Article 244(2)`):** Provides far deeper tribal self-governance in **4 North-Eastern States**—**Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram** (classic mnemonic **AMTM**—note that **Manipur, Nagaland, and Arunachal Pradesh are NOT in the Sixth Schedule!**). It creates **Autonomous District Councils (ADCs)** and Regional Councils (up to 30 members: 26 elected, 4 nominated by Governor) vested with **legislative, executive, judicial, and taxation powers** over land, forests (except reserved forests), shifting cultivation, village councils, inheritance, and customary courts.
3. **Union Territories with Legislatures (Symmetric–Asymmetric Hybrids):**
   - **NCT of Delhi (`Article 239AA`, 69th Amendment Act, 1991):** Possesses an elected Legislative Assembly and Chief Minister empowered to legislate on State and Concurrent Lists **EXCEPT three excluded List II entries**: **Entry 1 (Public Order)**, **Entry 2 (Police)**, and **Entry 18 (Land)**, while Parliament retains overriding competence on all lists. Following prolonged disputes over control of bureaucracy, the Supreme Court's 5-Judge Bench (*NCT of Delhi*, May 2023) affirmed the elected Ministry's control over "Services" (Entry 41, List II); Parliament responded with the **GNCTD (Amendment) Act, 2023**, creating the **National Capital Civil Service Authority (NCCSA)** and granting the Lieutenant Governor final discretion in disagreements. Following the 2025 Delhi Assembly political transition, the new Delhi Government formally **withdrew its Supreme Court challenge on 23 May 2025**, leaving the statutory NCCSA framework operational without a final constitutional adjudication.
   - **Puducherry (`Article 239A`, 14th Amendment Act, 1962)** and **Jammu & Kashmir (J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019)** operate as statutory-legislature Union Territories with distinct executive limits compared to full States.
4. **Historical `Article 370` and *In Re: Article 370* (11 December 2023):** Originally drafted as a transitional bridge reflecting the 1947 Instrument of Accession, `Article 370` granted Jammu and Kashmir its own State Constitution and restricted Parliamentary laws outside Defence, Foreign Affairs, and Communications unless concurred in by the State Government. In August 2019, Presidential Orders **CO 272** and **CO 273** rendered `Article 370` inoperative and Parliament bifurcated the State into two Union Territories (**Jammu & Kashmir** with a legislature and **Ladakh** without one).
   - **Core Holdings (5-Judge Unanimous Bench, 11 Dec 2023):** (a) `Article 370` was a **temporary, transitional provision**—J&K retained no separate "internal sovereignty" post-accession; (b) using `Article 367` (CO 272) to equate the State Legislative Assembly with the J&K Constituent Assembly was **impermissible**, yet the President independently held valid power under **`Article 370(3)`** (CO 273) once the J&K Constituent Assembly dissolved in 1957; (c) carving out Ladakh UT was valid under `Article 3(a)`, while the Bench left open whether `Article 3` permits downgrading an entire State into a UT given the Union's assurance to **restore statehood at the earliest**; and (d) **asymmetric federalism endures vigorously** across `Articles 371–371J`.
   - **Post-Verdict Political Developments:** Following the Court's deadline, J&K held Assembly elections in September–October 2024 (Chief Minister Omar Abdullah taking office in October 2024); with statehood still pending two years later, the J&K Legislative Assembly passed a formal resolution on **28 September 2026** pressing for immediate restoration of full statehood.
5. **Representational Asymmetry (`Fourth Schedule`):** Unlike the symmetric US or Australian Senates, India's population-based allocation of Rajya Sabha seats creates built-in representational asymmetry between populous northern heartland states and smaller peripheral states—amplifying the importance of safeguarding cultural and fiscal asymmetry elsewhere in the Constitution.

#### Special Category States (SCS): Origins, Gadgil–Mukherjee Formula, 14th FC Abolition and Ongoing Demands

A perennial UPSC Prelims and Mains confusion surrounds **Special Category Status (SCS)**. First and foremost: **the Constitution of India contains NO provision for "Special Category States"!** Unlike `Articles 371–371J`, SCS was an **executive/planning classification** introduced in **1969** on the recommendation of the **Fifth Finance Commission** (chaired by Mahavir Tyagi) and formally approved by the **National Development Council (NDC)** to assist states handicapped by harsh terrain, strategic borders, and weak resource bases.

The NDC operationalised Plan assistance through the **Gadgil Formula (1969)** (devised by economist D.R. Gadgil and subsequently revised in 1990–91 as the **Gadgil–Mukherjee Formula** under Pranab Mukherjee). Under NDC guidelines, five cumulative criteria governed eligibility for SCS: **(1)** hilly and difficult terrain; **(2)** low population density and/or sizeable share of tribal population; **(3)** strategic location along international borders; **(4)** economic and infrastructural backwardness; and **(5)** non-viable nature of state finances.

Originally conferred in **1969** on just **three states**—**Jammu & Kashmir, Assam, and Nagaland**—the list progressively expanded to **11 Special Category States**: all **8 North-Eastern States** (Assam, Nagaland, Manipur, Meghalaya, Tripura, Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram) plus **3 Himalayan States** (Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, and the erstwhile State of Jammu & Kashmir). SCS states enjoyed formidable fiscal privileges: roughly **30% of Central Plan Assistance** was earmarked exclusively for them; Central assistance came in a generous **90% grant : 10% loan** ratio (compared to **30% grant : 70% loan** for General Category States); unspent funds did not lapse at year-end; and special concessions in excise, customs, and corporate/income taxes encouraged private industrial relocation.

**Abolition by the 14th Finance Commission (2015–20):** Accepting the recommendations of the **14th Finance Commission** (chaired by Dr. Y.V. Reddy), the Union Government **effectively abolished the Special Category State classification** in 2015 by ending the distinction between Plan and Non-Plan grants and raising overall vertical tax devolution for *all* states from 32% to 42% (leaving resource gaps to be bridged through Finance Commission devolution and Revenue Deficit Grants rather than discretionary NDC plan formulae). However, the Union retained preferential **90:10 funding ratios in Centrally Sponsored Schemes** exclusively for the **8 North-Eastern and 3 Himalayan States** (now categorised simply as NE & Himalayan States).

**Ongoing State Demands (Bihar, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha) and Policy Alternatives:** Despite SCS's abolition, demands for Special Category Status have remained politically explosive—especially resurgent in coalition politics since 2024:
- **Andhra Pradesh** demands SCS citing the severe revenue loss of Hyderabad following the 2014 bifurcation into Telangana and verbal parliamentary assurances given in February 2014.
- **Bihar** cites acute poverty density, recurrent Kosi–Gandak floods, lack of coastline, and the loss of its industrial/mineral hinterland to Jharkhand in 2000.
- **Odisha** highlights its large tribal population and chronic vulnerability to Bay of Bengal cyclones (alongside earlier pleas from Rajasthan over desert terrain).

The Union Government—citing the 14th Finance Commission's bar and the **Inter-Ministerial Group (IMG)** finding that plains states like Bihar fail the cumulative hilly/strategic-border criteria—has consistently declined to revive SCS, preferring instead to extend **Special Financial Assistance / Development Packages** (such as targeted capital outlays in the Union Budgets for Andhra Pradesh's Amaravati capital and Polavaram project, and Bihar's expressways, flood mitigation, and power infrastructure). Policy experts increasingly advocate reviving the principle of the **Raghuram Rajan Committee (2013)**, which proposed replacing the binary, politicised SCS label with an objective **Multi-Dimensional Composite Development Index (MDI)** directing flexible catch-up transfers to the *"Least Developed"* states regardless of topography.

> [!prelims]
> **Asymmetric Federalism & SCS — Five Must-Know Prelims Traps:**
> 1. **SCS is NOT constitutional:** It originated in **1969** via the **5th Finance Commission** and **National Development Council (Gadgil–Mukherjee Formula)**, covered **11 States** (8 NE + HP, UK, erstwhile J&K), and was **abolished post-14th Finance Commission (2015)**.
> 2. **Part XXI (`Arts. 371–371J`)** covers **12 States**—Maharashtra, Gujarat, Nagaland, Assam, Manipur, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Sikkim, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, Goa, and Karnataka. *(Note: **Bihar, Odisha, Rajasthan, and Uttar Pradesh** have NO Article 371 provision!)*
> 3. **Sixth Schedule** covers **ONLY 4 States** (**AMTM**: Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram)—**Manipur, Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh, and Sikkim are EXCLUDED**!
> 4. **Parliamentary Law Opt-Out:** Only **`Art. 371A` (Nagaland)** and **`Art. 371G` (Mizoram)** require an affirmative State Assembly resolution for Parliamentary laws on customary law and land ownership to apply.
> 5. **Minimum Assembly Size Exceptions (`Art. 170` norm = 60):** Sikkim (`371F`), Arunachal Pradesh (`371H`), and Goa (`371I`) have a constitutional floor of **30 seats**; Mizoram (`371G`) has a floor of **40 seats**.

### Contemporary Federal Fault Lines (2024–2026)

Between 2024 and late 2026, India's federal compact has been tested along **six sharp, interconnected fault lines** that frequently anchor UPSC Mains GS-2 questions:

#### 1. Post-2026 Delimitation and Southern States' Demographic Penalty

Under **`Articles 81` and `82`**, Lok Sabha seats are allocated among States in proportion to population, readjusted after each decennial Census. Recognising in the 1970s that population-linked seat reallocation would paradoxically **reward high-fertility states** and **penalise states that achieved rapid population stabilisation**, Parliament enacted the **42nd Amendment Act (1976)** freezing inter-state Lok Sabha seat shares at the **1971 Census** until 2000. The **84th Amendment Act (2001)** extended this inter-state freeze until the **first Census taken after the year 2026**, while the **87th Amendment Act (2003)** permitted *internal* boundary redrawing within each state on the 2001 Census without altering total state seat totals.

That constitutional countdown has now collided with demographic reality. Southern states (Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, and Telangana) achieved below-replacement Total Fertility Rates decades ago, whereas populous northern states (Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, and Rajasthan) continued growing rapidly. Projections show that a purely population-based delimitation on a post-2026 Census could slash the five southern states' share of the Lok Sabha from roughly **24% (129 of 543 seats)** down toward **19–20%**—triggering intense southern anxiety over political marginalisation and "taxation without proportionate representation".

Events between 2025 and 2026 brought this fault line to the centre of national politics:
- On **16 June 2025**, the Union notified **Census of India 2027** (Cabinet approved December 2025; Houselisting scheduled April–September 2026 and Population Enumeration in February 2027)—making it the pivotal first post-2026 Census triggering `Article 82` delimitation.
- On **25 July 2025**, in ***Prof. K. Purushottam Reddy v. Union of India***, the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional embargo freezing state-level seat increases prior to the post-2026 Census (dismissing pleas for premature Assembly seat expansion in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana).
- On **17 April 2026**, the **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026**—which sought to expand the Lok Sabha to **850 seats** and redraw state shares using the **2011 Census**—was **defeated in the Lok Sabha** (298 votes in favour to 230 against, falling well short of the mandatory `Article 368` two-thirds special majority), driven directly by southern and opposition federal concerns.

> [!debate]
> **Post-2026 Delimitation: Democratic Equality vs Federal Fairness**
> - **The Case for Population-Based Reallocation ("One Person, One Vote"):** Under the 1971 freeze, a single Lok Sabha MP in Uttar Pradesh or Bihar currently represents over **25–30 lakh** citizens, whereas an MP in Tamil Nadu or Kerala represents around **16–18 lakh** citizens—creating severe malapportionment that violates the democratic principle of equal voting weight (`Article 81(2)(a)`).
> - **The Case Against Pure Population Reallocation (Federal Compact):** Southern states followed national population-control policy diligently, generate over a third of India's GDP and disproportionate direct-tax revenues, yet face losing parliamentary voice precisely for succeeding in demographic transition—threatening North–South federal cohesion.
> - **Constructive Middle-Path Solutions:** Scholars (e.g., Milan Vaishnav, Yogendra Yadav, and federal commissions) propose three consensual fixes: **(1)** extending the 1971 inter-state share freeze for another 25 years while expanding total House size *proportionately* across all states so no state loses seat share; **(2)** adopting a **weighted federal formula** combining population with demographic performance; or **(3)** pairing Lok Sabha population readjustment with **reforming the Rajya Sabha into an equal-representation chamber** (or granting it enhanced federal veto powers over fiscal and constitutional matters).

#### 2. Fiscal Transfers, Non-Shareable Cesses and State Taxation Turf

Fiscal friction has sharpened across three fronts:
- **Cesses & Surcharges Shrinking the Divisible Pool:** Because `Article 271` surcharges and earmarked cesses remain outside the `Article 270` divisible pool, their expansion shrank the shareable pool from ~89% (13th FC) to ~78% (15th FC), compressing real transfers. Although the **16th Finance Commission (tabled 1 February 2026)** retained vertical devolution at **41%** and added a **10% GDP-contribution weight** (benefiting 14 largely higher-income/southern states such as Karnataka), its **discontinuation of Post-Devolution Revenue Deficit Grants (RDGs)** drew protests from fiscally stressed and smaller states, while 14 poorer and north-eastern states saw reduced *inter se* shares.
- **Article 293(3) Borrowing Caps:** Several opposition-ruled states—led by Kerala in ***State of Kerala v. Union of India*** (**1 April 2024**, `2024 INSC 253`, referred to a **5-Judge Constitution Bench**)—challenged the Union's deduction of state PSU off-budget loans and Public Account liabilities from their annual Net Borrowing Ceilings under `Article 293(3)`.
- **Legislative Pushback on Taxation Federalism:** While the 9-Judge Bench in ***MADA v. SAIL*** (25 July 2024) affirmed States' independent taxing power over mineral rights and mineral-bearing land (`Entries 49 & 50, List II`), Parliament invoked Entry 50's *"subject to any limitations imposed by Parliament"* clause to enact the **MMDR Amendment Act, 2026** (assented August 2026, inserting **Section 9D** restricting state levies except on Union-prescribed conditions)—setting up fresh constitutional scrutiny over whether Parliament can curb Entry 49 land taxes indirectly.

#### 3. Governor–State Friction and Stalled State Bills

Confrontations between Raj Bhavans and elected ministries in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Punjab, and West Bengal—spanning indefinite withholding of assent to Bills (`Articles 200–201`), Vice-Chancellor appointments as ex-officio Chancellors, and departures from Cabinet-approved Assembly addresses—reached an apex in 2025. After a two-judge Supreme Court bench (*State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor*, **8 April 2025**) fixed 1–3 month timelines and declared ten stalled Tamil Nadu bills "deemed assented" under `Article 142`, President Droupadi Murmu invoked `Article 143(1)`. Answering 14 constitutional questions on **20 November 2025** (*Special Reference No. 1 of 2025*, `2025 INSC 1333`), a unanimous **5-Judge Constitution Bench** held that courts **cannot impose rigid numerical deadlines** or grant **"deemed assent"** under `Article 142`—yet crucially affirmed that Governors enjoy **no indefinite pocket veto**, clarifying that prolonged, unexplained inaction remains open to a limited judicial **mandamus** directing timely constitutional action.

#### 4. CBI General Consent Withdrawals (Section 6, DSPE Act, 1946)

Because "Police" and "Public Order" fall in the **State List (Entries 1 & 2)** while the **Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI)** derives statutory powers from the **Delhi Special Police Establishment (DSPE) Act, 1946**, **`Section 6`** mandates **State Government consent** before the CBI can exercise jurisdiction within any State. States historically granted standing **"general consent"** to facilitate seamless anti-corruption probes against central officials. Since 2018, however, multiple non-NDA ruled states (including West Bengal, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Punjab, Telangana, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Jharkhand, and earlier Maharashtra, Rajasthan, and Chhattisgarh) **withdrew general consent**, alleging selective partisan targeting of opposition leaders and compelling the CBI to seek case-by-case approval. *(Subsequent political turnovers led Maharashtra, Chhattisgarh, Rajasthan, and **West Bengal on 8 June 2026** to restore general consent.)*

Three legal rules govern this flashpoint for Prelims: **(1)** withdrawal operates **prospectively** and does not affect ongoing cases registered prior to withdrawal; **(2)** Constitutional Courts—the **Supreme Court and High Courts**—retain unfettered power to direct a CBI investigation in any State **without State consent** (*State of West Bengal v. Committee for Protection of Democratic Rights*, 2010); and **(3)** in ***State of West Bengal v. Union of India*** (**10 July 2024**), the Supreme Court held West Bengal's `Article 131` original suit challenging CBI's unilateral post-withdrawal FIRs to be **maintainable**, underscoring the federal gravity of `Section 6`.

#### 5. Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) and Disaster Relief Fund Disputes (NDRF / SDRF)

States increasingly challenge two discretionary fiscal bottlenecks:
- **CSS Conditionalities & Co-Branding:** States contend that rigid `Article 282` guidelines, Single Nodal Agency (SNA) fund-parking norms, and withholding of central instalments (e.g., under Samagra Shiksha, NHM, or PM-AWAS) over central nomenclature or state branding turn cooperative welfare partnerships into conditional patronage.
- **Disaster Relief Financing (`NDRF` vs `SDRF`):** Under the **Disaster Management Act, 2005**, primary relief flows from each State's **State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF)** (cost-shared 75:25 for General States and 90:10 for NE/Himalayan States), supplemented for severe calamities by the **National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF)** (100% Union-funded). In 2024, **Karnataka** (severe drought) and **Tamil Nadu** (Cyclone Michaung floods) approached the Supreme Court alleging arbitrary delays and politicised appraisal by Union inter-ministerial committees in releasing NDRF grants; similar debates surfaced over declaring Kerala's Wayanad landslides a "disaster of severe nature". Aiming to build predictable rules, the **16th Finance Commission (2026–31)** recommended a total SDRF/SDMF corpus of **₹2,04,401 crore** (Union share **₹1,55,916 crore**) while preserving the 90:10 and 75:25 sharing ratios.

#### 6. Synchronised Elections ("One Nation, One Election")

The pending **Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill, 2024** (referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee following the Ram Nath Kovind High-Level Committee Report of March 2024) proposes synchronising Lok Sabha and State Assembly polls via an **"unexpired term"** mechanism whenever an Assembly falls mid-term. While proponents highlight reduced governance paralysis and lower expenditure, federal critics argue that cutting short an incoming State Assembly's full five-year mandate to align with the Lok Sabha clock subordinates independent state democratic mandates to the national electoral cycle and could erode regional party space.

> [!news]
> **2025–2026 Federal Flashpoints Timeline for GS-2 Value Addition:**
> - **23 May 2025:** New Delhi Government withdrew its Supreme Court challenge against the GNCTD Amendment Act, 2023 (NCCSA).
> - **16 June 2025:** **Census of India 2027** formally notified (Houselisting Apr–Sep 2026; Enumeration Feb 2027), reopening the post-2026 delimitation debate; **25 July 2025** SC upheld seat-freeze embargo (*Prof. K. Purushottam Reddy*).

## 3. Commissions on Centre–State Relations

For the first seventeen years after the Constitution came into force (1950–1967), the Indian National Congress governed both New Delhi and virtually every state capital. Consequently, Centre–State friction was absorbed informally inside party forums rather than through constitutional confrontation. That political equilibrium shattered in the **Fourth General Elections of 1967**, when non-Congress coalitions captured power in nine states. Overnight, disputes erupted over partisan Governors, arbitrary dismissals under `Article 356`, central deployment of paramilitary forces, control over All-India Services, and financial dependency on the extra-constitutional Planning Commission—triggering six landmark institutional reviews and federal charters spanning more than four decades.

### Pre-Sarkaria Milestones (1966–1977)

Four early interventions—one Union commission and three state-level political charters—set the ideological terms of India's federal debate well before the Sarkaria Commission was constituted.

#### First Administrative Reforms Commission (1966–1969) and the Setalvad Study Team

Constituted by the Central Government in **January 1966** as a six-member commission under **Morarji Desai** (succeeded by **K. Hanumanthaiya** in 1967), the **First Administrative Reforms Commission (1st ARC)** appointed a specialised **Study Team on Centre–State Relationships** headed by former Attorney General **M.C. Setalvad** (which submitted its report in 1967). Drawing on Setalvad's analysis, the 1st ARC submitted its final Centre–State report in **June 1969** containing **22 concrete recommendations**.

Rejecting constitutional overhaul, the 1st ARC held that the existing constitutional text was sufficiently flexible to address federal tensions if healthy conventions were observed. Its principal recommendations included: **(1)** establishing a permanent **Inter-State Council** under `Article 263`; **(2)** appointing non-partisan persons possessing long public life and administrative stature as **Governors**; **(3)** delegating maximum administrative powers to States; **(4)** transferring larger untied financial resources to States to curtail fiscal dependency; and **(5)** affirming the Centre's power to deploy Central armed forces in States either on State request or *suo motu* when constitutional breakdowns loomed. Although the Union Government took no immediate structural action on these proposals, the 1st ARC laid the conceptual blueprint later adopted by the Sarkaria Commission.

#### Rajamannar Committee (1969–1971, Tamil Nadu)

In **September 1969**, the DMK Government of Tamil Nadu appointed a three-member **Centre–State Relations Inquiry Committee** chaired by former Madras High Court Chief Justice **Dr. P.V. Rajamannar** (alongside Dr. A. Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar and P. Chandra Reddy) to examine unitary bias and secure maximum state autonomy. Submitting its landmark report in **May 1971**, the committee traced growing centralisation to four causes: constitutional provisions favouring the Centre, prolonged single-party dominance, acute fiscal reliance on Union grants, and the overriding role of the Planning Commission.

Going far beyond administrative tweaks, the Rajamannar Committee proposed **eight radical structural changes**:
1. Constitute an **Inter-State Council** immediately and consult it on all parliamentary bills of national importance affecting States;
2. Make the **Finance Commission** a permanent constitutional body;
3. Disband the **Planning Commission** and replace it with an independent **statutory body**;
4. **Completely delete `Articles 356, 357, and 365`** (ending President's Rule and coercive Union directions);
5. Omit the provision stating that State Ministries hold office during the **pleasure of the Governor**;
6. Transfer specified entries from the **Union and Concurrent Lists to the State List**, and vest **residuary powers exclusively in the States**;
7. **Abolish All-India Services** (IAS, IPS, and IFoS); and
8. Reallocate taxation entries so States possess independent, buoyant revenue sources.

Unsurprisingly, the Union Government rejected the Rajamannar recommendations in toto; nevertheless, the report remains the most rigorous legal articulation of state-autonomy federalism in post-Independence India.

#### Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973) and West Bengal Memorandum (1977)

Regional dissatisfaction escalated sharply during the 1970s through two prominent political charters:

- **Anandpur Sahib Resolution (October 1973):** Adopted by the Working Committee of the **Shiromani Akali Dal** at Anandpur Sahib in Punjab (and reaffirmed in 1978), this charter demanded that the Union Government's jurisdiction be strictly confined to **four subjects**—**Defence, Foreign Affairs, Communications, and Currency**—with **all residuary powers vested in the States**. It further demanded that India's Constitution be recast as *"federal in the real sense"*, guaranteeing equal authority and representation for all constituent States at the Centre.
- **West Bengal Memorandum (December 1977):** Published by the CPI(M)-led Left Front Government of West Bengal under Chief Minister Jyoti Basu and circulated nationally, this memorandum called for sweeping constitutional revision: **(1)** replacing the word **"Union"** in the Constitution with **"Federal"**; **(2)** restricting the Centre to **Defence, Foreign Affairs, Currency, Communications, and Economic Coordination**, transferring all other subjects—including residuary powers—to States; **(3)** **deleting `Articles 356, 357, and 360`**; **(4)** making **State consent mandatory** before forming new states or altering state borders under `Article 3`; **(5)** devolving **75% of total Central tax revenue** to the States; **(6)** granting the **Rajya Sabha equal powers** with the Lok Sabha; and **(7)** **abolishing All-India Services**, retaining only separate Central and State civil services. The Union Government rejected the memorandum outright, yet mounting political pressure—particularly during the Punjab crisis and non-Congress state assertions—forced New Delhi to constitute a comprehensive national commission in 1983.

### The Sarkaria Commission (1983–1988)

Constituted by the Central Government in **June 1983**, the **Sarkaria Commission** remains the gold standard of Indian federal inquiry. Headed by retired Supreme Court Judge **Justice R.S. Sarkaria**—assisted by members **B. Sivaraman** (former Cabinet Secretary) and **Dr. S.R. Sen** (eminent economist)—the three-member body consulted State Governments, political parties, jurists, and economists extensively before submitting its monumental 1,600-page report in **October 1987** (officially released in **January 1988**).

#### Core Philosophy and Implementation Record

Rejecting radical decentralisation demands from Rajamannar and West Bengal, the Sarkaria Commission affirmed that a **strong Centre is indispensable** to safeguard India's unity, territorial integrity, and socio-economic development against fissiparous forces. At the same time, it drew a sharp distinction between strength and over-centralisation—warning memorably that excessive centralisation produces *"blood pressure at the Centre and anaemia at the periphery"*. Instead of rewriting the Constitution, Sarkaria advocated **functional decentralisation, cooperative institutions, and strict constitutional conventions**. Out of its **247 recommendations**, the Union Government has **implemented approximately 180 recommendations**—most notably establishing the permanent **Inter-State Council in May 1990** and enacting the **80th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2000** pooling Central taxes into a single divisible pool.

#### Landmark Recommendations of the Sarkaria Commission

1. **Permanent Inter-State Council (`Article 263`):** Establish a permanent **Inter-Governmental Council** (to distinguish it from narrow sectoral councils) with a dedicated secretariat for continuous Centre–State political consultation. *(Implemented via the Presidential Order of 28 May 1990.)*
2. **Sparing Use of `Article 356` (President's Rule):** Invoke `Article 356` strictly as a **"measure of last resort"** only after all constitutional alternatives fail; issue a prior warning to an errant State; require a **"speaking" Governor's report** setting out material facts; mandate a **floor test** on the Assembly floor rather than subjective Raj Bhavan headcounts; and **never dissolve the State Legislative Assembly before Parliament approves the proclamation**. *(Endorsed and elevated into binding law by the Supreme Court in **S.R. Bommai**, 1994.)*
3. **Appointment, Tenure and Role of the Governor:** Select an eminent person from **outside the State**, detached from active local politics (and avoid appointing an active politician of the ruling Central party to a State governed by another party); amend `Article 155` to make **consultation with the State Chief Minister mandatory**; protect the normal **5-year tenure** except for rare, compelling reasons; bar retired Governors from returning to active partisan politics; and follow a clear **four-step hierarchy in hung Assemblies** (pre-poll alliance → single largest party → post-poll coalition with partners joining government → post-poll coalition with outside support).
4. **Assent to Reserved State Bills (`Articles 200–201`):** Whenever the President withholds assent to a reserved State Bill, the Union Government should **communicate clear reasons** to the State Government rather than exercising silent veto power.
5. **Legislative, Concurrent and Residuary Powers:** Retain the **residuary power of taxation** exclusively with Parliament, but transfer **all other (non-tax) residuary powers to the Concurrent List**; establish a strict convention of **consulting States** before Parliament enacts laws on **Concurrent List** subjects; and retain the existing federal balance of the Rajya Sabha.
6. **All-India Services and Central Armed Forces:** **Do NOT abolish All-India Services**—instead strengthen existing services and create new ones to maintain national administrative cohesion. Similarly, retain the Centre's authority to deploy Central armed forces in States even without State consent during grave crises, while treating prior consultation with the State as highly desirable.
7. **Fiscal, Planning and Cultural Harmony:** Pool net proceeds of **corporation tax** and major Central taxes into a broader shareable pool *(realised via the 80th Amendment, 2000)*; restrict Central surcharges (`Art. 271`) strictly to brief emergencies; revamp the Planning Commission and National Development Council; activate **Zonal Councils**; enforce the **Three-Language Formula** uniformly across India; empower the **Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities**; and decentralise broadcasting operations without breaking national radio/TV cohesion.

*(Bridging Sarkaria and Punchhi, the **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution — NCRWC, 2000–2002**, chaired by former CJI **Justice M.N. Venkatachaliah**, endorsed Sarkaria's ISC framework, proposed appointing Governors via a committee including the State Chief Minister, and urged establishing a statutory **Inter-State Trade and Commerce Commission** under `Article 307`.)*

### The Punchhi Commission (2007–2010)

Nearly two decades after Sarkaria, India's polity had transformed profoundly: the **1991 economic liberalisation**, entrenched **multi-party coalition politics**, the **73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments (1992)** adding a third democratic tier, rising internal security threats (cross-border terrorism, Left-Wing Extremism, communal riots), and globalisation-era international treaties created challenges Sarkaria had never confronted. To examine these shifts afresh, the UPA Government constituted the **Second Commission on Centre–State Relations** on **27 April 2007** under former Chief Justice of India **Justice Madan Mohan Punchhi**, alongside members **Dhirendra Singh**, **Vinod Kumar Duggal**, **Prof. N.R. Madhava Menon**, and **Vijay Shanker** (appointed following the demise of fiscal economist Dr. Amaresh Bagchi in 2008).

In **March/April 2010**, the Commission submitted a massive **seven-volume, 1,456-page report** spanning constitutional governance, fiscal relations, local self-government, and internal security. Across its seven volumes, the Commission formulated **over 310 recommendations** (later consolidated by the Inter-State Council Secretariat, removing cross-volume overlaps, into **273 actionable recommendations** for examination by the ISC Standing Committee). Going decisively beyond Sarkaria's cautious reliance on unwritten conventions, Punchhi proposed **explicit constitutional and statutory safeguards**:

1. **Radical Overhaul of the Governor's Office (`Articles 155–156`):**
   - **Delete the "pleasure of the President" doctrine** (`Article 156(1)`) and guarantee a **fixed 5-year tenure** so Governors cannot be arbitrarily dismissed upon regime change in New Delhi;
   - Provide for removal of a Governor **only through impeachment by the State Legislature** (following a procedure akin to Presidential impeachment under `Article 61`);
   - Appoint Governors through a **bipartisan collegium** comprising the Prime Minister, Home Minister, Lok Sabha Speaker, and the **concerned State Chief Minister**;
   - **Abolish the statutory practice of burdening Governors as ex-officio Chancellors of State Universities**—confining them strictly to constitutional duties to eliminate Raj Bhavan–Cabinet clashes over Vice-Chancellors; and
   - Codify clear constitutional rules governing discretionary powers, sanction for prosecuting ministers, and hung Assemblies.
2. **Mandatory Six-Month Timeline for Bill Assent (`Articles 200–201`):** Prescribe a strict **six-month time limit** for Governors to decide whether to assent to or reserve a State Bill (`Art. 200`) and a parallel **six-month ceiling** for the President to decide on reserved State Bills (`Art. 201`)—directly anticipating the 2023–2025 gubernatorial deadlock litigated in *Special Reference No. 1 of 2025*.
3. **"Localised Emergency" (`Articles 355 & 356`):** Amend `Articles 355` and `356` to empower the Union to bring **only a specific troubled district or local area** under Central administration for a limited duration (up to **three months**) without dismissing the duly elected State Government or suspending the Assembly—paired with explicit constitutional codification of the *S.R. Bommai* (1994) safeguards.
4. **Concurrent List & International Treaty Consultation (`Article 253`):** Make **prior consultation with States through the Inter-State Council mandatory** before introducing Parliamentary Bills on **Concurrent List** subjects (adopting a *"broad agreement"* convention); and enact a dedicated **Parliamentary statute regulating `Article 253`** so that international treaties impacting State List domains (such as WTO agricultural accords or climate obligations) require structured consultation with State Governments before ratification.
5. **Empowering the Inter-State Council, National Integration Council & Rajya Sabha:** Transform the **Inter-State Council** into a permanent, continuing **auditing and dispute-management institution** backed by an autonomous secretariat; accord **statutory/constitutional stature** and a revitalised role to the **National Integration Council (NIC)** to tackle communal harmony and internal security; promote **equality of seats for States in the Rajya Sabha** to strengthen genuine federal representation; and require **Zonal Councils** to convene at least **twice every year**.
6. **Internal Security, All-India Services, Fiscal Discipline & Local Bodies:** Empower the Centre to deploy Central armed forces ***suo motu* for a brief period (up to one week)** in cases of acute communal conflagrations when state machinery falters; expand **All-India Services** into new specialised cadres (**Health, Education, Engineering, and Judiciary**); establish an **Inter-State Trade and Commerce Commission** under `Article 307`; cap non-shareable cesses/surcharges and trim tied Centrally Sponsored Schemes; and synchronise **State Finance Commissions** with the Union Finance Commission to empower Panchayats and Municipalities.

## 4. Inter-State Water Disputes (Article 262)

Supporting nearly eighteen per cent of humanity on barely four per cent of the world's freshwater resources, India is a hydrologically stressed federation in which almost every major river system—the Indus tributaries, Ganga–Yamuna, Brahmaputra, Narmada, Godavari, Krishna, Mahanadi, Cauvery, Mahadayi, and Pennaiyar—traverses multiple state boundaries. Because river waters directly govern agrarian survival, drinking-water security in burgeoning metropolises, hydropower generation, and ecological sustainability, inter-state river conflicts routinely escalate into India's sharpest horizontal federal disputes.

### Constitutional Scheme: Article 262 vs Seventh Schedule Entries

The Constitution splits water governance delicately across two Seventh Schedule entries and one specialised adjudicatory Article:

- **State List — `Entry 17`:** Grants State Legislatures competence over *"Water, that is to say, water supplies, irrigation and canals, drainage and embankments, water storage and water power **subject to the provisions of Entry 56 of List I**."*
- **Union List — `Entry 56`:** Empowers Parliament over *"Regulation and development of inter-State rivers and river valleys to the extent to which such regulation and development under the control of the Union is declared by Parliament by law to be expedient in the public interest."*

Recognising that adversarial litigation in ordinary civil courts is poorly equipped to handle dynamic monsoon hydrology, multi-basin engineering trade-offs, and emotionally charged sub-national politics, the framers inserted **`Article 262`** in Part XI containing **two sweeping provisions**:

1. **`Article 262(1)` — Parliamentary Power to Provide for Adjudication:** Parliament may **by law** provide for the adjudication of any dispute or complaint with respect to the use, distribution, or control of the waters of, or in, any inter-State river or river valley.
2. **`Article 262(2)` — Parliamentary Power to Bar Court Jurisdiction:** Notwithstanding anything in the Constitution, Parliament may **by law** provide that **neither the Supreme Court nor any other court** shall exercise jurisdiction in respect of any such dispute or complaint.

Crucially, `Article 262` does **not** itself bar the Supreme Court or establish a tribunal; rather, it **authorises Parliament by law** to establish the adjudicatory machinery and exclude judicial jurisdiction.

### Statutory Framework: River Boards Act 1956 vs ISRWD Act 1956

Exercising its twin powers under `Entry 56` of List I and `Article 262`, Parliament enacted two foundational statutes in 1956 whose practical trajectories diverged dramatically:

1. **The River Boards Act, 1956 (Enacted under Entry 56, List I — A Dead Letter):** Designed as a proactive, preventive mechanism, this Act empowered the Central Government to establish **advisory River Boards** for regulating and developing inter-state rivers and river valleys **on the request of the concerned State Governments**. **Crucial Prelims Fact:** **NOT A SINGLE River Board has ever been constituted** under the River Boards Act, 1956, because States resisted central involvement in Entry 17 irrigation planning. *(Separate ad hoc basin bodies—such as the Damodar Valley Corporation under a 1948 Act, Betwa River Board 1976, Brahmaputra Board 1980, and Narmada Control Authority—were set up outside the 1956 River Boards Act.)*
2. **The Inter-State River Water Disputes (ISRWD) Act, 1956 (Enacted under Article 262):** Serves as the reactive adjudicatory statute. When a State Government submits a formal complaint regarding an inter-state water dispute and the Central Government concludes that negotiations cannot settle it, the Centre constitutes an **ad hoc Water Disputes Tribunal** (chaired by a sitting or retired Supreme Court Judge nominated by the Chief Justice of India, plus two other Supreme Court or High Court Judges). Invoking `Article 262(2)`, **`Section 11`** of the Act explicitly **bars the Supreme Court (including its `Article 131` original jurisdiction) and all other courts** from exercising jurisdiction over any water dispute referable to a tribunal.

#### The 2002 Amendment (Implementing Sarkaria Commission Reforms)

Because early tribunals suffered indefinite delays, Parliament enacted the **Inter-State River Water Disputes (Amendment) Act, 2002** based on the **Sarkaria Commission's** recommendations, introducing strict statutory timelines and teeth:
- **1-Year Setup Deadline:** Once a State formally requests adjudication and negotiations fail, the Central Government must constitute a Water Disputes Tribunal within **one year**.
- **3 + 2 Year Adjudication Cap:** The Tribunal must submit its report and decision within **three years**, extendable by the Central Government for unavoidable reasons by a maximum of **two additional years** (total **five years**; plus a 1-year window for clarification/supplementary reports).
- **Force of a Supreme Court Decree (`Section 6(2)`):** Once the Central Government publishes the Tribunal's final decision in the Official Gazette, the award becomes **final and binding** on all party States and carries the **same legal force as an order or decree of the Supreme Court**.

### Supreme Court's Article 136 Jurisdiction Despite the Article 262 Bar

A classic constitutional puzzle tested in UPSC Prelims and Mains asks: if `Article 262(2)` and `Section 11` of the ISRWD Act expressly bar *"neither the Supreme Court nor any other court"* from exercising jurisdiction over inter-state river water disputes, how did the Supreme Court entertain appeals against the Cauvery Tribunal award and alter water allocations in 2018? The landmark answer was settled in the Cauvery judgment.

> [!case]
> ***State of Karnataka v. State of Tamil Nadu*** (**16 February 2018** — 3-Judge Bench: CJI Dipak Misra, Justices Amitava Roy & A.M. Khanwilkar — **Cauvery Award Judgment**):
> - **`Article 131` Barred, `Article 136` Survives:** The Court drew a decisive boundary between its **exclusive original jurisdiction under `Article 131`** (which stands **strictly excluded** by `Article 262(2)` read with Section 11 of the ISRWD Act—so a state cannot file an original water-sharing suit directly in the Supreme Court) and its **extraordinary appellate Special Leave Petition (SLP) jurisdiction under `Article 136`**, which remains **unbarred**. Even though Section 6(2) clothes a notified tribunal award with the *force* of a Supreme Court decree for execution, the tribunal does not become the Supreme Court; it remains a statutory tribunal amenable to `Article 136` scrutiny on legal, jurisdictional, or equitable grounds.
> - **Rivers Are "National Assets" (Rejection of Harmon Doctrine):** Rejecting any upper-riparian claim of absolute territorial sovereignty (the archaic *Harmon Doctrine*), the Bench declared that **no single state can claim exclusive ownership** over an inter-state river; waters of an inter-state river are **national assets** governed by the international principle of **equitable apportionment** (Helsinki Rules, 1966 & Berlin Rules, 2004).
> - **Substantive Modification & Enforcement:** Accounting for Bengaluru's acute global-city drinking-water demand and Tamil Nadu's accessible groundwater reserves, the Court raised **Karnataka's share by 14.75 TMC** (reducing Tamil Nadu's annual release at Biligundlu from 192 TMC to **177.25 TMC**) and directed the Union to frame a binding **Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA)** and **Cauvery Water Regulation Committee (CWRC)** under Section 6A of the Act.
> - **Mandamus to Constitute Tribunals:** Separately, the Court has consistently affirmed that `Article 262` does not prevent it from issuing a judicial direction compelling the Central Government to perform its statutory duty under the ISRWD Act when negotiations fail—exercised to trigger the **Mahanadi Tribunal (2018)** and the **Pennaiyar Tribunal (2 February 2026)**.

### Structural Bottlenecks, ISRWD (Amendment) Bill and the Entry 17 vs Entry 56 Debate

Despite the 2002 Amendment's five-year statutory cap, India's tribunal system remains mired in systemic gridlock: the **Ravi–Beas Tribunal** has dragged on for **40 years**, **Krishna–II** for **22 years**, and **Vansadhara** and **Mahanadi** remain unfinalised or unnotified. Five institutional bottlenecks drive this paralysis: **(1)** ad hoc tribunals take years to constitute, staff, and reconstitute whenever judge-members retire; **(2)** India lacks an independent, real-time, basin-wide **hydrological data bank**, causing states to weaponise conflicting flow statistics; **(3)** endless `Article 136` SLPs and delayed Central gazette notifications block finality; **(4)** awards allocate fixed water volumes assuming static historical rainfall rather than climate-change-induced variability and ecological flows; and **(5)** populist sub-nationalism makes political compromise electorally costly for Chief Ministers.

#### The Inter-State River Water Disputes (Amendment) Bill, 2019 (Proposals & Status)

To replace fragmented ad hoc tribunals with a modern institutional architecture, the Union Government introduced the **ISRWD (Amendment) Bill, 2019**, anchored on **four structural pillars**:
1. **Mandatory Pre-Tribunal Dispute Resolution Committee (DRC):** Before any dispute reaches a tribunal, an expert **DRC** (comprising water-sector professionals, not politicians) must attempt negotiated resolution within **one year** (extendable by **six months**).
2. **Single Permanent Standing Tribunal:** Dissolve all existing ad hoc tribunals and replace them with **one permanent Inter-State River Water Disputes Tribunal** chaired by a retired Supreme Court Judge (assisted by Vice-Chairpersons and Judicial/Expert Members), assigning disputes to dedicated temporary **Benches** that wind up immediately upon issuing an award—capping total adjudication strictly at **4.5 years** (2 years + 1-year extension + 1.5 years for clarifications).
3. **Automatic Binding Finality:** Eliminate the requirement of separate Central Gazette notification so that the Tribunal Bench's decision becomes **automatically final and binding** upon pronouncement—removing political veto delays in New Delhi.
4. **Transparent National Hydrological Data Bank:** Mandate an authorised central agency to maintain a unified, transparent national data bank and information system for every river basin.

**Verified Legislative Status:** Although the **Lok Sabha passed the Bill on 31 July 2019**, it remained pending in the Rajya Sabha and **lapsed in June 2024** upon the dissolution of the 17th Lok Sabha under **`Article 107(5)`**. As of **November 2026**, the reform has not yet been re-introduced and enacted into law.

#### Should Water Shift from State List (Entry 17) to the Concurrent List?

Faced with recurring river battles, groundwater over-extraction, and climate-driven droughts, several expert panels—including the **Mihir Shah Committee (2016)** (which also recommended replacing CWC and CGWB with a unified **National Water Commission**) and Parliamentary Standing Committees on Water Resources—have debated moving "Water" from **Entry 17 (State List)** to the **Concurrent List** (or enacting an overarching **National Water Framework Law** under Entry 56 / `Article 252`).

Proponents contend that river basins are hydrological units indifferent to political borders, requiring integrated surface–groundwater management, ecological minimum flows, and unified drought planning across upstream and downstream states. States, however, fiercely oppose transferring Entry 17 to the Concurrent List—warning that water is inseparable from **agriculture (`Entry 14`)** and **land (`Entry 18`)**, and that centralisation would erode the federal compact under *S.R. Bommai*. The balanced **Mains way forward** avoids a divisive constitutional amendment and focuses instead on **four cooperative fixes**: **(1)** re-enacting the **Single Permanent Tribunal + DRC + Open Hydrological Data Bank** reform; **(2)** enacting a consensual **River Basin Management Act** under `Entry 56` creating multi-state **River Basin Organisations (RBOs)** with equal state representation; **(3)** shifting water policy from supply-side concrete dams to **demand-side management** (micro-irrigation, drought-resilient crop diversification away from water-guzzling paddy/sugarcane in water-scarce basins); and **(4)** building dynamic **climate-indexed sharing formulas** for distress monsoon years.

## 5. Inter-State Council, Full Faith & Credit, Zonal Councils & Trade

Cooperative federalism cannot survive on vertical bargains between New Delhi and state capitals alone; a continental linguistic federation demands equally robust **horizontal institutions** to coordinate policy, enforce legal acts across borders, resolve regional disputes, and preserve an unbroken national market. To sustain inter-state harmony without eroding state autonomy, India combines **constitutional mechanisms**—the Inter-State Council (`Article 263`), Full Faith and Credit (`Article 261`), and Freedom of Inter-State Trade (`Part XIII, Articles 301–307`)—with **statutory regional bodies**: the five **Zonal Councils (1956)** and the **North-Eastern Council (1971)**. Distinguishing clearly between constitutional, statutory, and extra-constitutional forums is one of UPSC Prelims' most recurring tests.

### The Inter-State Council (Article 263)

Placed in Part XI alongside river water adjudication, **`Article 263`** empowers the **President**—at any time it appears that the **public interests** would be served thereby—to establish an **Inter-State Council (ISC)** and to define the nature of its duties, organisation, and procedure. Unlike the Finance Commission (`Article 280`), which is mandatory every five years, `Article 263` is an **enabling constitutional provision** left to Presidential invocation.

#### Three Constitutional Duties and Advisory Character

`Article 263` charges the Inter-State Council with **three distinct duties**:
- **`Article 263(a)` — Dispute Inquiry:** Inquiring into and advising upon disputes which may have arisen between States;
- **`Article 263(b)` — Common Subjects:** Investigating and discussing subjects in which some or all of the States, or the Union and one or more of the States, have a common interest; and
- **`Article 263(c)` — Policy Coordination:** Making recommendations upon any such subject and, in particular, recommendations for the **better coordination of policy and action** with respect to that subject.

A vital constitutional distinction separates `Article 263(a)` from the Supreme Court's exclusive original jurisdiction under **`Article 131`**. Whereas the Supreme Court adjudicates strictly **legal disputes** and delivers **binding judicial decrees**, the Inter-State Council **complements** the Court by examining **both legal and non-legal (political, administrative, or policy) disputes**—yet its findings and recommendations are **strictly advisory and NOT legally binding** on either the Centre or the States.

#### Establishment (1990), Composition and Standing Committee

Before 1990, the President invoked `Article 263` solely to create narrow, sectoral councils—the Central Council of Health (1952), Central Council of Local Government (1954; later Urban Development), and four Regional Councils for Sales Tax (1968). Following the **Sarkaria Commission's (1988)** strong recommendation (which had proposed naming the body the **"Inter-Governmental Council"** to signal its comprehensive federal stature), the **Janata Dal Government** led by Prime Minister **V.P. Singh** established the permanent **Inter-State Council** by a **Presidential Order dated 28 May 1990**.

Its composition brings together the entire political executive of the Union and its constituent units:
- **Chairman:** The **Prime Minister of India**.
- **Members:**
  1. **Chief Ministers of all States**;
  2. **Chief Ministers of Union Territories having Legislative Assemblies** (NCT of Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu & Kashmir);
  3. **Administrators / Lieutenant Governors of Union Territories** not having Legislative Assemblies;
  4. **Governors of States under President's Rule** (`Article 356`); and
  5. **Union Cabinet Ministers** (including the Union Home Minister) nominated by the Prime Minister—originally **six Cabinet Ministers** under the 1990 Presidential Order, expanded to **nine Union Cabinet Ministers** (with additional Union Ministers designated as **permanent invitees**) under the reconstitution notified on **8 November 2024**.

To ensure continuous intergovernmental work between plenary sessions, a **Standing Committee of the Inter-State Council** was established in **1996** (and reconstituted alongside the Council in **November 2024**). Crucially for Prelims, while the **Prime Minister** chairs the full Council, the **Union Home Minister** chairs the **Standing Committee** (assisted by Union Cabinet Ministers and Chief Ministers across regions). Both bodies are supported by the **Inter-State Council Secretariat (ISCS)**, established in **1991** under a Secretary to the Government of India in the Ministry of Home Affairs; since **2011**, the ISCS also serves as the common secretariat for the **five Zonal Councils**.

#### Chronic Dormancy Critique

Despite holding the highest constitutional pedigree for Centre–State consensus, the Inter-State Council suffers from severe institutional under-utilisation. Although its procedural rules envisage **three meetings every year** (in camera, deciding by consensus), the full Council has convened **only 11 times since 1990**—holding its inaugural meeting on **10 October 1990** and its **11th meeting on 16 July 2016**. As of **November 2026**, **more than ten years have elapsed without a full Inter-State Council sitting**, even after its November 2024 reconstitution! *(Its Standing Committee has convened somewhat more actively—for instance, holding its 12th meeting in November 2017 and 13th meeting in May 2018 to process volumes of the Punchhi Commission Report.)* Overshadowed in practice by the **NITI Aayog Governing Council**, the **GST Council**, and ad hoc Chief Ministers' conferences, the ISC represents an untapped constitutional bridge urgently needing revival—with mandatory annual meetings and an independent secretariat outside the Home Ministry—before sensitive decisions on post-2026 delimitation and fiscal federalism are taken.

### Public Acts, Records and Judicial Proceedings (Article 261 — Full Faith and Credit)

Because every State Legislature and State Executive operates within its own territorial boundary, an uncoordinated federation could descend into legal chaos if one State refused to recognise the statutes, birth/marriage certificates, property deeds, or court decrees of another State. Modelled on **Article IV, Section 1 of the US Constitution** and **Section 118 of the Australian Constitution**, **`Article 261`**—the **"Full Faith and Credit" Clause**—cements nationwide legal unity across three crisp clauses:

1. **`Article 261(1)` — Nationwide Recognition:** Full faith and credit shall be given throughout the territory of India to **public acts** (both legislative enactments and executive acts of government), **records** (official books, registers, revenue records, and public documents), and **judicial proceedings** of the Union and of every State.
2. **`Article 261(2)` — Parliamentary Mode of Proof:** The manner in which and the conditions under which such acts, records, and proceedings shall be proved, and the effect thereof, shall be as provided by **law made by Parliament** (e.g., evidentiary rules under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 / formerly Indian Evidence Act, 1872).
3. **`Article 261(3)` — Execution of Civil Judgments Only:** Final judgments or orders delivered or passed by **civil courts** in any part of the territory of India shall be **capable of execution anywhere within that territory** according to law.

> [!remember]
> **Critical Exclusion (`Article 261(3)`):** The direct nationwide execution mandate under `Article 261(3)` applies **strictly to CIVIL judgments and orders**—it **does NOT apply to criminal or penal judgments**! Courts of one State do not directly execute the penal laws or criminal sentences of another State under `Article 261` (inter-state criminal warrants, transfers, and prisoner execution are governed separately by statutory criminal procedure laws such as the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023).

### Zonal Councils and the North-Eastern Council

Whereas the Inter-State Council is constitutional yet dormant, India's regional **Zonal Councils** and the **North-Eastern Council** are **statutory bodies** that have evolved into the republic's most vigorous platforms of horizontal cooperation.

#### Five Zonal Councils (States Reorganisation Act, 1956)

A perennial UPSC Prelims trap is assuming Zonal Councils are constitutional bodies. In reality, **Zonal Councils are STATUTORY bodies—NOT constitutional bodies**. They were established by **Part III (Sections 15–22) of the States Reorganisation Act, 1956**, inspired by **Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru's** December 1955 vision during Parliament's debates on linguistic reorganisation: fearing that linguistic states might breed narrow regionalism, Nehru proposed grouping contiguous states into advisory zones to arrest linguistic bitterness and cultivate a *"habit of cooperative working"*.

When demarcating the five zones, Parliament weighed natural divisions, river systems, communication networks, cultural and linguistic affinity, and requirements of economic development and national security.

| Council & Statutory Basis | Headquarters | Exact Member States and Union Territories |
|---|---|---|
| **Northern Zonal Council** *(SRA, 1956)* | **New Delhi** | **4 States:** Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Punjab, Rajasthan; **4 UTs:** Chandigarh, NCT of Delhi, Jammu & Kashmir, Ladakh |
| **Central Zonal Council** *(SRA, 1956)* | **Prayagraj** | **4 States:** Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Uttar Pradesh |
| **Eastern Zonal Council** *(SRA, 1956)* | **Kolkata** | **4 States:** Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha, West Bengal *(Trap: **Sikkim is NOT a member**—it sits in the NEC!)* |
| **Western Zonal Council** *(SRA, 1956)* | **Mumbai** | **3 States:** Goa, Gujarat, Maharashtra; **1 UT:** Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu |
| **Southern Zonal Council** *(SRA, 1956)* | **Chennai** | **5 States:** Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Telangana; **1 UT:** Puducherry *(**Andaman & Nicobar Islands** and **Lakshadweep** are not formal members, but participate as **special invitees**)* |
| **North-Eastern Council (NEC)** *(Separate **NEC Act, 1971**)* | **Shillong** | **8 States:** Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Tripura + **Sikkim** *(added as 8th state by **NEC Amendment Act, 2002**)* |

**Composition, Leadership and Functions of the Five Zonal Councils:**
- **Common Chairman:** The **Union Home Minister** serves as the ex-officio Chairman of all five Zonal Councils.
- **Rotating Vice-Chairman:** The **Chief Ministers** of the member States in each zone act as **Vice-Chairman by rotation**, holding office for **one year** at a time.
- **Other Members:** Two other Ministers from each member State (nominated by the Governor) and two members from each Union Territory included in the zone.
- **Non-Voting Advisers:** Each Council is assisted by non-voting advisers: one person nominated by **NITI Aayog** (originally Planning Commission), the **Chief Secretary** of each member State, and the **Development Commissioner** (or another nominated officer) of each member State. Additionally, each zone maintains a **Standing Committee of Chief Secretaries** to sift technical issues prior to ministerial meetings.
- **Role and Operational Surge (2014–2026):** Purely **deliberative and advisory**, Zonal Councils address economic and social planning, inter-state border disputes, safeguards for linguistic minorities, inter-state transport, power grids, river/canal coordination, Fast Track Special Courts, nutrition, cybercrime, and narcotics control. Far from remaining paper bodies, they have become India's busiest horizontal federal forum: PIB data cited at the **28th Western Zonal Council (Panaji, 7 September 2026)** revealed that Zonal Council and Standing Committee meetings nearly tripled from **25 (2004–2014)** to **71 (2014 – September 2026)**, examining **1,893 issues** and resolving **1,445 (~76%)**—extending their mandate from dispute resolution into active welfare-scheme monitoring.

#### The North-Eastern Council (NEC — NEC Act, 1971)

Because the North-East confronted distinct border-security, connectivity, and developmental needs, Parliament did not place it inside the 1956 Zonal Councils framework. Instead, it created the **North-Eastern Council (NEC)** under a **separate statute—the North-Eastern Council Act, 1971** (operational from August 1972; headquarters at **Shillong**). Originally encompassing the "Seven Sisters" (Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, and Tripura), the **NEC (Amendment) Act, 2002** inducted **Sikkim** as its **eighth member state** and formally designated the NEC as a **statutory regional planning body** for the North-Eastern region.

Three distinctive features separate the NEC sharply from the five Zonal Councils:
1. **Dual State Representation (Governors AND Chief Ministers):** Unlike the five Zonal Councils (where Governors are *not* members), the NEC includes **BOTH the Governors and the Chief Ministers** of all **eight** member states, alongside **three members nominated by the President**!
2. **Leadership Structure (2018 Reorganisation):** Since **June 2018**, the **Union Home Minister** serves as ex-officio **Chairman** of the NEC, while the **Union Minister for Development of North Eastern Region (DoNER)** serves as ex-officio **Vice-Chairman** (replacing the earlier arrangement where DoNER chaired the Council).
3. **Unified Regional Planning & Security Review Mandate:** Beyond normal advisory tasks, the NEC formulates **unified and coordinated regional plans** for common-interest infrastructure (highways, rail, power, digital connectivity, Act East commerce) and **periodically reviews measures taken by member states to maintain security and public order** across the sensitive frontier. Its **73rd Plenary Session (Shillong, 4 June 2026)** focused on High-Level Task Force blueprints boosting regional connectivity, investment, and trade linkages with South-East Asia.

#### Inter-State Border Disputes: Cooperative Breakthroughs and Pending Flashpoints

Colonial administrative lines and post-1956 linguistic reorganisation left persistent inter-state territorial disputes that Zonal Councils, NEC, and bilateral ministerial dialogues actively mediate:

- **North-East Border Accords:** Carving Meghalaya, Nagaland, Mizoram, and Arunachal Pradesh out of undivided Assam spawned overlapping boundary claims. Recent bilateral mediation mediated by the Union Home Ministry delivered historic breakthroughs: **Assam and Meghalaya** signed an **MoU on 29 March 2022** settling **six of 12 disputed sectors** (talks continuing on the remaining six); **Assam and Arunachal Pradesh** followed the **Namsai Declaration (July 2022)** with an **April 2023 MoU** covering 123 disputed villages, currently undergoing Survey of India ground demarcation. Conversely, **Assam–Mizoram** remains in ministerial talks (after 2021 clashes), and **Assam–Nagaland** remains pending before the Supreme Court.
- **Maharashtra–Karnataka (Belagavi Dispute):** Claiming Marathi-speaking Belagavi, Karwar, and Nipani placed in Karnataka by the 1956 Act, Maharashtra sought central intervention; the **Mahajan Commission (1966–67)** awarded Belagavi to Karnataka and 264 villages to Maharashtra—accepted by Karnataka but rejected by Maharashtra, whose **2004 `Article 131` suit** remains pending in the Supreme Court.
- **Punjab–Haryana (Chandigarh):** Planned as a shared Union Territory capital in 1966 and promised to Punjab under the unimplemented **Rajiv–Longowal Accord (1985)**, Chandigarh remains intertwined with the Ravi–Beas/SYL impasse.

> [!example]
> **Institutional Asymmetry in Indian Horizontal Federalism:**
> A striking paradox defines India's inter-state machinery in 2026: the **constitutional apex forum—the Inter-State Council (`Art. 263`)—has not held a single full sitting since July 2016**, whereas the **statutory Zonal Councils (1956 Act)** convened **71 meetings** between 2014 and September 2026, resolving nearly **three-fourths (1,445 of 1,893)** of issues raised, alongside facilitating historic bilateral border agreements in the North-East (Assam–Meghalaya 2022 and Assam–Arunachal 2023). Revitalising the constitutional ISC alongside Zonal Councils would bridge high-politics macro-disputes (delimitation, fiscal devolution) with practical regional administration.

### Inter-State Trade, Commerce and Intercourse (Part XIII, Articles 301–307)

Before 1950, hundreds of princely states and British provinces erected internal customs barriers, transit tolls, and protectionist duties that choked India's domestic economy. Inspired by **Section 92 of the Australian Constitution**, the framers inserted **Part XIII (`Articles 301` to `307`)** to eliminate internal economic frontiers and guarantee that India functions as a **single, unified national market**.

#### Constitutional Architecture: Articles 301 to 307

| Article | Core Constitutional Provision | Key Prelims Nuance / Exception |
|---|---|---|
| **`Art. 301`** | Trade, commerce, and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be **free** (subject to other provisions of Part XIII). | Covers **BOTH inter-state AND intra-state** trade and movement; protects commercial movement against barriers, not immunity from valid regulation or non-discriminatory taxation. |
| **`Art. 302`** | **Parliament** may by law impose restrictions on trade, commerce, or intercourse in the **public interest**. | Note: `Art. 302` says *"restrictions"* (does **not** expressly add the word *"reasonable"*, unlike State power under `Art. 304(b)`!). |
| **`Art. 303`** | **`303(1)`:** Neither Parliament nor States may give **preference** or **discriminate** between States on trade/commerce entries; **`303(2)`:** Exception for **scarcity of goods**. | **ONLY Parliament** (NEVER a State Legislature!) may pass preferential or discriminatory laws to deal with a **scarcity of goods** in any part of India. |
| **`Art. 304`** | **`304(a)`:** State may levy **non-discriminatory taxes** on goods imported from other States matching taxes on local goods; **`304(b)`:** State may impose **reasonable restrictions** in the **public interest**. | **Classic Trap (`Art. 304(b)` Proviso):** No Bill or amendment under `Art. 304(b)` shall be **introduced or moved** in a State Legislature **without the PREVIOUS SANCTION OF THE PRESIDENT**! *(Lack of prior sanction is cured under `Art. 255` if the President later assents.)* |
| **`Art. 305`** | Saves existing pre-1950 laws and laws creating **State Monopolies** (nationalisation of trade, business, industry, or service). | Amended by the **4th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1955** to insulate nationalisation (e.g., state road transport monopolies) from `Art. 301` challenges. |
| **`Art. 306`** | *Repealed* by the **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956**. | Originally permitted Part B princely states to retain transitional internal customs duties up to 10 years. |
| **`Art. 307`** | Empowers **Parliament by law** to appoint an **authority** to carry out the purposes of `Articles 301–304`. | **NEVER appointed so far** (despite repeated proposals by the **NCRWC 2002** and **Punchhi Commission 2010** for an Inter-State Trade and Commerce Commission). |

#### From Atiabari (1961) to Jindal Stainless (2016) and the GST Common Market

For over five decades, Part XIII litigation revolved around whether state taxes—particularly **Entry Taxes** levied on goods entering a local area under erstwhile `Entry 52` of List II—violated `Article 301`. In ***Atiabari Tea Co. Ltd. v. State of Assam* (1961, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court held that taxing statutes directly and immediately impeding the free movement of trade infringed `Article 301` unless cleared under `Article 304(b)`. A year later, in ***Automobile Transport (Rajasthan) Ltd. v. State of Rajasthan* (1962, 7-Judge Bench)**, the Court softened *Atiabari* by inventing the **"Compensatory Tax Doctrine"**—ruling that regulatory measures and *compensatory taxes* (levied to finance trade-facilitating infrastructure like roads and bridges) aided rather than hindered commerce and thus stood outside `Article 301`. Because proving whether every rupee of entry tax directly "compensated" traders produced decades of chaotic litigation across High Courts, the issue reached a historic **9-Judge Constitution Bench** in 2016.

> [!case]
> ***Jindal Stainless Ltd. v. State of Haryana*** (**11 November 2016** — **9-Judge Constitution Bench, 7–2 Majority**, authored by CJI T.S. Thakur; Justices D.Y. Chandrachud & Ashok Bhushan dissenting):
> - **Overruled the "Compensatory Tax Doctrine":** The majority expressly **overruled** the 54-year-old *Atiabari* (1961) and *Automobile Transport* (1962) compensatory tax doctrine, declaring it a judicial invention with **no textual foundation** in Part XIII.
> - **Non-Discriminatory Taxes Do NOT Violate `Article 301`:** Held that taxation is an inherent sovereign power of States under the Seventh Schedule—not a "restriction" on free trade. Only **discriminatory taxes** (that tax out-of-state goods more heavily than locally manufactured goods, breaching **`Article 304(a)`**) offend `Article 301`.
> - **`Article 304(a)` and `304(b)` Are Independent:** Crucially held that States may levy non-discriminatory Entry Taxes **without requiring prior Presidential sanction under `Article 304(b)`** (since `304(a)` governs fiscal parity while `304(b)` governs non-fiscal restrictions on trade movement).
> - **Post-2017 Economic Integration:** Even as *Jindal Stainless* vindicated States' constitutional taxing sovereignty, Parliament enacted the **101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016** (omitting Entry 52, List II) and launched **GST (1 July 2017)**, dismantling state border check-posts, cascading octroi, and entry taxes nationwide to forge **"One Nation, One Market"** via the GST Council (`Art. 279A`). Today, Part XIII's frontier has shifted toward **non-fiscal internal barriers**—such as protectionist state **domicile quotas in private-sector employment** (e.g., Haryana's 75% local-jobs statute struck down by the Punjab & Haryana High Court in November 2023) and interstate movement curbs on agricultural commodities.

> [!prelims]
> **Part XIII, Article 261 & Inter-State Bodies — Master Prelims Checklist:**
> - **Constitutional vs Statutory Trap:** **ISC (`Art. 263`)** = **Constitutional** (created by **President**, 1990; PM Chair, Home Minister Standing Committee Chair). **5 Zonal Councils (1956 Act)** & **NEC (1971 Act)** = **Statutory** (Home Minister Chair of all!). **NITI Aayog (2015)** = **Extra-constitutional** (PM Chair).
> - **Sikkim & Islands Trap:** **Sikkim** is a member of the **NEC (since 2002)**, **NOT** the Eastern Zonal Council. **A&N Islands and Lakshadweep** are **special invitees** (not formal members) to the Southern Zonal Council. **NEC** uniquely includes **both Governors AND CMs**.
> - **`Article 261(3)` Trap:** Nationwide execution applies **ONLY to civil court decrees**, **NEVER** to criminal/penal sentences.
> - **Part XIII (`Arts. 301–307`) Traps:** Borrowed from **Australia (s. 92)**; `Art. 301` covers **inter-state AND intra-state** trade; **ONLY Parliament** may discriminate during **scarcity of goods (`Art. 303(2)`)**; `Art. 304(b)` State restriction Bills need **prior Presidential sanction** before introduction (curable via assent under `Art. 255`); **`Art. 307` authority has NEVER been constituted**.

## 1. Nature of Indian Federalism

- **Unitary vs Federal & Stepan's Typology:** Unitary states (UK, France, Japan) centralise sovereignty; federal states (USA, Canada, Australia, India) divide powers constitutionally. **Alfred Stepan** distinguishes **"coming-together"** federations (USA, Switzerland, Australia — symmetric, indestructible states, state residuary) from **"holding-together"** federations (India, Canada, Belgium, Spain — devolved from unitary base, strong Centre, asymmetric).
- **Canadian Borrowings (4):** Strong-Centre federation; **Union residuary powers (`Art. 248`)**; **centrally appointed Governors (`Art. 155`)**; SC advisory jurisdiction (`Art. 143`). *(Concurrent List & `Art. 301` trade = **Australia**.)*
- **`Art. 1` ("Union of States"):** Word **"federation"** appears **NOWHERE** in the Constitution. **Dr. Ambedkar (4 Nov 1948)** gave **2 reasons**: (1) NOT an agreement among States; (2) **no right to secede** — indestructible Union. With **`Art. 3`** (simple-majority boundary changes), India is an **"indestructible Union of destructible States"** (contrast US *Texas v. White*, 1869). Ambedkar praised **elasticity**: federal normally, unitary in emergencies without formal amendment.
- **"Union of India" vs "Territory of India":** **Union of India** = **28 States** only; **Territory of India (`Art. 1(3)`)** = **28 States + 8 UTs + acquired territories**.
- **7 Federal Features:** Dual Polity (+ 73rd/74th 3rd tier); Written Constitution; Division of Powers (`Art. 246`, 7th Schedule: Union 97→98 active, State 66→59 active, Concurrent 47→52; GST `Art. 246A`); Constitutional Supremacy; Rigidity (`Art. 368(2)` proviso: special majority + **half States**); Independent Judiciary (`Art. 131`); Bicameralism (**Rajya Sabha**, exclusive `Arts. 249, 312`).
- **15+ Unitary Features:** Strong Centre & Union residuary (`Art. 248`); destructible States (`Art. 3`); Single Constitution; Flexibility & Parliament-only amendment initiation; Unequal RS seats (**4th Schedule**: 1–31); Emergencies (`Arts. 352, 356, 360`); Single Citizenship; Integrated Judiciary; All-India Services (`Art. 312`: IAS, IPS, IFoS 1966); Integrated Audit (**CAG**, `Art. 148`); 5 Parliamentary State List gateways (`Arts. 249, 250, 252, 253, 356`); Central Governors (`Arts. 155–156`); Integrated **ECI (`Art. 324`)**; Absolute Presidential veto over reserved Bills (`Arts. 200–201`) & `Art. 304(b)` prior sanction; Fiscal dependence (`Part XII`, `Art. 293(3)`) & `Arts. 256/257/365`; Central HC appointments & President-only SPSC removals (`Art. 317`); CAPF deployment (`Art. 355`, Entry 2A List I).
- **Scholar–Label Pairs:** **K.C. Wheare** (*quasi-federal*); **Granville Austin** (*cooperative federalism*); **Morris-Jones** (*bargaining federalism*); **Ivor Jennings** (*federation with strong centralising tendency*); **Paul Appleby** (***extremely federal*** — Centre depends on State field administration!); **Alexandrowicz** (*sui generis*); **K. Santhanam** (fiscal dominance & Planning Commission).
- **Judicial Arc:** ***West Bengal v. UoI* (1962)** (5–1 centralist; Subba Rao dissent) → ***State of Rajasthan* (1977)** (*amphibian*) → ***S.R. Bommai* (1994, 9-Judge)** (**federalism = Basic Structure**; States NOT appendages of Centre) → ***Kuldip Nayar* (2006)** (RS domicile ended) → ***NCT of Delhi* (2018/2023)** (**collaborative/pragmatic federalism**) → ***In Re: Art. 370* (11 Dec 2023)** (asymmetric federalism endures) → ***MADA v. SAIL* (Jul 2024)** & ***Lalta Prasad Vaish* (Oct 2024)** (9-judge 8:1 protection of List II Entries 49/50 & 8) → ***Special Reference No. 1 of 2025* (20 Nov 2025, `2025 INSC 1333`)** (no rigid timelines / Art. 142 deemed assent, yet no indefinite Governor pocket veto).

## 4. Inter-State Water Disputes (Article 262)

- **Constitutional Split:** **Water = State List (`Entry 17`)** subject to **Union List (`Entry 56`)** (inter-state rivers). **`Art. 262`** empowers **Parliament by law** to: **(1)** adjudicate river disputes, and **(2)** **bar SC and all courts**.
- **Two 1956 Acts:** **River Boards Act, 1956** (advisory, state request — **0 boards ever set up!**); **ISRWD Act, 1956** (`Sec. 11` bars courts; **2002 Amdt**: **1-yr** setup, **3 + 2 yrs** award, notified award = **SC decree** under `Sec. 6(2)`).
- **9 Tribunals + 10th Ordered (1969–2026):** **Krishna-I** (1969, Bachawat; 1976 award 2,060 TMC); **Godavari** (1969; 1980 award; Polavaram–Banakacherla); **Narmada** (1969; 1979 award; NCA); **Ravi–Beas** (1986, Eradi; **40 yrs old**; SYL Canal; Punjab 2004 Act voided 2016); **Cauvery** (1990; 2007 award → **2018 SC verdict** + **CWMA**; Mekedatu); **Krishna-II** (2004, Brijesh Kumar; **22 yrs old**, unnotified; Oct 2023 AP–TS ToR); **Vansadhara** (2010; AP–OD; pending gazette); **Mahadayi/Mandovi** (2010; GA–KA–MH; notified 2020; **PRAWAH 2023**; Kalasa–Banduri); **Mahanadi** (2018; OD–CG; ongoing); **Pennaiyar** (TN–KA–PY; **SC 2 Feb 2026** ordered tribunal). *(Non-tribunal: **Mullaperiyar** KL–TN 1886 lease; **UYRB** 1995.)*
- ***Karnataka v. Tamil Nadu* (16 Feb 2018):** `Art. 262(2)` bars **`Art. 131`**, NOT **`Art. 136` SLPs** (or mandamus for tribunal setup). Rivers = **"national assets"** (no Harmon Doctrine; equitable apportionment); KA +14.75 TMC (Bengaluru); TN Biligundlu 177.25 TMC.
- **ISRWD (Amendment) Bill, 2019 & Entry 17:** Proposed **DRC (1 yr + 6 mo)**, **Single Permanent Tribunal** (**4.5-yr cap**), automatic finality, **National Hydrological Data Bank**; passed LS Jul 2019, **lapsed June 2024** (`Art. 107(5)`). Middle path over Concurrent shift: **RBOs under Entry 56** + Single Tribunal + demand management.

## 5. Inter-State Council, Full Faith & Credit, Zonal Councils & Trade

- **Inter-State Council (`Art. 263`):** **Constitutional**, **advisory/non-binding**, set up by **President** on **28 May 1990** (Janata Dal, Sarkaria). Duties: **(a)** disputes (legal/non-legal, complements `Art. 131`), **(b)** common subjects, **(c)** policy coordination. **Composition:** **PM (Chair)**, all CMs, UT CMs/Admins, Governors (if PR), PM-nominated Cabinet Ministers (**6 originally; 9 in 8 Nov 2024 reconstitution** + invitees). **Standing Committee (1996)** chaired by **Home Minister**; Secretariat (1991; Zonal Councils since 2011). **Dormant:** **11 meetings total; last 16 July 2016** (>10 yrs gap).
- **Full Faith & Credit (`Art. 261`):** Nationwide recognition of public acts, records & judicial proceedings (Parliament sets proof); **`Art. 261(3)`** enforces **CIVIL judgments ONLY** — **criminal/penal excluded**.
- **Zonal Councils (STATUTORY):** **States Reorganisation Act, 1956 (Part III, Secs. 15–22)** (Nehru). **5 Zones:** **Northern** (New Delhi: HR, HP, PB, RJ + CH, DL, JK, LA), **Central** (Prayagraj: CG, MP, UK, UP), **Eastern** (Kolkata: BR, JH, OD, WB — **no Sikkim**), **Western** (Mumbai: GA, GJ, MH + DNH&DD), **Southern** (Chennai: AP, KA, KL, TN, TS + PY; **A&N & Lakshadweep special invitees**). **Home Minister** Chair; CMs rotate Vice-Chair (1 yr). Active: **71 meetings (2014–Sept 2026)** vs 25 (2004–14); **1,445/1,893 (~76%) resolved** (28th Western ZC, Panaji, 7 Sept 2026).
- **North-Eastern Council (NEC):** Separate **NEC Act, 1971** (HQ **Shillong**); **8 states** (**Sikkim added 2002**, statutory planning body); includes **both Governors & CMs** + 3 President nominees; **Home Minister** (Chair since 2018) & **DoNER Minister** (Vice-Chair); reviews security; **73rd Plenary (Shillong, 4 Jun 2026)**.
- **Border Disputes:** **Assam–Meghalaya** (Mar 2022 MoU: 6/12 areas), **Assam–Arunachal** (Namsai Jul 2022 + Apr 2023 MoU: 123 villages), Assam–Mizoram, Assam–Nagaland (SC), **MH–KA Belagavi** (Mahajan 1966–67; 2004 Art. 131 suit), **PB–HR Chandigarh** (1985 Accord).
- **Inter-State Trade (`Part XIII, Arts. 301–307`):** From **Australia (s. 92)**. `Art. 301`: **inter-state & intra-state** freedom; `302`: Parliament public-interest restrictions; `303(1)` non-discrimination & **`303(2)` ONLY Parliament** scarcity exception; `304(a)` non-discriminatory import tax; **`304(b)`** State public-interest restrictions need **prior Presidential sanction** (cured via assent, `Art. 255`); `305` saves **state monopolies** (4th Amdt); `306` repealed (7th Amdt); **`307` authority never appointed**. ***Jindal Stainless* (11 Nov 2016, 9-Judge, 7–2)** overruled *Atiabari*/*Automobile Transport* compensatory tax doctrine & upheld non-discriminatory state entry taxes without `304(b)` prior sanction; **101st Amdt + GST (2017)** abolished check-posts.
