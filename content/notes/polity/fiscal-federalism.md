## 1. The logic of fiscal federalism in India

**Fiscal federalism** governs how revenue powers and expenditure duties are shared across tiers of government—and how mismatches between them are bridged. India's Constitution intentionally builds a **Vertical Fiscal Imbalance (VFI)**: to preserve a unified national market and avoid destructive tax competition, it assigns the most buoyant taxes (income tax, corporation tax, customs, central GST) to the Union, while placing the heaviest social and developmental responsibilities (health, education, policing, agriculture, rural infrastructure) on the States, which incur **over 60 per cent of general government expenditure**.

Compounding this is a deep **Horizontal Fiscal Imbalance (HFI)**: sharp disparities in per capita Gross State Domestic Product (GSDP), geography, and tax base mean that poorer or hilly states cannot fund comparable public services alone. To correct both imbalances while upholding federal dignity, the Constitution relies on two premier bodies: the **Finance Commission (Article 280)** for intergovernmental transfers, and the **GST Council (Article 279A)** for cooperative indirect-tax governance.

## 2. The Finance Commission (Articles 280 and 281)

Widely termed the "balancing wheel of fiscal federalism", the **Finance Commission** is a **quasi-judicial constitutional body** constituted by the **President** under **Article 280(1)** every **five years** (or earlier if needed). The First Finance Commission (**K.C. Neogy**) was appointed in 1951; sixteen Commissions have reported to date.

### Composition, qualifications and functions

Under **Article 280(1)–(2)**, the Commission comprises a **Chairman and four other members** appointed by the President. Crucially, **Parliament**—*not* the President—prescribes their qualifications via the **Finance Commission (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1951**: the **Chairman** must have experience in **public affairs**, and the four members must be drawn from persons who (1) are or have been or are qualified to be a **High Court judge**, (2) hold specialised knowledge of **government finance and accounts**, (3) possess wide experience in **financial matters and administration**, or (4) have special knowledge of **economics**. Members hold office for the period fixed by presidential order and are **eligible for reappointment**. As a quasi-judicial body, the Commission exercises civil-court powers to summon witnesses and inspect public records.

Under **Article 280(3)**, the Commission advises the President on four fronts:

- **Tax devolution (clause (a)):** Vertical distribution of net tax proceeds between Union and States (Article 270) and horizontal allocation among States.
- **Grants-in-aid (clause (b)):** Principles governing statutory grants out of the Consolidated Fund of India under **Article 275**.
- **Local bodies (clauses (bb) and (c)):** Measures to augment a State's Consolidated Fund to supplement **Panchayats** and **Municipalities** based on **State Finance Commission (SFC)** recommendations—inserted by the **73rd and 74th Amendments, 1992**.
- **Sound finance (clause (d)):** Any other matter referred by the President (e.g., disaster financing, fiscal consolidation, power-sector and subsidy reforms).

Under **Article 279**, **"net proceeds"** (gross collections minus collection costs) are ascertained and certified by the **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)**, whose certificate is final.

> **Prelims pointer:** Four classic Article 280 traps: (1) **Parliament** (1951 Act)—not the Constitution or President—sets member qualifications; (2) Members are **eligible for reappointment**; (3) Recommendations are **advisory, not legally binding**, though accepted by strong convention; (4) Under **Article 281**, the President lays the report alongside an **Explanatory Memorandum on action taken** before **both Houses of Parliament**.

## 3. The 15th versus the 16th Finance Commission (2021–26 vs 2026–31)

Modern tax devolution pivoted when the **14th Finance Commission** (Y.V. Reddy, 2015–20) raised States' vertical share from **32 per cent** (13th FC) to **42 per cent**, replacing discretionary plan transfers with untied funds. The **15th Finance Commission** (N.K. Singh, covering 2020–21 and **2021–26**) adjusted this by one percentage point to **41 per cent** strictly to meet the needs of the newly formed Union Territories of **Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh** from the Union's share. Prelims 2025 tested its other recommendations: it brought back a **tax effort** criterion, and proposed **₹4,800 crore** to reward States for educational outcomes and **₹45,000 crore** as a performance incentive for agricultural reforms.

### The 16th Finance Commission (2026–31)

Constituted on **31 December 2023** under Chairman **Dr. Arvind Panagariya** (full-time members **Annie George Mathew** and **Dr. Manoj Panda**; part-time members **T. Rabi Sankar** and **Dr. Soumya Kanti Ghosh**; Secretary **Ritvik Ranjanam Pandey**), the **16th Finance Commission** submitted its report on **17 November 2025**. On **1 February 2026**, the Finance Minister tabled the report and the Union's **Action Taken Report** in Parliament for the five-year award period **1 April 2026 to 31 March 2031**.

- **Vertical devolution retained at 41 per cent:** Despite 18 States seeking 50 per cent, the 16th FC retained States' share at **41 per cent**, pointing to the Union's own needs for defence and capital spending. It held that the Constitution does not let a Finance Commission cap cesses and surcharges or add them to the pool, and suggested a bargain instead: the Union folds most of them into ordinary taxes, and the States accept a somewhat smaller share of the larger pool. The Union Government accepted the **41 per cent** share and agreed to disclose **CAG-certified Article 279 net proceeds** annually for transparency.
- **Horizontal devolution — blending equity with GDP contribution:** While keeping **Income Distance** as the top equity weight, the 16th FC dropped **Tax and Fiscal Efforts** (2.5 per cent) and introduced a landmark **10 per cent "Contribution to GDP"** criterion—calculated via the **square root of each state's GSDP share** (2018–19 to 2023–24 average, excluding 2020–21) to reward economic growth without letting giant states dwarf smaller ones. It also redefined **Demographic Performance** using slower 1971–2011 population growth (replacing inverse Total Fertility Rate), lowered the **Area** floor from 2 per cent to 1.5 per cent, and expanded **Forest** to include open forests.

| Horizontal devolution criterion | 15th FC (2021–26) | 16th FC (2026–31) | Key methodological shift in 16th FC |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Income Distance** (Per Capita GSDP) | **45.0%** | **42.5%** | Distance from avg per capita GSDP of top 3 large states |
| **Population (2011 Census)** | **15.0%** | **17.5%** | Expenditure need via 2011 Census |
| **Demographic Performance** | **12.5%** | **10.0%** | Shifted from inverse TFR to slower 1971–2011 growth rate |
| **Area** | **15.0%** | **10.0%** | Minimum state floor cut from 2% to 1.5% |
| **Forest / Forest & Ecology** | **10.0%** | **10.0%** | Broadened to include **open forests** + gain bonus |
| **Tax and Fiscal Efforts** | **2.5%** | **0%** | **Discontinued** |
| **Contribution to GDP** *(New)* | **0%** | **10.0%** | **New efficiency metric** (√GSDP share) |
| **Total** | **100%** | **100%** | **Accepted** by Union Government (1 Feb 2026) |

Under this formula, **Uttar Pradesh** holds the largest share (**17.619 per cent**), followed by **Bihar (9.948 per cent)**, and **Sikkim** the smallest (**0.335 per cent**). **14 states** (predominantly higher-income and southern states, e.g., Karnataka rising from 3.647 per cent to **4.131 per cent**) gained inter se share, while **14 states** (mostly lower-income and north-eastern states) lost share.

- **Overhaul of Article 275 Grants-in-aid:** Whereas the 15th FC recommended ₹10.33 lakh crore across five windows (₹2.95 lakh crore Revenue Deficit Grants to 17 states, ₹4.36 lakh crore Local Bodies, ₹1.22 lakh crore Union Disaster share, plus largely unaccepted sector/state-specific grants), the **16th FC streamlined grants to ₹9.47 lakh crore (₹9,47,409 crore)** for 2026–31 and **discontinued Post-Devolution Revenue Deficit Grants (RDGs), sector-specific grants, and state-specific grants altogether**. Its grants target two heads: **Local Bodies — ₹7,91,493 crore** (**₹4,35,236 crore Rural**, **₹3,56,257 crore Urban**; 80 per cent Basic [half untied, half tied to sanitation, waste, and water] and 20 per cent Performance, plus **₹56,100 crore** for wastewater in 10–40 lakh cities and a **₹10,000 crore Urbanisation Premium**, all contingent on three entry criteria: constitutional local bodies, audited accounts disclosure, and timely SFCs); and **Disaster Management — ₹1,55,916 crore Union share** of a **₹2,04,401 crore** SDRF/SDMF corpus (retaining 90:10 sharing for NE/Himalayan states and 75:25 for others).
- **Fiscal consolidation roadmap:** Union fiscal deficit to **3.5 per cent of GDP by 2030–31**; States capped at **3 per cent of GSDP**; strict ban on **off-budget borrowings** (uniform expanded debt/deficit definitions); combined debt projected down from **77.3 per cent (2026–27) to 73.1 per cent of GDP (2030–31)**; DISCOM privatisation; and rationalising unconditional cash transfers.

> **In the news:** Tabling the **16th Finance Commission Report** and Action Taken Report on **1 February 2026**, the Union Government accepted the **41 per cent** share and the new formula. The end of Revenue Deficit Grants drew sharp criticism from fiscally stressed states.

## 4. The GST Council: pooled sovereignty and constitutional mechanics

Proposed by the **Kelkar Task Force (2004)** and enacted by the **101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016** (assent **8 September 2016**), GST replaced cascading Union and State indirect taxes on **1 July 2017**. The amendment inserted **Article 246A** (simultaneous legislative power), **Article 269A** (IGST), and **Article 279A**, under which the President constituted the **GST Council** in **September 2016**. It institutionalises **"pooled sovereignty"**: Centre and States merged exclusive indirect-tax silos into one shared constitutional forum.

### Composition, quorum and weighted voting (Article 279A)

The GST Council has **33 members**: (1) the **Union Finance Minister** (**Chairperson**); (2) the **Union Minister of State** for Revenue or Finance; and (3) the Finance/Taxation (or nominated) Minister of **each State**—with **Article 366(26B)** counting Union Territories with legislatures (**Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu & Kashmir**) as "States" (**2 Union + 31 State/UT = 33**). State ministers elect a **Vice-Chairperson** from among themselves; the Union Revenue Secretary is ex-officio Secretary, and the CBIC Chairperson is a permanent non-voting invitee.

| Rule (Art. 279A(7)–(9)) | Constitutional threshold | Federal consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Quorum** | **1/2 (50%)** of total members (17 of 33) | Broad participation required to transact business |
| **Vote weightage** | **Union = 1/3 (33.33%)**; **All States/UTs combined = 2/3 (66.67%)** | Every State/UT carries equal individual weight regardless of size |
| **Decision majority** | **3/4 (75%)** of weighted votes of members **present and voting** | **Mutual veto:** all States combined (66.67%) **cannot** outvote the Union; Union (33.33%) needs **over ~62.5%** of voting States to pass any proposal |

By convention, almost every decision is taken by **consensus** rather than formal voting.

### Mandate, exclusions and *Mohit Minerals* (2022)

Under **Article 279A(4)–(5)**, the Council recommends subsumed taxes, exemptions, model GST laws, thresholds, rate bands, special rates during disasters, special provisions for **11 states** (**Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Jammu & Kashmir, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, Tripura, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand**), and the date for bringing **five petroleum products** (**crude, petrol, diesel, natural gas, aviation turbine fuel**) under GST. Under **Article 279A(11)**, it must also establish a Centre–State/inter-state **dispute resolution mechanism** (not yet formally operationalised as a standing tribunal).

> **Prelims pointer:** The **five petroleum products** are inside GST but **deferred** pending a Council date (Art. 279A(5)). By contrast, **alcoholic liquor for human consumption** is **constitutionally excluded** from GST altogether (**Article 366(12A)**)—requiring a constitutional amendment to include. **Tobacco** bears *both* GST and Union excise duty.

In ***Union of India v. M/s Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd.*** (**19 May 2022**), striking down IGST on ocean freight under reverse charge on CIF imports, the Supreme Court held that GST Council recommendations carry **persuasive value** and are **not legally binding** on Parliament or State Legislatures, because **Article 246A** confers simultaneous, equal legislative power on both tiers. Calling Indian federalism a dialogue blending cooperative and "uncooperative" federalism, the Court preserved legislative autonomy—though governments routinely follow Council advice to sustain India's common market (and subordinate GST rules statutorily require prior Council recommendation).

> **In the news:** Three milestones reshaped GST during 2025–2026: at its **56th meeting (3 September 2025)**, the Council approved **"GST 2.0"** rate rationalisation (effective **22 September 2025**), collapsing the four-tier slab structure into a **two-rate grid** (**5% merit** and **18% standard**) plus a **40% demerit/luxury rate**, and **exempting all individual life and health insurance policies**; on **24 September 2025**, the **GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT)** was formally launched under its President, **Justice Sanjaya Kumar Mishra**; on **1 February 2026**, after pandemic loan repayment, the **GST Compensation Cess** exited completely (₹0 in BE 2026–27; tobacco/pan masala moved to 40% GST alongside excise amendments and the Health Security and National Security Cess Act); and at its **57th meeting (8 October 2026)**, the Council recommended removing the **arrest provisions** of the GST law, raising the prosecution threshold from **₹1 crore to ₹5 crore**, and cutting the general penalty from **₹25,000 to ₹10,000**.

## 5. Fiscal federalism challenges and the way forward

Five structural tensions test India's fiscal federalism today:

- **Shrinking divisible pool via cesses and surcharges:** Because **Article 271 surcharges** and earmarked **cesses** bypass the Article 270 divisible pool, rising central recourse to them erodes states' share. The 16th Finance Commission's own figures show the divisible pool falling from about **89 per cent** of gross tax revenue in the 13th Commission's period to about **82 per cent** in the 14th's and **78 per cent** in the 15th's, so that 41 per cent of the pool came to only about 32 per cent of gross tax revenue. The end of the GST compensation cess in 2026 eases this somewhat.
- **North–South equity-versus-efficiency debate:** Replacing the **1971 Census** with the **2011 Census** (15th FC onward) alarmed demographic-frontrunner states. While the 16th FC's **10 per cent Contribution to GDP** weight rewarded economic scale, **14 states**, most of them poorer or north-eastern, lost share, and the end of Revenue Deficit Grants adds to their loss.
- **Borrowing caps, off-budget debt and populist subsidies:** State welfare spending and unconditional cash transfers collide with strict Union enforcement of **Article 293(3)** Net Borrowing Ceilings (including deducting PSU off-budget loans, referred to a 5-judge Bench in *State of Kerala v. Union of India*, 2024).
- **CSS proliferation and conditionalities:** Heavy use of **Article 282** tied grants with rigid cost-sharing ratios (60:40 / 90:10) narrows state spending autonomy compared to untied tax devolution.
- **Weak State Finance Commissions (SFCs):** Delayed constitution and ignored reports under **Articles 243-I and 243-Y** starve third-tier Panchayats and Municipalities of predictable resources.

### Balanced way forward

Strengthening fiscal federalism demands cooperative institutional reform: placing **sunset clauses** on earmarked cesses or sharing surcharge receipts above a threshold; giving the Finance Commission a **permanent secretariat**, alongside annual disclosure of the CAG-certified net proceeds; operationalising the **Article 279A(11) GST dispute body** and charting a consensus roadmap for petroleum products; rationalising CSS into fewer flexible block grants alongside an independent **Fiscal Council** (N.K. Singh FRBM Review Committee, 2017) for symmetric Centre–State debt oversight; and enforcing timely five-year **SFC** cycles with local property-tax reforms.

> **Mains angle:** Fiscal federalism bridges **GS-2 (Polity)** and **GS-3 (Economy)**. Pair constitutional mechanics (Arts. 270, 271, 275, 279A, 280, 282, 293) with **2025–2026 data**—the **16th FC's 41% retention, 10% GDP criterion, and RDG exit**; **GST 2.0 two-slab rationalisation, GSTAT launch, and Compensation Cess exit**; *Mohit Minerals* (2022); and the divisible pool's fall to about **78% of gross tax revenue**—and conclude with concrete institutional fixes (permanent FC cell, Fiscal Council, cess discipline, empowered SFCs).
