## 1. From traders to rulers

The East India Company came to India in 1600 with a royal charter to trade, not to govern. A century and a half later it was governing. Victory at Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764) gave it the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa under the Treaty of Allahabad (1765): the right to collect their revenue. Clive's "dual government" followed, in which the Company took the revenue while the Nawab kept the responsibility. It produced corruption, collapsing revenues and the Bengal famine of 1770, in which roughly a third of Bengal's people are believed to have died.

By 1772 the Company was close to bankruptcy and asked the British government for a loan. Parliament agreed, but it attached conditions, and those conditions began a story that runs for eighty-five years. Each Act of Parliament between 1773 and 1853 did three things a little more than the one before: it brought the Company under the control of the British state, it concentrated power in India in one Governor-General, and it turned the Company from a trading firm into an arm of administration. The story ends in 1858, when the Crown took over altogether.

```text
1773 Regulating Act ── Parliament steps in
        │
1784 Pitt's India Act ── Board of Control
        │
1793 · 1813 Charter Acts ── monopoly trimmed
        │
1833 Charter Act ── Governor-General of India
        │
1853 Charter Act ── legislature apart, open ICS
        │
1858 Crown rule (see the next note)
```

These Acts matter for two papers. GS-2 asks how the Constitution's "historical underpinnings" took shape; GS-1 asks how British rule organised administration in India. Prelims, in both history and polity, tests which Act introduced which change.

## 2. The Regulating Act, 1773

The Regulating Act was the first step taken by the British government to control and regulate the Company's affairs in India. For the first time it recognised that the Company had political and administrative functions, not merely commercial ones, and it laid the foundation of central administration in India.

- The Governor of Bengal became the **Governor-General of Bengal**, with an Executive Council of four members. Warren Hastings was the first.
- The Governors of the Bombay and Madras Presidencies were made subordinate to the Governor-General of Bengal.
- A **Supreme Court at Calcutta** (Fort William) was provided for and set up in 1774, with a Chief Justice, Sir Elijah Impey, and three other judges.
- Company servants were forbidden to engage in private trade or to accept presents or bribes from "the natives".
- The Court of Directors, the Company's governing body, now had to report on its revenue, civil and military affairs in India to the British government.

The Act was a first draft, and it showed. The Governor-General had no veto and could be outvoted by his own council, which is exactly what happened to Hastings at the hands of Philip Francis and his allies. Nor did the Act say clearly where the Supreme Court's jurisdiction ended and the Council's began. The **Act of Settlement, 1781**, patched some of these gaps. It exempted the Governor-General and his Council from the Supreme Court's jurisdiction for acts done in their official capacity, kept revenue matters out of the Court's reach, and required the Court to respect the personal laws of Hindus and Muslims.

> **Prelims pointer:** Three titles, three dates. Governor-General *of Bengal* came with the Regulating Act (1773, Warren Hastings). Governor-General *of India* came with the Charter Act of 1833 (Lord William Bentinck). *Viceroy* came with the Government of India Act 1858 (Lord Canning).

## 3. Pitt's India Act, 1784

The Regulating Act had left real power where it began, with the Company. Pitt's India Act, named after Prime Minister William Pitt the Younger, split that power in two. The Court of Directors kept the Company's commercial affairs. A new **Board of Control** in London took charge of its political affairs, and it could supervise and direct all civil, military and revenue operations of the Company's possessions in India. This arrangement, in which the Company managed trade while a body of the British state directed government, is known as the **system of double government**.

The Act made two further changes that examiners like. For the first time, the Company's territories in India were called the "British possessions in India", which was a quiet assertion of British sovereignty. And the British government was given supreme control over the Company's affairs and its administration in India. The Governor-General's council was cut to three members. An amending Act of 1786 then gave Lord Cornwallis what Hastings had lacked: the power to override his council in special cases, and to serve as Commander-in-Chief as well.

## 4. The Charter Acts, 1793 to 1853

The Company's charter, its licence from the Crown, came up for renewal every twenty years, and Parliament used each renewal to reshape the Company.

| Act | What changed | Remember it by |
|---|---|---|
| **1793** | Trade monopoly renewed for 20 years. The Governor-General's power to override his council, and his control over the subordinate presidencies, widened. Salaries of the Board of Control to be paid from Indian revenues | "Twenty more years" |
| **1813** | The Company's trade monopoly in India ended, except for trade in tea and with China. The Crown's sovereignty over the Company's territories asserted. Christian missionaries allowed in. ₹1 lakh a year set aside for education | "Monopoly broken, education funded" |
| **1833** | The Governor-General of Bengal became the Governor-General of India, with all civil and military power. The Company ended as a trading body. All law-making centralised | "Centralisation complete" |
| **1853** | The Governor-General's council split into executive and legislative wings. Civil services opened to competition. Company rule extended with no fixed term | "Legislature apart, merit in" |

### The Charter Act of 1813

The Act of 1813 reflected a changed Britain. Industrialists wanted the Indian market opened, and evangelicals wanted access for missionaries; the Act gave both. Ending the monopoly, except for tea and the China trade, turned India into a market for British manufactures. That is the background to the "deindustrialisation" debate in modern Indian history. The grant of one lakh rupees a year for the "revival and improvement of literature" and the encouragement of "learned natives" was the first public money for education, and the start of the long Orientalist–Anglicist argument that Macaulay's Minute settled in 1835.

### The Charter Act of 1833: the high point of centralisation

The Act of 1833 was the final step towards centralisation in British India. It made the Governor-General of Bengal the **Governor-General of India**, vested in him all civil and military power, and gave the Government of India authority over the whole of British territory. The Governors of Bombay and Madras lost their power to legislate. From now on the Governor-General in Council alone made law for all of British India, and laws made under this Act were called **Acts**, where earlier ones had been called **Regulations**.

The Act also ended the Company's career as a trader. It became a purely administrative body, holding its territories "in trust for His Majesty, His heirs and successors". A fourth member joined the Governor-General's council for law-making alone; the first was Thomas Babington Macaulay. Under him the First Law Commission began codifying Indian law, work that produced the Indian Penal Code (drafted in 1837, enacted in 1860). The Act also declared that no Indian should be debarred from any office under the Company by reason of religion, place of birth, descent or colour. It tried to introduce open competition for civil service posts, but the Court of Directors' opposition kept that on paper.

> **In the news:** The Indian Penal Code, which grew out of the Law Commission set up under the 1833 Act, was replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, with effect from 1 July 2024. The Code of Criminal Procedure and the Indian Evidence Act gave way to the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam on the same day.

### The Charter Act of 1853: the first separation of powers

The last of the Charter Acts separated, for the first time, the legislative and executive functions of the Governor-General's council. Six new members, called legislative councillors, were added for law-making. The legislative wing came to be called the Indian (Central) Legislative Council, and it worked like a mini-parliament, adopting the procedures of the British Parliament. Four of the six legislative members were appointed by the provincial governments of Madras, Bombay, Bengal and Agra. That made this the first introduction of **local representation** in the central legislature.

The Act also opened the **civil services to open competition**, ending the Directors' patronage. Indians became eligible, and the Macaulay Committee on the Indian Civil Service was appointed in 1854 to work out the scheme. Finally, the Act extended the Company's rule without fixing a term, which signalled that Parliament could end it whenever it chose. It did so five years later.

> **Remember:** The first Indian to enter the Indian Civil Service through open competition was Satyendranath Tagore, in 1863. Competition was opened in 1853, but the examination was held in London, with a low age limit, which kept Indians out for decades.

## 5. What these Acts built

Read together, the Acts show four lines of development that run straight into the twentieth century and, in part, into the Constitution.

| Line of development | How it grew under the Company |
|---|---|
| Control by the British state | Reports to London (1773) → Board of Control (1784) → sovereignty asserted (1813) → territories held "in trust" (1833) → rule held at Parliament's pleasure (1853) |
| A single central government | Bengal's primacy (1773) → the Governor-General's overriding power (1786, 1793) → one Governor-General of India and one law-maker (1833) |
| A legislature apart from the executive | A law member (1833) → a separate legislative wing with local representation (1853) |
| Rule of law and a professional bureaucracy | Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774) → codification (1833) → open competition for the civil service (1853) |

The centralisation of 1833 is the root of a pattern the later reforms had to undo, starting with the decentralisation of 1861. The separation of 1853 is the seed of every legislature that followed. A Mains answer on the "historical underpinnings" of the Constitution should show this continuity: the unitary tilt of Indian federalism, the career civil service and the codified criminal law all have Company-era beginnings.

> **Mains angle:** A question on how the Company's rule shaped Indian administration can be organised around these lines: centralisation, parliamentary control, the rule of law, and the civil service. Balance it with the costs: economic drain, the exclusion of Indians from real power, and the Act of 1813's role in opening India to British manufactures. Close by linking the legacy to the Constitution: a strong Centre, a professional bureaucracy, and courts as guardians of legality.

- **1765:** Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa (Treaty of Allahabad). "Dual government" followed, then the Bengal famine of 1770.
- **Regulating Act 1773:** the first parliamentary control of the Company, and the base of central administration. Created the Governor-General of Bengal (Warren Hastings) with a council of four, and subordinated Bombay and Madras. Provided for the Supreme Court at Calcutta (set up 1774: Elijah Impey and three judges). Banned private trade and presents.
- **Act of Settlement 1781:** the Governor-General in Council was exempted from the Supreme Court for official acts, and personal laws were respected.
- **Pitt's India Act 1784:** the Board of Control (political) beside the Court of Directors (commercial), so "double government". First use of "British possessions in India".
- **1786:** Cornwallis could override his council and was also Commander-in-Chief.
- **Charter Act 1793:** monopoly renewed for 20 years. Overriding power widened. Board of Control paid from Indian revenue.
- **Charter Act 1813:** monopoly ended except tea and the China trade. Missionaries allowed. ₹1 lakh a year for education.
- **Charter Act 1833:** Governor-General of India (Bentinck). Bombay and Madras lost law-making. "Regulations" became "Acts". The Company became an administrative body only. Macaulay was the law member, and the Law Commission was set up (IPC 1860). Open competition was proposed but not implemented.
- **Charter Act 1853:** legislative and executive functions separated. Six legislative members, four of them from provincial governments (local representation). Open competition for the ICS (Macaulay Committee, 1854). No fixed term for the charter.
- The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita replaced the IPC from 1 July 2024.
