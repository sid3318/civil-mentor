## 1. A right is only as strong as its remedy

A declaration of rights is worth little if nobody can enforce it. The framers knew this, so they did not stop at listing Fundamental Rights. **Article 32** makes the remedy itself a Fundamental Right: any person whose Fundamental Right has been violated can move the **Supreme Court directly**, without first going to a lower court. Because the remedy is a Fundamental Right, Parliament cannot take it away by ordinary law. This is what turns the rights in Part III from aspirations into guarantees.

> "If I was asked to name any particular article in this Constitution as the most important—an article without which this Constitution would be a nullity—I could not refer to any other article except this one. It is the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it." — B.R. Ambedkar on draft Article 25 (now Article 32), Constituent Assembly, 9 December 1948

In ***Romesh Thappar* (1950)** the Supreme Court described itself as the "protector and guarantor" of Fundamental Rights, and said it could not refuse to entertain applications seeking their protection. In ***L. Chandra Kumar* (1997)** it held that judicial review under Articles 32 and 226 is part of the **basic structure** of the Constitution, so even a constitutional amendment cannot remove it.

## 2. What Article 32 provides

Article 32 has four clauses.

1. It guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings to enforce the Fundamental Rights.
2. It empowers the Supreme Court to issue **directions, orders or writs** for this purpose, including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto.
3. It lets **Parliament** empower any other court to issue these writs within its local limits, without prejudice to the Supreme Court's powers. (Parliament has not yet done so. The High Courts' power comes not from this clause but from Article 226.)
4. It provides that the right shall not be **suspended** except as the Constitution provides. The Constitution provides for it in Article 359, which lets the President suspend the right to move any court to enforce Fundamental Rights during a national emergency.

The Supreme Court's jurisdiction under Article 32 is **original** (a person can come to it directly), but **not exclusive**. It is **concurrent** with the High Courts' jurisdiction under Article 226, so a person may go to either. And it covers only **Fundamental Rights**. A violation of an ordinary legal right, or of a constitutional right outside Part III, such as the right to property under Article 300A, cannot be brought under Article 32.

### Public interest litigation

In the early decades only the person whose right had been violated could approach the court, under the rule of **locus standi**. From the late 1970s the Supreme Court relaxed this rule, so that any public-spirited person or group could seek relief on behalf of those unable to approach the court themselves: bonded labourers, undertrial prisoners, pavement dwellers. In ***S.P. Gupta* (1981)** the Court gave the principle its classic statement. It even treated **letters and postcards** as writ petitions, which is sometimes called its "epistolary jurisdiction". Public interest litigation (PIL) became one of the most distinctive features of Indian constitutional law, and it grew out of Article 32.

## 3. The five writs

Writs are formal orders of a court, inherited from the English legal tradition, where they were "prerogative writs" issued in the King's name. The Constitution gives both the Supreme Court and the High Courts the power to issue them.

| Writ | Literal meaning | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Habeas corpus** | "To have the body of" | Release a person unlawfully detained |
| **Mandamus** | "We command" | Make a public authority perform a public duty it has failed to perform |
| **Prohibition** | "To forbid" | Stop a lower court or tribunal from exceeding its jurisdiction |
| **Certiorari** | "To be certified" or "to be informed" | Quash an order already passed by a lower court or tribunal |
| **Quo warranto** | "By what authority or warrant" | Test the legality of a person's claim to a public office |

### Habeas corpus

The court orders a person who has detained another to produce the detained person before it, so that it can examine the cause and legality of the detention. If the detention is illegal, the court sets the person free. Habeas corpus is the writ that most directly protects personal liberty, and it can be issued against **public authorities and private individuals** alike.

It is not issued where the detention is lawful, where the proceeding is for contempt of a legislature or a court, where the detention is by order of a competent court, or where the detention is outside the court's jurisdiction.

### Mandamus

A command issued by the court to a public official, asking them to perform official duties they have failed or refused to perform. It can be issued against any public body, corporation, inferior court, tribunal or government for the same purpose.

Mandamus **cannot** be issued:

- against a private individual or body;
- to enforce a departmental instruction that does not have statutory force;
- where the duty is discretionary, not mandatory;
- to enforce a contractual obligation;
- against the **President of India or the Governors** of states;
- against the Chief Justice of a High Court acting in a judicial capacity.

### Prohibition

Issued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal to stop it from exceeding its jurisdiction, or from usurping a jurisdiction it does not have. Mandamus directs activity; prohibition directs inactivity. Prohibition can be issued only against **judicial and quasi-judicial** authorities. It is not available against administrative authorities, legislative bodies, or private individuals and bodies.

### Certiorari

Issued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal, either to transfer a case pending before it, or to **quash its order** in a case. It lies on grounds of excess of jurisdiction, lack of jurisdiction, or an error of law. Certiorari is thus both preventive and curative, where prohibition is only preventive.

Until 1991, certiorari could be issued only against judicial and quasi-judicial authorities. In 1991 the Supreme Court held that it can be issued even against **administrative authorities** that affect the rights of individuals. Like prohibition, it is not available against legislative bodies or private individuals and bodies.

### Quo warranto

The court enquires into the legality of a person's claim to a public office, and so prevents the illegal usurpation of public office. The writ can be issued only for a **substantive public office of a permanent character**, created by a statute or by the Constitution. It cannot be issued for a ministerial office or a private office. Unlike the other writs, it can be sought by **any interested person**, not only the person aggrieved.

```text
 Is the wrong a detention?        → habeas corpus
 A public duty left undone?       → mandamus
 A lower court about to exceed
 its jurisdiction?                → prohibition
 A lower court's order already
 passed without jurisdiction?     → certiorari
 A public office held without
 legal authority?                 → quo warranto
```

> **Prelims pointer:** Habeas corpus lies even against private persons; prohibition and certiorari lie only against courts, tribunals and (for certiorari, since 1991) administrative authorities, never against legislatures or private persons. Mandamus cannot be issued against the President or a Governor. Quo warranto is the only writ that any interested person, not just the aggrieved one, can seek.

## 4. The Supreme Court and the High Courts compared

The High Courts' writ jurisdiction under **Article 226** is in some ways **wider** than the Supreme Court's.

| | Supreme Court (Article 32) | High Court (Article 226) |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Fundamental Rights only | Fundamental Rights **and "any other purpose"**, including ordinary legal rights |
| Territory | Can issue writs against any person or government in India | Against any person or government within its territory, or outside it if the cause of action arises within |
| Nature | A Fundamental Right, so the Court cannot refuse to exercise it | Discretionary, so the High Court may refuse |
| During an emergency | Suspended if the President so orders under Article 359 | Can likewise be barred for Fundamental Rights, but remains available for other legal rights |

> **Remember:** The Supreme Court's writ jurisdiction is narrower in scope (Fundamental Rights only) but its own exercise is itself a Fundamental Right. The High Court's is wider in scope ("any other purpose") but discretionary.

## 5. Article 33: Fundamental Rights and the armed forces

Article 33 empowers **Parliament**, and only Parliament, to restrict or abrogate the Fundamental Rights of the members of the **armed forces, para-military forces, police forces, intelligence agencies and analogous forces**. The aim is to ensure the proper discharge of their duties and the maintenance of discipline among them. Such a law cannot be challenged in any court for contravening a Fundamental Right.

Under this power Parliament has enacted, among others, the Army Act, 1950, the Navy Act, 1957, the Air Force Act, 1950, and the Police Forces (Restriction of Rights) Act, 1966. These laws restrict such rights as forming associations, speaking to the press and taking part in political activity.

## 6. Article 34: martial law

Article 34 provides for restrictions on Fundamental Rights while **martial law** is in force in any area of India. It empowers Parliament to **indemnify** any government servant or other person for any act done in connection with maintaining or restoring order in that area, and to validate any sentence passed or punishment inflicted there under martial law.

The Constitution does not define martial law or say how it is to be declared. Martial law means military rule: the suspension of ordinary law and the government of an area by military tribunals, in extraordinary circumstances such as war, invasion, insurrection, rebellion or riot. The Supreme Court has held that declaring martial law does not by itself suspend the writ of habeas corpus.

| Martial law | National emergency |
|---|---|
| Affects only Fundamental Rights | Affects Fundamental Rights, centre–state relations, the distribution of revenues and legislative powers, and the duration of Parliament |
| Suspends the government and ordinary courts in the area | Government and ordinary courts continue |
| Imposed when law and order break down for any reason | Imposed only on grounds of war, external aggression or armed rebellion |
| Imposed in a specific area | Can cover the whole country or any part of it |
| No specific provision in the Constitution; it is implicit | Specific and detailed provisions (Article 352) |

## 7. Article 35: laws only Parliament can make

Article 35 gives **Parliament** alone, not the state legislatures, the power to make laws on certain matters, so that these rights are uniform across the country. Parliament alone can make laws:

- prescribing residence as a condition for certain employments in a state or Union territory (Article 16(3));
- empowering courts other than the Supreme Court and the High Courts to issue writs (Article 32(3));
- restricting or abrogating the Fundamental Rights of the armed forces and similar forces (Article 33);
- indemnifying government servants for acts done during martial law (Article 34).

Parliament also has the power, and the duty, to make laws **prescribing punishment** for acts declared offences under Part III: untouchability (Article 17) and trafficking and forced labour (Article 23). Any law in force on these matters at the commencement of the Constitution continues until Parliament alters it.

## 8. Laws saved from challenge: Articles 31A, 31B and 31C

Part III also contains three Articles that **save certain laws** from being challenged for violating Fundamental Rights. They were the battleground of the long contest between Parliament and the courts over land reform and property.

- **Article 31A**, added by the **1st Amendment (1951)**, saves five categories of laws from challenge under Articles 14 and 19: the acquisition of estates by the State, the State taking over the management of property, the amalgamation of corporations, the extinguishment or modification of the rights of directors or shareholders, and the extinguishment or modification of mining leases. A state law under this Article needs the President's assent to be protected.
- **Article 31B**, also from the 1st Amendment, protects the laws placed in the **Ninth Schedule** from challenge for violating any Fundamental Right. In ***I.R. Coelho* (2007)** the Supreme Court held that laws added after 24 April 1973 can be reviewed if they damage the basic structure.
- **Article 31C**, added by the **25th Amendment (1971)**, protects laws giving effect to the Directive Principles in **Article 39(b) and (c)**, on the distribution of material resources and the prevention of concentration of wealth, from challenge under Articles 14 and 19. In ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)** the Court struck down the part of Article 31C that excluded judicial review. The **42nd Amendment (1976)** extended the protection to all Directive Principles, but in ***Minerva Mills* (1980)** the Court struck down that extension, holding that the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is part of the basic structure.

> **In the news:** The Supreme Court continues to use Article 32 to address executive excess. On **13 November 2024**, in a case filed directly before it under Article 32, the Court laid down nationwide guidelines against the demolition of the homes of persons merely accused of crimes, the so-called "bulldozer justice". It held that the executive cannot act as judge and punish people by demolishing their property, as that violates the rule of law and the right to shelter under Article 21.

> **Mains angle:** Article 32 invites questions on judicial power. **Access to justice:** how PIL opened the courts to the poor, with examples from bonded labour, prisoners' rights and the environment. **The other side:** criticism that PIL has led to judicial overreach, a flood of frivolous petitions and courts taking over executive functions, adding to the pendency of cases. **Liberty in a crisis:** *ADM Jabalpur* (1976), when the Court held that habeas corpus could be suspended during the Emergency, and its overruling in *Puttaswamy* (2017), along with the 44th Amendment's protection of Articles 20 and 21. **The armed forces:** the balance between discipline and rights under Article 33. Conclude that an independent judiciary with the power to enforce rights is what makes Part III meaningful, while self-restraint keeps that power legitimate.

- **Article 32:** the right to move the Supreme Court to enforce Fundamental Rights is itself a Fundamental Right. Ambedkar called it "the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it" (9 December 1948, on draft Article 25). "Protector and guarantor" (*Romesh Thappar*, 1950).
- **Basic structure:** judicial review under Articles 32 and 226 (*L. Chandra Kumar*, 1997).
- **Clauses:** (2) directions, orders and writs; (3) Parliament may empower other courts (not done yet); (4) suspension only as the Constitution provides (Article 359, during an emergency).
- **Jurisdiction:** original but not exclusive, concurrent with Article 226. Covers Fundamental Rights only (not Article 300A).
- **PIL:** relaxed locus standi (*S.P. Gupta*, 1981); letters treated as petitions (epistolary jurisdiction).
- **Habeas corpus:** produce the detained person. Lies against public authorities and private persons. Not where detention is lawful, for contempt, by a competent court, or outside jurisdiction.
- **Mandamus:** perform a public duty. Not against private persons, non-statutory instructions, discretionary duties, contracts, the President or Governors, or a Chief Justice acting judicially.
- **Prohibition:** stop a lower court exceeding jurisdiction; preventive. Only against judicial and quasi-judicial bodies.
- **Certiorari:** quash a lower court's order; preventive and curative. Against administrative authorities too since 1991. Never against legislatures or private persons.
- **Quo warranto:** "by what authority". Only for a substantive public office created by statute or the Constitution. Any interested person may seek it.
- **Article 226** is wider in scope ("any other purpose") but discretionary; Article 32 covers Fundamental Rights only but is itself a Fundamental Right.
- **Article 33:** Parliament may restrict the Fundamental Rights of the armed forces, police, intelligence and analogous forces (Army Act 1950, Navy Act 1957, Air Force Act 1950, Police Forces (Restriction of Rights) Act 1966).
- **Article 34:** martial law; Parliament may indemnify. Martial law is not defined. It does not by itself suspend habeas corpus.
- **Article 35:** only Parliament legislates on Articles 16(3), 32(3), 33 and 34, and prescribes punishment for offences under Articles 17 and 23.
- **Article 31A** (1st Amendment): five kinds of laws saved from Articles 14 and 19. **Article 31B** (1st Amendment): the Ninth Schedule (*I.R. Coelho*, 2007, the 24 April 1973 cut-off). **Article 31C** (25th Amendment, 1971): Article 39(b) and (c). The judicial-review bar was struck down in *Kesavananda* (1973); the 42nd Amendment's extension to all directives was struck down in *Minerva Mills* (1980).
- **Bulldozer guidelines,** 13 November 2024, under Article 32: demolitions as punishment violate the rule of law and Article 21.
