## 1. Constitutional Amendment Procedure (Part XX, Article 368)

Every written constitution confronts an enduring architectural paradox. If its amending procedure is too permissive, a transient electoral majority can rewrite the foundational compact at will, dissolving the boundary between fundamental law and ordinary legislation. If its amending procedure is too unyielding, a living society outgrows its parchment rules, creating pressure that eventually breaks the constitutional order through extra-constitutional upheaval. The framers of the Indian Constitution consciously engineered a middle course—creating a charter firm enough to anchor a vast, diverse federal republic yet adaptable enough to absorb social, economic, and political transformation across generations.

Speaking in the Constituent Assembly during the general debate on the Draft Constitution, Jawaharlal Nehru captured this philosophy of organic endurance:

> "While we want this Constitution to be as solid and as permanent a structure as we can make it, nevertheless there is no permanence in Constitutions. There should be a certain flexibility. If you make anything rigid and permanent, you stop a nation's growth, the growth of a living, vital, organic people." — Jawaharlal Nehru, Constituent Assembly, 8 November 1948

### Philosophy of Constitutional Change: Blend of Rigidity and Flexibility

Constitutional scholars classify constitutions as either **rigid** or **flexible** depending on how they are amended. A **rigid constitution**, exemplified by the United States Constitution (Article V), requires an arduous special procedure distinct from ordinary law-making—two-thirds supermajorities in both Houses of Congress followed by ratification by three-fourths of the fifty states—resulting in only twenty-seven amendments over more than two centuries. A **flexible constitution**, exemplified by the uncodified British Constitution, can be altered by Parliament through the very same simple-majority legislative process used for ordinary statutes.

India deliberately synthesises both traditions, producing a **unique blend of rigidity and flexibility** that earned explicit admiration from British constitutional jurist **K.C. Wheare**, who observed that India's Constitution *"strikes a good balance between extreme flexibility and extreme rigidity"* and praised the graded variety of its amending procedures as wise yet rarely found elsewhere. Similarly, **Granville Austin** noted that the amending process proved to be one of the most ably conceived aspects of the Constitution, allowing the document to adjust to unanticipated challenges without losing its essential stability.

The formal amending power resides in **Part XX**, comprising a single provision—**`Article 368`**—structurally borrowed from the **Constitution of South Africa**, while India added the federal requirement of state ratification for provisions affecting Centre–State relations and constitutional institutions. Originally, `Article 368` bore the marginal heading *"Procedure for amendment of the Constitution."* Following the landmark confrontation in *I.C. Golaknath* (1967), the **`24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971`** substituted the heading with **"Power of Parliament to amend the Constitution and procedure therefor"**, inserted **`Article 368(1)`** affirming that Parliament acts in exercise of its **"constituent power"** to amend by way of *"addition, variation or repeal"*, renumbered the original text as **`Article 368(2)`**, and inserted **`Article 368(3)`** insulating Article 368 amendments from `Article 13`.

Between 1950 and November 2026, Parliament has enacted **106 Constitutional Amendment Acts**—the most recent being the **`106th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2023`** (*Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam* on women's legislative reservation), which was brought into force on **16 April 2026**. While 106 amendments over seventy-six years appears numerically high beside the United States, the comparison is misleading: India's single Constitution governs both the Union and the States in extraordinary administrative detail, incorporates matters that other federations leave to ordinary statutes, and has repeatedly accommodated linguistic reorganisation, new states, decennial reservation extensions, and cooperative-federal reforms like GST.

```keyfacts
# Article 368 & Constitutional Amendment Architecture
Part XX · Article 368 | Borrowed from South Africa | Expressly empowers Parliament to amend by way of addition, variation, or repeal (constituent power)
3 Amendment Tracks | 2 Inside Art. 368 + 1 Outside | Simple Majority (ordinary law), Special Majority of Parliament, and Special Majority + ≥ Half the States
106 Amendment Acts | Enacted up to Nov 2026 | Latest in force: 106th Act, 2023 (Women's Quota, notified 16 April 2026); 131st Bill defeated April 2026
8 Entrenched Federal Items | Proviso to Art. 368(2) | Requires ratification by at least half the State Legislatures; GST Council (Art. 279A) added by 101st Amend
```

### Architecture of Article 368 and the Three Amendment Pathways

A crucial conceptual nuance—frequently tested in UPSC Prelims—distinguishes between the **two** formal methods prescribed inside `Article 368` and the **three** distinct ways in which the Indian Constitution can actually be amended. Because the Constitution itself expressly authorizes Parliament to alter numerous structural and administrative provisions through ordinary legislation declared *outside* the purview of `Article 368`, the constitutional text operates along three graded tracks of ascending rigidity.

#### Track 1: Amendment by Simple Majority of Parliament (Outside Article 368)

Numerous provisions of the Constitution can be altered by a **simple majority** of the two Houses of Parliament—meaning a majority of the members **present and voting** in each House, exactly like an ordinary legislative Bill. Crucially, the relevant Articles expressly stipulate that *"no such law shall be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of Article 368."* Consequently, these laws are NOT assigned Constitutional Amendment Act numbers, CAN be resolved through a **Joint Sitting (`Article 108`)** in case of inter-House deadlock, and remain subject to the President's ordinary veto powers under **`Article 111`** (including returning the Bill once for reconsideration).

Aspirants must master the **complete, exhaustive list of provisions** amendable by simple majority outside `Article 368`, as UPSC routinely tests individual items from this catalogue:

1. **Admission or establishment of new states (`Article 2` read with `Article 4`):** Empowering Parliament to admit territories not already part of the Union of India into the federation (note that while *Sikkim* was integrated via the 35th and 36th Amendments because special constitutional terms under `Art. 2A` and `Art. 371F` were inserted, plain admission under `Art. 2` uses simple majority).
2. **Formation of new states and alteration of areas, boundaries, or names of existing states (`Article 3` read with `Article 4`):** Allowing Parliament to carve out new states, merge territories, or rename existing states with consequential amendments to the First and Fourth Schedules—exemplified historically by the creation of Telangana (2014) and recently by the **Kerala (Alteration of Name) Act, 2026**, which renamed Kerala as **Keralam** with effect from **25 August 2026**. *(Contrast: ceding Indian territory to a foreign state is NOT covered by Article 3 and requires an Article 368 amendment—In Re: Berubari Union, 1960.)*
3. **Abolition or creation of Legislative Councils in States (`Article 169`):** Parliament may create or abolish a State Legislative Council (*Vidhan Parishad*) by a **simple majority**, provided the concerned **State Legislative Assembly** has first passed a resolution to that effect by a **special majority** (majority of total membership of the Assembly and not less than two-thirds of members present and voting). `Article 169(3)` expressly excludes such Parliamentary laws from `Article 368`.
4. **Second Schedule (`Articles 59(3), 65(3), 75(6), 97, 125, 148(3), 158(3), 164(5), 186, 221`):** Emoluments, allowances, privileges, and related entitlements of the President, Governors, Speakers and Deputy Speakers, Chairmen and Deputy Chairmen, Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, and the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG).
5. **Quorum in Parliament (`Article 100(3)`):** Fixing the minimum attendance required to transact business (currently one-tenth of the total membership of each House *"until Parliament by law otherwise provides"*).
6. **Salaries and allowances of Members of Parliament (`Article 106`):** Determined periodically by Parliament through ordinary legislation.
7. **Rules of procedure in Parliament (`Article 118`):** Each House regulates its own procedure and conduct of business (and the President makes rules for Joint Sittings after consulting the Chairman of Rajya Sabha and Speaker of Lok Sabha).
8. **Privileges of Parliament, its members, and its committees (`Article 105(3)`):** Enabling Parliament to define and codify parliamentary privileges by ordinary law.
9. **Use of English language in Parliament (`Article 120(2)`):** Parliament enacted ordinary law continuing the use of English for transacting parliamentary business beyond the original fifteen-year window (1965).
10. **Number of puisne judges in the Supreme Court (`Article 124(1)`):** The Constitution originally fixed the Supreme Court at the Chief Justice plus seven other judges *"until Parliament by law prescribes a larger number."* Parliament has raised this strength repeatedly by simple majority—most recently via the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019 to **34 judges** (Chief Justice plus 33 puisne judges).
11. **Conferment of additional jurisdiction on the Supreme Court (`Article 138`):** Parliament may enlarge the Supreme Court's jurisdiction and powers with respect to any matter in the Union List by ordinary law. *(Prelims trap: Parliament can ENLARGE SC jurisdiction by simple majority under Art. 138, whereas CURTAILING SC jurisdiction requires Track 3—Special Majority plus state ratification!)*
12. **Use of official language (`Article 343(3)`):** Authorising Parliament to provide by ordinary law (Official Languages Act, 1963) for the continued use of English for official Union purposes alongside Hindi.
13. **Citizenship — acquisition and termination (`Article 11`):** Granting Parliament plenary power to regulate citizenship through ordinary statutes such as the Citizenship Act, 1955 and its subsequent amendments (including the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019).
14. **Elections to Parliament and State Legislatures (`Article 327`):** Enacting and amending electoral laws such as the Representation of the People Acts of 1950 and 1951.
15. **Delimitation of constituencies (`Article 82` and `Article 170`):** Establishing Delimitation Commissions and readjusting territorial constituencies after a census via ordinary Delimitation Acts (note, however, that lifting or altering the constitutional *freeze* on total state-wise seats requires a formal `Article 368` amendment, as seen in the 84th and 87th Amendments and the defeated 131st Bill).
16. **Union Territories (`Article 239A`):** Empowering Parliament by ordinary law to create a local legislature, a Council of Ministers, or both for specified Union Territories (currently Puducherry); `Article 239A(2)` explicitly removes such laws from `Article 368`. *(By contrast, the special constitutional status of NCT of Delhi rests inside `Articles 239AA–239AB`, inserted by the 69th Amendment.)*
17. **Fifth Schedule (`Paragraph 7`):** Empowering Parliament to amend, vary, or repeal any provision of the Fifth Schedule governing the administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes, expressly outside `Article 368`.
18. **Sixth Schedule (`Paragraph 21`):** Empowering Parliament to amend, vary, or repeal any provision of the Sixth Schedule governing the administration of Tribal Areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram, likewise expressly outside `Article 368`.

#### Track 2: Amendment by Special Majority of Parliament (Article 368(2))

The vast majority of constitutional provisions—including the entire **Part III (Fundamental Rights)**, **Part IV (Directive Principles of State Policy)**, **Part IVA (Fundamental Duties)**, Emergency Provisions, Anti-Defection provisions (excluding clauses affecting courts), and every other provision not falling within Track 1 or Track 3—require a **special majority** in each House of Parliament separately under `Article 368(2)`.

This special majority imposes a **dual arithmetic lock** that must be satisfied simultaneously in **both the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha**:

- **Condition A (Absolute Total-Strength Threshold):** A **majority of the total membership** of the House—meaning more than 50 per cent of the **sanctioned total strength** of the House, **irrespective of vacancies or absentees**.
- **Condition B (Supermajority Voting Threshold):** A majority of **not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting** (abstentions are excluded from "present and voting," though present abstainers do not reduce the Condition A denominator).

Strictly speaking, `Article 368` mandates the special majority for the **final passage** of the Bill. However, to prevent procedural anomalies and ensure broad consensus throughout, the Rules of Procedure of both Houses (such as Lok Sabha Rule 158) require this special majority at **all effective stages of the Bill**: when the motion that the Bill be taken into consideration is adopted, when each clause and schedule is voted upon during the clause-by-clause stage, and when the final motion that the Bill be passed is put to the House. (Individual *amendments* moved to specific clauses during debate may be carried by a simple majority, after which the amended clause itself must secure the special majority.)

> [!example]
> **Worked Arithmetic of Article 368(2) — Illustrated by the Defeat of the 131st Amendment Bill (17 April 2026):**
> Consider a Lok Sabha with a total membership of **543 seats** (meaning Condition A requires at least **272 affirmative votes**, even if 50 seats are vacant or 100 MPs are absent):
> - *Scenario 1 (Low turnout):* Suppose 360 MPs are present and voting. Two-thirds of 360 is 240. Even if 260 MPs vote "Ayes" (satisfying Condition B's 2/3rd mark), the Bill **FAILS** because 260 is below Condition A's absolute floor of **272**.
> - *Scenario 2 (High turnout — Real-World 2026 Case):* On **17 April 2026**, during the Lok Sabha division on the **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026** (seeking an 850-seat Lok Sabha and expedited delimitation/women's quota), **528 MPs** were present and voting. Condition A required **272 votes**, whereas Condition B required two-thirds of 528 = **352 votes**. The Bill received **298 votes in favour** and **230 votes against**—easily clearing Condition A (298 > 272), yet falling **54 votes short** of Condition B's 352 threshold. Speaker Om Birla declared the Bill **defeated**, forcing withdrawal of two linked ordinary Bills.

#### Track 3: Special Majority of Parliament Plus Ratification by Half the States (Proviso to Article 368(2))

Constitutional provisions that define the **federal structure**, the **distribution of legislative and executive authority**, the **higher judiciary**, or the **amending clause itself** cannot be altered unilaterally by Parliament. Under the **proviso to `Article 368(2)`**, any Bill seeking to make any change in these entrenched provisions must first pass **each House of Parliament by the Track 2 special majority**, and **before being presented to the President for assent**, must also be **ratified by resolutions passed by the Legislatures of not less than one-half of the States** (currently at least **14 out of 28 States**).

Crucially, State Legislatures ratify the amendment by a **simple majority**—a majority of the members of the House (or of *both* Houses in bicameral states) present and voting—and NOT by a special majority. Furthermore, the ratification requirement applies exclusively to **"States"**; Union Territory legislatures (Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu & Kashmir) are NOT counted in the state-ratification denominator.

Textually, the **proviso to `Article 368(2)`** enumerates the entrenched federal provisions across **five sub-clauses (a) to (e)**, which standard constitutional treatises (including M. Laxmikanth) unpack into **eight distinct federal items**:

1. **Election of the President and its manner (`Articles 54 and 55` — sub-clause (a)):** Because elected MLAs of the States participate alongside MPs in the Presidential electoral college, altering how the President is elected requires state consent (for example, the `70th Amendment, 1992` adding Delhi and Puducherry MLAs to `Art. 54` required state ratification).
2. **Extent of the executive power of the Union and of the States (`Articles 73 and 162` — sub-clause (a)):** Because executive power is co-extensive with legislative competence, altering Union or State executive boundaries directly shifts federal equilibrium.
3. **High Courts for Union Territories (`Article 241` — sub-clause (a)):** Safeguards judicial architecture across Union Territories and common High Courts.
4. **Goods and Services Tax (GST) Council (`Article 279A` — sub-clause (a), added by the `101st Amendment Act, 2016`):** To reassure States surrendering autonomous indirect taxation (VAT, octroi, entry tax) into the unified GST framework, Parliament entrenched the GST Council itself inside the proviso to `Article 368(2)`—ensuring the Centre can never unilaterally dismantle or dilute States' two-thirds voting weight in the Council without half the States agreeing!
5. **Supreme Court and High Courts (`Chapter IV of Part V` [Arts. 124–147] and `Chapter V of Part VI` [Arts. 214–231] — sub-clause (b)):** Protects India's integrated, independent judiciary from unilateral Parliamentary alteration (the `99th Amendment` creating NJAC obtained state ratification before being struck down on Basic Structure grounds; conversely, `Para 7` of the Tenth Schedule was struck down in *Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992 because it curtailed `Arts. 136` and `226` *without* state ratification!).
6. **Distribution of legislative powers between the Union and the States (`Chapter I of Part XI` [Arts. 245–255] — sub-clause (b)) and ANY of the Lists in the Seventh Schedule (sub-clause (c)):** Shields the Union, State, and Concurrent Lists from unilateral central encroachment—dramatically enforced in *Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah* (2021) regarding Entry 32 of the State List!
7. **Representation of States in Parliament (`Fourth Schedule` and linked allocation provisions — sub-clause (d)):** Protects States' seat allocations in the Council of States (Rajya Sabha) and parliamentary representation.
8. **Power and procedure of amending the Constitution (`Article 368` itself — sub-clause (e)):** Prevents Parliament from circumventing federal safeguards by first amending `Article 368` alone to delete the state ratification proviso!

| Dimension | Track 1: Simple Majority | Track 2: Special Majority | Track 3: Special Majority + State Ratification |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Constitutional Basis** | Specific Articles outside `Art. 368` | `Article 368(2)` main clause | Proviso to `Article 368(2)` |

| **Parliamentary Vote Needed** | >50% of members present & voting (each House) | >50% of total membership **AND** ≥ 2/3 present & voting (each House separately) | Same Track 2 dual threshold in each House separately |

### Step-by-Step Procedural Rules and Judicial Enforcement of Ratification

Passing a formal Constitutional Amendment Bill under `Article 368` follows a strict procedural architecture that departs sharply from ordinary legislation and Money Bills at every critical milestone:

```flow
# Step-by-Step Passage of a Constitutional Amendment Bill (Article 368)
Stage 1: Introduction | Initiated in EITHER House of Parliament (never a State Legislature) by a Minister OR a Private Member · NO prior Presidential permission required
Stage 2: First House Vote | Passed by Special Majority: >50% of Total Membership AND ≥ 2/3 of Members Present & Voting
Stage 3: Second House Vote | Passed INDEPENDENTLY by the same Special Majority · NO Joint Sitting (Art. 108) permitted if Houses disagree
Federal Provision? (Proviso to Art. 368(2)) | Track 2: Sent directly to President || Track 3: Sent to State Legislatures — ≥ Half the States must ratify by Simple Majority (no deadline)
Stage 4: Mandatory Presidential Assent | President MUST give assent (24th Amendment, 1971) — CANNOT withhold assent or return the Bill for reconsideration
Stage 5: Enactment & Review | Becomes a Constitutional Amendment Act upon assent; subject to Judicial Review on Procedural Compliance (Rajendra N. Shah) & Basic Structure (Kesavananda)
```

Every single procedural rule carries sharp Prelims significance:

1. **Initiation Exclusively in Parliament (Either House):** A Constitutional Amendment Bill can be introduced in **either the Lok Sabha or the Rajya Sabha**, never in a State Legislature. Unlike Money Bills (`Art. 110`), the Rajya Sabha enjoys **completely co-equal power** with the Lok Sabha on constitutional amendments.
2. **Minister or Private Member:** The Bill may be moved either by a **Union Minister** (Government Bill) or by a **Private Member** (any MP who is not a minister). While no Private Member's Constitutional Amendment Bill has ever been enacted into law, Parliament has debated several, and the constitutional right of introduction is unrestricted.
3. **No Prior Recommendation of the President:** Unlike `Article 3` state-reorganisation Bills, Money Bills (`Art. 117(1)`), or Financial Bills Category I, a Constitutional Amendment Bill requires **no prior permission or recommendation of the President** for introduction.
4. **Separate Passage by Special Majority & Absolute Bar on Joint Sitting:** Both Houses must pass the Bill in identical form by the dual special majority. Because **`Article 108`** applies only to ordinary Bills and Financial Bills, **no Joint Sitting** can be convened to resolve a deadlock between the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha over a Constitutional Amendment Bill. History illustrates the teeth of this rule: the **24th Amendment Bill, 1970** (Privy Purses abolition) fell short by a fraction of a vote in the Rajya Sabha; the **64th and 65th Amendment Bills, 1989** (Panchayats and Municipalities under Rajiv Gandhi) were defeated in the Rajya Sabha; key restorative clauses of the **45th Bill (44th Act, 1978)** were rejected in the Rajya Sabha; and most recently, the **131st Amendment Bill, 2026** failed in the Lok Sabha itself on **17 April 2026**.
5. **State Ratification Rules (No Time Limit, Simple Majority):** Where the proviso to `Article 368(2)` applies, the Bill is circulated to State Legislatures after passage by both Houses of Parliament. Once **at least half the States** pass ratifying resolutions by **simple majority**, the constitutional condition is satisfied—even if the remaining States reject the Bill or take no action. The Constitution prescribes **no time limit** within which States must ratify or reject a Bill, and remains silent on whether a State can withdraw its ratification once communicated (though constitutional practice treats ratification as final once conveyed).
6. **Mandatory Presidential Assent (`24th Amendment Act, 1971`):** Before 1971, `Article 368` simply stated that the Bill *"shall be presented to the President for his assent and upon such assent being given to the Bill, the Constitution shall stand amended,"* leaving theoretical ambiguity over whether the President could exercise a veto. The **`24th Amendment Act, 1971`** eliminated all ambiguity by substituting the mandatory command: **"the President shall give his assent."** Thus, on a Constitutional Amendment Bill, the President enjoys **neither absolute veto, nor suspensive veto (return for reconsideration), nor pocket veto**.
7. **Bill Number vs Act Number Distinction:** Because lapsed, withdrawn, or defeated Amendment Bills still consume sequential Bill numbers, the **Amendment Bill number is always higher than the resulting Amendment Act number**. For instance, the **122nd Amendment Bill** became the **`101st Amendment Act`** (GST), the **123rd Bill** became the **`102nd Act`** (NCBC), the **124th Bill** became the **`103rd Act`** (EWS), the **126th Bill** became the **`104th Act`**, the **127th Bill** became the **`105th Act`**, and the **128th Bill** became the **`106th Act`** (Women's Reservation).

```compare
# Constitutional Amendment Bill vs Ordinary Bill vs Money Bill
Constitutional Amendment Bill (Art. 368) | Ordinary Bill (Arts. 107–108) | Money Bill (Art. 110)
Where Introduced :: Either House of Parliament | Either House of Parliament | Lok Sabha ONLY
Who Can Introduce :: Minister OR Private Member | Minister OR Private Member | Minister ONLY
Prior Presidential Permission :: NOT required | NOT required (except Art. 3 Bills) | MANDATORY
Majority Required :: Special Majority (>50% total + 2/3 present & voting) in each House | Simple Majority (>50% present & voting) | Simple Majority in Lok Sabha
Rajya Sabha Powers :: Co-equal — can reject or amend; blocks passage | Co-equal — can amend or detain up to 6 months | Subordinate — cannot amend or reject; must return within 14 days
Joint Sitting (Art. 108) :: NOT permitted | Permitted on deadlock | NOT permitted
Presidential Veto :: NONE — assent mandatory (24th Amdt, 1971) | Assent, withhold, or return once (Art. 111) | Assent or withhold (cannot return for reconsideration)
```

#### Navigating the Four Types of Majorities in the Constitution

A perennial source of confusion in Prelims MCQs is conflating Article 368's special majority with other constitutional voting thresholds. Every aspirant should internalise all **four** constitutional majorities side by side:

| Majority Type | Exact Formula | Key Constitutional Applications |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Simple (Functional) Majority** | >50% of members **present and voting** | Ordinary Bills, Money Bills, Financial Bills, No-Confidence & Confidence Motions, President's Rule (`Art. 356`), Financial Emergency (`Art. 360`), Track 1 amendments (`Arts. 2–4, 169`), State ratification of Art. 368 Bills |
| **2. Effective Majority** | >50% of **effective strength** (Total strength minus actual vacancies) | Removal of Vice-President in Rajya Sabha (`Art. 67(b)` — "all the then members"), Deputy Chairman of RS (`Art. 90(c)`), Speaker/Deputy Speaker of LS (`Art. 94(c)`) & State Assemblies (`Art. 179(c)`) |
| **3. Special Majority — Type A (Pure 2/3rd)** | ≥ 2/3 of members **present and voting** (NO >50% total-strength floor) | Rajya Sabha resolution enabling Parliament to legislate on a State List subject in national interest (`Article 249`) or create a new All-India Service (`Article 312`) |
| **4. Special Majority — Type B (`Article 368(2)`)** | >50% of **total membership** **AND** ≥ 2/3 of members **present and voting** | Constitutional Amendments (`Art. 368`), removal of SC/HC Judges (`Arts. 124(4), 217`), CAG (`Art. 148`), CEC (`Art. 324`), approval/continuation of National Emergency (`Art. 352`), State Assembly resolution under `Art. 169` |
| **5. Super-Special Majority (`Article 61`)** | ≥ 2/3 of the **total membership** of each House (strictest threshold in the Constitution!) | **Impeachment of the President of India** (`Article 61`) ONLY |

> [!prelims]
> **Five Classic UPSC Prelims Traps on Article 368:**
> 1. **Private Members CAN introduce** a Constitutional Amendment Bill—and need **no prior permission** of the President (UPSC Prelims 2013 & 2022 trap!).
> 2. **No Joint Sitting** is permitted for a Constitutional Amendment Bill—even if the Lok Sabha passes it unanimously and the Rajya Sabha rejects it (UPSC Prelims 2013).
> 3. **State ratification needs ONLY a Simple Majority** in the State Legislature, NOT a special majority—and Union Territory legislatures do not count toward the "one-half of the States" threshold.
> 4. **Mandatory Presidential Assent** was NOT in the original 1950 text; it was inserted by the **`24th Amendment Act, 1971`**.
> 5. **Enlarging vs Curtailing SC Jurisdiction:** Parliament can *confer additional* Union List jurisdiction on the Supreme Court by **simple majority (`Art. 138`)**, whereas *curtailing* or modifying core SC/HC jurisdiction under Chapter IV of Part V / Chapter V of Part VI triggers **Track 3 (state ratification)**!

#### Judicial Enforcement of the Procedural Federal Lock: From *Sajjan Singh* to *Rajendra N. Shah* (2021)

Can Parliament sidestep state ratification by drafting an amendment that leaves the text of the Seventh Schedule or judicial Articles superficially untouched while hollowing out their substance? The Supreme Court's jurisprudence draws a sharp line between **incidental effects** and **substantive/direct alterations**:

- **Incidental Effect (*Shankari Prasad*, 1951 & *Sajjan Singh*, 1965):** Challengers argued that adding `Articles 31A–31B` and expanding the Ninth Schedule indirectly narrowed High Courts' `Article 226` writ jurisdiction, thereby requiring state ratification. Applying the **"pith and substance"** doctrine, the Supreme Court held that because the amendments' true objective was agrarian reform under Part III and their impact on `Article 226` was merely *incidental*, state ratification was NOT mandatory.
- **Direct Ouster of Judicial Review (*Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu*, 1992):** The **`52nd Amendment Act, 1985`** inserted the Tenth Schedule (Anti-Defection Law) without obtaining state ratification. Its **Paragraph 7** explicitly barred the jurisdiction of all courts—including the Supreme Court (`Art. 136`) and High Courts (`Arts. 226, 227`)—over disqualification disputes. A five-judge bench unanimously held that Paragraph 7 directly curtailed the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts and therefore **imperatively required ratification by half the States** under the proviso to `Article 368(2)`. Because no ratification was obtained, Paragraph 7 was struck down as procedurally unconstitutional—though by a **3–2** majority (Justices M.N. Venkatachaliah, K. Jayachandra Reddy, and S.C. Agrawal; Justices L.M. Sharma and J.S. Verma dissenting), the Court applied the **doctrine of severability** to save the rest of the Tenth Schedule.
- **Substantive "Change in Effect" on the State List (*Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah*, 20 July 2021):** The Supreme Court elevated procedural federal protection to a new landmark when adjudicating the **`97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011`**, which added **Part IXB (`Arts. 243ZH–243ZT`)** imposing detailed uniformity rules on co-operative societies without state ratification.

> [!case]
> ***Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah* (20 July 2021) — 3-Judge Bench (Justices R.F. Nariman, K.M. Joseph, B.R. Gavai):**
> - **Constitutional Issue:** "Co-operative societies" falls exclusively under **Entry 32 of the State List (List II)**. Parliament enacted the `97th Amendment, 2011` inserting Part IXB (prescribing board sizes, reserved seats, term limits, supersession caps, and audit norms for all co-operatives) without obtaining ratification from half the States under the proviso to `Article 368(2)`.
> - **Unanimous Holding on Ratification (3–0):** Even though the 97th Amendment did not formally edit a single word of Entry 32 of List II, Part IXB substantially fenced in and curtailed States' exclusive legislative competence over co-operative societies. A **substantive "change in effect"** in a Seventh Schedule List triggers the mandatory ratification proviso of `Article 368(2)` just as surely as a textual alteration. Consequently, Part IXB was **unconstitutional insofar as it applied to State co-operative societies**.
> - **Severability Split (2–1):** Justices R.F. Nariman and B.R. Gavai applied severability to **preserve Part IXB solely for multi-State co-operative societies** (which fall under Union List Entry 44), while Justice K.M. Joseph dissented, arguing that an unratified amendment affecting a federal clause is *stillborn* and non-severable in full.
> - **What Survived Untouched:** Both **`Article 19(1)(c)`** (Fundamental Right to form co-operative societies) and **`Article 43B`** (DPSP promoting co-operatives) remained completely valid and unaffected!

### Critical Evaluation of the Amendment Procedure

Constitutional commentators—including K.C. Wheare, Sir Ivor Jennings, and Indian legal scholars—have identified **six major procedural criticisms** of `Article 368`:

1. **No Special Constitutional Convention or Constituent Assembly:** Unlike the United States (where Article V provides an alternative route of a national Constitutional Convention called by two-thirds of the states), India vests constituent power in the **ordinary Parliament**. Critics worry that elected legislators acting simultaneously as ordinary lawmakers and constituent authority may blur partisan expediency with constitutional permanence—as witnessed vividly during the 1975–77 Emergency.
2. **States Have No Power to Initiate Amendments:** Except for passing a State Assembly resolution requesting Parliament to create or abolish a Legislative Council under `Article 169` (which Parliament is not even bound to enact!), State Legislatures **cannot initiate** any constitutional amendment. In the USA, two-thirds of states can compel Congress to call a convention; in Switzerland, citizens themselves possess the constitutional *initiative*.
3. **Major Bulk Amendable by Parliament Alone:** Outside the eight entrenched items in the proviso to `Article 368(2)`, Parliament can unilaterally amend the overwhelming majority of the Constitution—including Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, Emergency Powers, and even the boundaries or very existence of States (`Art. 3`)—without consulting State Legislatures.
4. **No Time Frame for Ratification & Silence on Rescission:** `Article 368` prescribes **no deadline** within which States must ratify or reject an amendment, nor does it clarify whether a State Legislature can **withdraw its ratification** before the half-states threshold is reached—a lacuna that has occasionally generated federal ambiguity.
5. **Absence of a Joint Sitting Mechanism:** While barring `Article 108` protects federalism and minority consensus in the Rajya Sabha, critics note that an obstructive upper House can veto even broadly backed reforms (as happened in 1989 to the 64th/65th Panchayati Raj Bills, delaying grassroots constitutionalisation by three years).
6. **Sketchy Procedural Drafting Leaving Gaps for Litigation:** `Article 368` leaves several procedural questions unaddressed—such as whether an amendment Bill can be referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee (settled affirmatively by parliamentary convention), how indirect federal impacts trigger ratification (finally settled only in *Rajendra N. Shah*, 2021), and whether there are substantive limits on amending power (settled only by *Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973).

Yet on balance, **Dr B.R. Ambedkar's** defence in the Constituent Assembly has largely been vindicated by seventy-six years of constitutional history. Rejecting the American referendum/convention model as too rigid for a newly independent developing democracy and rejecting total flexibility as dangerous to federal cohesion, the framers built a **self-correcting three-tier system**. When combined with the judiciary's dual locks—the substantive **Basic Structure Doctrine** (*Kesavananda*, 1973) and the enforceable **federal ratification shield** (*Rajendra N. Shah*, 2021)—`Article 368` has preserved India's constitutional continuity across wars, coalition eras, economic liberalisation, and majoritarian swings while still empowering Parliament to block unconsented rewrites, as demonstrated dramatically by the Lok Sabha defeat of the 131st Amendment Bill in April 2026.

### Phase I: Nation-Building, Agrarian Reform, and Territorial Integration (1st to 21st Amendments, 1951–1967)

Within fifteen months of the Constitution's commencement, urgent friction emerged between socio-economic reform legislation and early High Court and Supreme Court rulings on free speech, caste reservations, and zamindari abolition.

- **`1st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951`** *(Unique Prelims Fact: Passed by the **Provisional Parliament**—the former Constituent Assembly sitting as unicameral legislature before the first general elections of 1951–52!)*:
  - Added **`Article 15(4)`** enabling special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes, SCs, and STs—reversing *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* (1951).
  - Inserted **`Article 31A`**, **`Article 31B`**, and the **`Ninth Schedule`** (initially containing 13 land-reform laws) to insulate zamindari abolition from judicial invalidation after *Kameshwar Singh v. State of Bihar* (1950).
  - Inserted three additional grounds of restriction in **`Article 19(2)`**—**public order**, **friendly relations with foreign states**, and **incitement to an offence**—while adding the word **"reasonable"** before restrictions (responding to *Romesh Thappar* and *Brij Bhushan*, 1950).
  - Amended **`Article 19(6)`** to shield State trading and nationalisation/state monopolies from freedom-of-trade challenges.
- **`2nd Amendment Act, 1952`:** Removed the original constitutional upper ceiling of 7,50,000 population per Lok Sabha constituency (`Article 81`), readjusting the scale of representation.
- **`4th Amendment Act, 1955`:** Reversed *State of West Bengal v. Bela Banerjee* (1954) by making the **adequacy of compensation** for compulsory property acquisition non-justiciable (`Art. 31(2)`), clarified that deprivation without transfer of ownership to the State does not trigger compensation, expanded `Article 31A`, and placed additional statutes in the Ninth Schedule.
- **`7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956`** *(Companion to the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 based on the Fazl Ali Commission)*:
  - Abolished the four-fold classification of States (**Parts A, B, C, and D**) and **repealed Part VII**, reorganising India into **14 States and 6 Union Territories**.
  - Added the proviso to **`Article 153`** enabling the **same person to be appointed Governor for two or more States**, and amended **`Article 231`** allowing a **common High Court** for two or more States and/or Union Territories.
  - Provided for **Additional and Acting Judges** in High Courts (`Article 224`) and extended High Court jurisdiction to Union Territories.
  - Inserted **`Article 350A`** (facilities for primary education in mother tongue for linguistic minorities) and **`Article 350B`** (Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities).
- **`9th Amendment Act, 1960`:** Enacted pursuant to the Supreme Court's advisory opinion in *In Re: Berubari Union* (1960) to transfer **Berubari Union** (West Bengal) to Pakistan under the 1958 Nehru–Noon Agreement, establishing that ceding Indian territory requires an `Article 368` amendment.
- **Territorial Integration Trio — `10th (1961)`, `12th (1962)`, and `14th (1962)` Amendments:** Incorporated former Portuguese and French enclaves into the Indian Union—**Dadra and Nagar Haveli** as a UT (`10th`), **Goa, Daman and Diu** as a UT (`12th`), and **Puducherry** as a UT (`14th`). The `14th Amendment` also inserted **`Article 239A`**, empowering Parliament by simple law to create legislatures and Councils of Ministers for specified Union Territories.
- **`11th Amendment Act, 1961`:** Changed the mode of electing the **Vice-President** from a joint meeting of both Houses to an **electoral college** comprising all members (elected and nominated) of both Houses (`Article 66`), and clarified (`Art. 71(4)`) that Presidential or Vice-Presidential elections cannot be challenged on the ground of any vacancy in the electoral college.
- **`13th Amendment Act, 1962`:** Conferred full statehood on **Nagaland** and inserted **`Article 371A`** protecting Naga religious/social practices, customary law, and ownership of land and resources.
- **`15th Amendment Act, 1963`:** Raised the **retirement age of High Court judges from 60 to 62 years** (`Art. 217`), empowered High Courts to issue writs beyond their territorial limits if the cause of action arises wholly or partly within their territory (`Art. 226(2)`), and authorised the President to decide High Court judge age disputes in consultation with the CJI (`Art. 217(3)`).
- **`16th Amendment Act, 1963` ("Anti-Secession Amendment"):** Acting on the Committee on National Integration and Regionalism (C.P. Ramaswami Aiyar), added **"the sovereignty and integrity of India"** as a ground for reasonable restrictions under **`Articles 19(2), 19(3), and 19(4)`** and inserted the same pledge into the **Third Schedule** oaths for candidates, legislators, ministers, and judges.
- **`17th Amendment Act, 1964`:** Inserted the second proviso to `Article 31A(1)` guaranteeing **market-value compensation** when land under personal cultivation within the statutory ceiling limit is acquired, and inserted **44 more land-reform laws** into the Ninth Schedule (directly sparking *Sajjan Singh*, 1965 and *Golaknath*, 1967!).
- **`18th Amendment Act, 1966`:** Added Explanations to `Article 3` clarifying that Parliament's power to form a new State includes uniting a part of any State or Union Territory with any other State or Union Territory (facilitating the bifurcation of Punjab into Punjab, Haryana, and Chandigarh).
- **`21st Amendment Act, 1967`:** Added **Sindhi** as the 15th language in the **Eighth Schedule** (the first expansion of the original 14 languages).

### Phase II: Judicial Confrontation, Centralisation, and the Emergency Era (24th to 42nd Amendments, 1971–1976)

Between 1967 and 1976, Indian constitutionalism witnessed its fiercest confrontation between parliamentary assertion and judicial review—triggered by *Golaknath* (1967), the *Bank Nationalisation* ruling (*R.C. Cooper*, 1970), and the *Privy Purses* ruling (*Madhav Rao Scindia*, 1970), culminating in the Emergency of June 1975–March 1977.

- **`24th Amendment Act, 1971`:** Directly reversed *Golaknath* (1967) by asserting Parliament's power to amend any part of the Constitution including Fundamental Rights (`Art. 368(1)`), insulating Article 368 amendments from `Article 13` (`Arts. 13(4)` & `368(3)`), updating the heading of `Article 368`, and making **Presidential assent mandatory** under `Article 368(2)`.
- **`25th Amendment Act, 1971`:** Curtailed the Fundamental Right to Property by replacing the word *"compensation"* with *"amount"* in `Article 31(2)`, and inserted **`Article 31C`** declaring that no law giving effect to Directive Principles under **`Articles 39(b)` and `39(c)`** could be voided for violating `Articles 14, 19, or 31`—adding a second limb barring judicial review of any legislative declaration to that effect (the second limb was struck down in *Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973).
- **`26th Amendment Act, 1971`:** Reversed *Madhav Rao Scindia* (1970) by abolishing the **privy purses** and official recognition/privileges of former rulers of Princely States (repealed `Arts. 291 & 362`; inserted `Art. 363A`).
- **`31st Amendment Act, 1973`:** Increased the maximum elected strength of the **Lok Sabha from 525 to 545** (raising State seats from 500 to 525 while lowering UT seats from 25 to 20) to reflect population growth recorded in the 1971 Census.
- **`33rd Amendment Act, 1974`:** Amended `Articles 101` and `190` so that the Speaker or Chairman may refuse to accept an MP's or MLA's **resignation** if satisfied, after inquiry, that it is **not voluntary or genuine** (preventing coerced resignations).
- **`35th (1974)` and `36th (1975)` Amendments (Sikkim Integration):** The `35th Amendment` ended Sikkim's protectorate status and invented the unique category of **"Associate State"** by inserting `Article 2A` and the original **Tenth Schedule**. A year later, after a referendum in Sikkim, the `36th Amendment` admitted **Sikkim as the 22nd full-fledged State** of India, inserting **`Article 371F`** (special provisions for Sikkim) and repealing `Article 2A` and the original Tenth Schedule.
- **`38th Amendment Act, 1975`:** Passed during the Emergency, made the President's and Governors' satisfaction in promulgating **Ordinances (`Arts. 123, 213`)** and declaring **National, State, and Financial Emergencies (`Arts. 352, 356, 360`)** final and non-justiciable (clauses largely repealed by the 44th Amendment), while enabling simultaneous Emergency proclamations on different grounds.
- **`39th Amendment Act, 1975`:** Enacted days before the Supreme Court was to hear Prime Minister Indira Gandhi's election appeal, inserted **`Article 329A`** placing election disputes involving the **President, Vice-President, Prime Minister, and Lok Sabha Speaker** beyond the jurisdiction of ordinary courts and retroactively validating Indira Gandhi's 1971 Rae Bareli election—prompting the Supreme Court to strike down **`Article 329A(4)`** in ***Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975)** (the entire Article was later repealed by the 44th Amendment).

#### Anatomy of the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 ("The Mini-Constitution")

Enacted during the extended fifth Lok Sabha on the recommendations of the **Swaran Singh Committee** (assented 18 December 1976; effective largely from 3 January 1977), the **`42nd Amendment Act`** spanned 59 clauses and remains the most sweeping revision in Indian constitutional history—aptly termed a **"Mini-Constitution"**:

1. **Preamble Altered (Only Time in History):** Added three words—**`Socialist`**, **`Secular`**, and **`Integrity`** (*"unity of the Nation"* became *"unity and integrity of the Nation"*).
2. **Fundamental Duties Added:** Inserted **`Part IVA`** (`Article 51A`) codifying ten Fundamental Duties of citizens (modeled on the USSR Constitution).
3. **Directive Principles Expanded:** Added three new Articles—**`Article 39A`** (equal justice and free legal aid), **`Article 43A`** (participation of workers in management of industries), and **`Article 48A`** (protection and improvement of environment, forests, and wildlife)—and modified **`Article 39(f)`** to guarantee children opportunities for healthy development in freedom and dignity.
4. **DPSP Primacy over Fundamental Rights (`Art. 31C`):** Expanded `Article 31C` to shield laws implementing **ANY Directive Principle in Part IV** (not merely `39(b)` and `39(c)`) from challenge under `Articles 14, 19, and 31`—**struck down 4–1 in *Minerva Mills* (1980)**.
5. **Anti-National Activities (`Art. 31D`):** Empowered Parliament to legislate against "anti-national activities" and ban "anti-national associations" overriding `Articles 14, 19, and 31`—**repealed by the `43rd Amendment, 1977`**.
6. **Unlimited Amending Power (`Arts. 368(4) & 368(5)`):** Declared that no constitutional amendment could be questioned in any court on any ground (`368(4)`) and that there shall be no limitation whatsoever on Parliament's constituent power (`368(5)`)—**struck down unanimously in *Minerva Mills* (1980)**.
7. **Executive Binding on President (`Art. 74(1)`):** Explicitly bound the President to act in accordance with the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister.
8. **Legislature Terms, Seat Freeze & Quorum:** Extended the tenure of the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies from **5 to 6 years** (`Arts. 83, 172`); froze Lok Sabha and State Assembly seat allocations on the **1971 Census** until **2001**; abolished constitutional quorum requirements (`Arts. 100, 189`); and authorised Parliament to evolve its own privileges without British House of Commons reference.
9. **Severe Curtailment of Judicial Review:** Inserted `Art. 32A` (barring SC from examining validity of state laws under Art. 32), `Art. 131A` (giving SC exclusive jurisdiction over central laws), `Art. 144A` (requiring a minimum 7-judge bench and 2/3rd majority in SC to invalidate a central law), `Art. 226A` (barring HCs from examining central laws), and `Art. 228A` (requiring special 2/3rd majority in HCs to invalidate state laws)—**ALL repealed by the `43rd Amendment, 1977`**! (It also inserted `Article 139A` on transfer of certain cases to the SC, which was retained.)
10. **Tribunals Constitutionalised (`Part XIVA`):** Inserted **`Article 323A`** (Administrative Tribunals) and **`Article 323B`** (Tribunals for tax, land, labour, election, and other matters)—retained, though their exclusion of High Court review was later invalidated in *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997).
11. **Federal Rebalancing — Five Subjects Shifted from State List to Concurrent List:** Transferred **(i) Education**, **(ii) Forests**, **(iii) Weights and Measures** (except establishment of standards), **(iv) Protection of wild animals and birds**, and **(v) Administration of justice; constitution and organisation of all courts except the Supreme Court and High Courts** from List II to List III! It also inserted **`Article 257A`** allowing the Centre to unilaterally deploy Union armed forces in any State to deal with grave law-and-order situations (repealed by the 44th Amendment).
12. **Emergency & All-India Services:** Enabled National Emergency to be proclaimed in a **part of India** rather than only the whole country (`Art. 352`—retained); doubled the one-time approval period for President's Rule (`Art. 356`) from **6 months to 1 year** (rolled back by 44th); authorised creation of an **All-India Judicial Service** (`Art. 312`—retained in text, still uncreated); and curtailed civil servants' disciplinary safeguard under `Article 311(2)` by abolishing the second show-cause notice on proposed penalty (retained).

### Phase III: Democratic Restoration and Safeguards (43rd and 44th Amendments, 1977–1978)

Following the 1977 general elections, the Janata Party government enacted two restorative statutes to dismantle the authoritarian excesses of the Emergency while retaining benign social provisions of the 42nd Amendment (such as the Preamble words, Fundamental Duties, DPSP additions, and Concurrent List transfers).

- **`43rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1977`:** Performed rapid judicial surgery by repealing **`Articles 31D, 32A, 131A, 144A, 226A, and 228A`**—restoring the Supreme Court's and High Courts' full jurisdiction to review both central and state laws without artificial two-thirds judicial supermajorities, and deleting the "anti-national activities" clause.
- **`44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978`** *(assented 30 April 1979; largely effective 20 June 1979)*:
  - **Restored 5-Year Legislative Terms & Quorum:** Returned the tenure of the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies from 6 years back to **5 years** (`Arts. 83, 172`), restored parliamentary quorum rules (`Arts. 100, 189`), deleted reference to the British House of Commons in `Arts. 105 & 194`, and inserted **`Article 361A`** granting constitutional protection to true media reporting of Parliamentary and State Legislative proceedings.
  - **Presidential Reconsideration Power (`Art. 74(1)` proviso):** Empowered the President to send back Cabinet advice **once** for reconsideration, while remaining bound by the advice tendered *after* such reconsideration.
  - **Right to Property Shifted Out of Part III:** Omitted **`Article 19(1)(f)`** and **`Article 31`** from Fundamental Rights and inserted **`Article 300A`** in Part XII, making property a legal/constitutional right (while inserting `Art. 30(1A)` to safeguard compensation for minority educational institutions).
  - **Ironclad Emergency Safeguards (`Arts. 352, 356, 358, 359`):**
    - Replaced the vague ground **"internal disturbance"** with **"armed rebellion"** in `Article 352`.
    - Required a **written recommendation of the Cabinet** before the President can proclaim National Emergency—inserting the word **"Cabinet"** into the Constitution for the first and only time (`Article 352(3)`).
    - Shortened Parliamentary approval of National Emergency from 2 months to **1 month**, raised the required vote from simple to **special majority**, mandated renewal every **6 months**, and empowered the Lok Sabha to revoke an Emergency by simple majority (with 1/10th of MPs able to force a special sitting).
    - Restricted automatic suspension of `Article 19` (`Art. 358`) strictly to **war or external aggression** (NOT armed rebellion).
    - Made **`Articles 20` and `21` absolutely non-suspendable** even during a National Emergency (`Article 359`)—permanently overruling the dark precedent of *ADM Jabalpur* (1976).
    - Rolled back one-time `Article 356` President's Rule duration from 1 year to **6 months**, and forbade extension beyond **1 year** (up to 3 years) unless *both* a National Emergency is in operation AND the Election Commission certifies elections cannot be held.
  - **Restored Judicial Review & Repealed Central Force Deployment:** Deleted the 38th Amendment's non-justiciability clauses on Ordinances (`Arts. 123, 213`) and Emergencies (`Arts. 352, 356, 360`), repealed `Article 329A` (restoring SC jurisdiction over Presidential/VP election disputes under `Art. 71`), repealed `Article 257A`, and added DPSP **`Article 38(2)`** (minimising inequalities in income, status, facilities, and opportunities).
  - **Unnotified Clause (`Art. 22(4)`):** Provided for reducing maximum preventive detention without Advisory Board clearance from 3 months to **2 months**—a clause that successive Central Governments have **never notified into force** (*A.K. Roy*, 1982).

> [!remember]
> **Critical UPSC Trap — What the 44th Amendment Did NOT Undo:**
> Aspirants routinely assume that the 44th Amendment repealed *all* authoritarian provisions of the 42nd Amendment. **False!** Although the Janata government's original 45th Amendment Bill sought to repeal **`Articles 368(4)–(5)`**, roll back the expanded **`Article 31C`**, define "secular" and "socialist," and introduce a national **referendum** for basic-feature amendments, those specific clauses were **defeated in the Congress-majority Rajya Sabha**. Consequently, `Articles 368(4)–(5)` and the widened `Article 31C` survived the 44th Amendment and had to be struck down by the Supreme Court in ***Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980)**! Likewise, the **five subjects shifted to the Concurrent List**, the **Preamble words**, **Fundamental Duties**, and **Part XIVA Tribunals** were consciously retained.

### Phase IV: Political Reforms, Federal Empowerment, and Social Justice Families (52nd to 100th Amendments, 1985–2015)

From the mid-1980s through 2015, constitutional amendments clustered into six distinct thematic families that every Prelims and Mains candidate must command effortlessly:

#### 1. Political Integrity, Universal Franchise, and National Capital Governance
- **`52nd Amendment Act, 1985` (Anti-Defection Law):** Inserted the new **`Tenth Schedule`** and amended `Articles 101, 102, 190, and 191` to disqualify MPs and MLAs who defect from their political parties (its Paragraph 7 judicial bar was later struck down in *Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992).
- **`61st Amendment Act, 1988`** *(effective 28 March 1989)*: Amended **`Article 326`** to lower the minimum voting age for Lok Sabha and State Assembly elections from **21 to 18 years**, expanding India's electorate by tens of millions of young citizens.
- **`69th Amendment Act, 1991` & `70th Amendment Act, 1992`:** The `69th Amendment` gave Delhi special status as the **National Capital Territory (NCT) of Delhi** with a 70-member elected Legislative Assembly and a Council of Ministers capped at 10 per cent (7 ministers) via **`Articles 239AA and 239AB`**, excluding public order, police, and land from Assembly competence. The `70th Amendment` included the elected MLAs of **NCT of Delhi and Puducherry** in the **Presidential Electoral College (`Article 54`)**.
- **`91st Amendment Act, 2003`:** Enacted sweeping governance reforms: (i) capped the size of Union and State Councils of Ministers at **15 per cent** of the total strength of the Lok Sabha or State Legislative Assembly (`Arts. 75(1A), 164(1A)`), with a **minimum of 12 ministers** (including Chief Minister) for smaller States; (ii) **deleted Paragraph 3 ("split" by one-third of legislature party)** from the Tenth Schedule so that only a **two-thirds merger** protects defectors; and (iii) barred disqualified defectors from being appointed ministers or holding any remunerative political post until re-elected (`Arts. 75(1B), 164(1B), 361B`).

#### 2. Grassroots Democracy, Elementary Education, and Co-operatives
- **`73rd Amendment Act, 1992` & `74th Amendment Act, 1992`** *(effective 24 April 1993 and 1 June 1993 respectively)*: Revolutionised Indian federalism into a **three-tier democracy**—the `73rd` added **`Part IX`** (`Arts. 243–243O`) and the **`Eleventh Schedule`** (**29 functional items**) for **Panchayati Raj Institutions**, while the `74th` added **`Part IXA`** (`Arts. 243P–243ZG`) and the **`Twelfth Schedule`** (**18 functional items**) for **Municipalities**, mandating regular five-year elections, State Election Commissions, State Finance Commissions, and at least one-third reservation for women.
- **`86th Amendment Act, 2002`:** Transformed elementary education across three parts of the Constitution simultaneously—inserted **`Article 21A`** making free and compulsory education for children aged **6 to 14 years** a Fundamental Right (operationalised by the RTE Act, 2009), substituted **`Article 45`** to direct early childhood care and education for children **below age 6**, and added the 11th Fundamental Duty **`Article 51A(k)`** obliging parents/guardians to provide educational opportunities to children aged 6–14.
- **`97th Amendment Act, 2011`:** Elevated co-operative societies to constitutional status by adding *"or co-operative societies"* in **`Article 19(1)(c)`**, inserting DPSP **`Article 43B`**, and adding **`Part IXB`** (`Arts. 243ZH–243ZT`)—the last of which was partially struck down in ***Rajendra N. Shah* (2021)** for State co-operatives due to lack of state ratification, surviving only for multi-State co-operatives.

#### 3. Constitutional Commissions and Eighth Schedule Languages
- **Commissions Progression:** The **`65th Amendment Act, 1990`** replaced the single Special Officer under `Article 338` with a multi-member **National Commission for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes**. The **`89th Amendment Act, 2003`** (effective 2004) bifurcated this body into two separate constitutional commissions—**NCSC (`Article 338`)** and **NCST (`Article 338A`)**—before the **`102nd Amendment Act, 2018`** added the third sibling, **NCBC (`Article 338B`)**.
- **Eighth Schedule Language Additions (14 → 22):** Building on the **`21st (1967)`** (Sindhi), the **`71st Amendment Act, 1992`** added **Konkani, Manipuri, and Nepali** (taking the count to 18), the **`92nd Amendment Act, 2003`** added **Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, and Santhali** (reaching the current **22 scheduled languages**), and the **`96th Amendment Act, 2011`** changed the spelling of *"Oriya"* to **"Odia"**. (Separately, the **`58th Amendment Act, 1987`** inserted **`Article 394A`** authorising an authoritative text of the Constitution in Hindi.)

#### 4. Reservation Amendments and the Delimitation Freeze
- **SC/ST Promotion Quartet & Private-College Quota:** Responding to *Indra Sawhney* (1992) and consequential-seniority rulings, Parliament enacted four interlocking amendments:
  - **`77th (1995)`:** Inserted **`Article 16(4A)`** permitting reservation in **promotions** for SCs and STs.
  - **`81st (2000)`:** Inserted **`Article 16(4B)`** treating unfilled **carry-forward backlog vacancies** as a separate class excluded from the **50 per cent ceiling** on current-year reservations.
  - **`82nd (2000)`:** Added a proviso to **`Article 335`** allowing relaxation of qualifying marks or evaluation standards for SCs/STs in promotional examinations.
  - **`85th (2001)`:** Amended `Article 16(4A)` to grant **consequential seniority** to SC/ST government servants promoted via reservation, retrospectively from 17 June 1995. *(All four were upheld subject to quantifiable-data safeguards in M. Nagaraj, 2006.)*
  - **`93rd (2005)`:** Inserted **`Article 15(5)`** enabling reservation for SEBCs, SCs, and STs in admissions to educational institutions including **private unaided institutions**, while **expressly exempting minority educational institutions** (upheld in *Ashoka Kumar Thakur*, 2008 and *Pramati Educational Trust*, 2014).
- **Delimitation Freeze Duo (`84th` & `87th`):** Because northern and southern states achieved sharply different fertility declines after 1971, the **`84th Amendment Act, 2001`** extended the 42nd Amendment's freeze on state-wise Lok Sabha and State Assembly seat allocations (fixed on the **1971 Census**) for another 25 years—until the **first census taken after 2026**—so population-control states would not lose parliamentary clout, while allowing intra-state readjustment of constituency boundaries on the 1991 Census. Two years later, the **`87th Amendment Act, 2003`** updated that intra-state readjustment benchmark from the 1991 Census to the **2001 Census**, without altering the total number of seats allotted to any State.

#### 5. Fiscal Federalism, Regional Special Provisions, Judiciary, and Borders
- **High-Yield Administrative & Regional Amendments:** **`53rd (1986)`** (Mizoram, `Art. 371G`), **`55th (1986)`** (Arunachal Pradesh, `Art. 371H`), **`56th (1987)`** (Goa statehood, `Art. 371I`), **`80th (2000)`** (10th Finance Commission's **Alternative Scheme of Devolution**, pooling central taxes except cesses/surcharges into a single divisible pool under `Art. 270`), **`88th (2003)`** (Service Tax `Art. 268A`—subsequently omitted by the 101st Amendment), **`94th (2006)`** (freed Bihar from mandatory Tribal Welfare Minister under `Art. 164(1)` proviso and extended it to **Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand** alongside MP and Odisha), and **`98th (2012)`** (inserted **`Article 371J`** granting special development provisions for the **Hyderabad–Karnataka / Kalyana-Karnataka** region).
- **`99th Amendment Act, 2014` (NJAC — Struck Down):** Ratified by over half the States, it inserted **`Articles 124A, 124B, and 124C`** to replace the judge-led Collegium with a six-member **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)** (CJI, two senior-most SC judges, Union Law Minister, and two "eminent persons"). On **16 October 2015**, a five-judge Constitution Bench in ***Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India*** (Fourth Judges Case) struck down both the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act by a **4–1** majority as violating **independence of the judiciary** and the **Basic Structure**, automatically reviving the Collegium!
- **`100th Amendment Act, 2015` (India–Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement):** Amended the **First Schedule** across Assam, West Bengal, Meghalaya, and Tripura to operationalise the **1974 Land Boundary Agreement** and **2011 Protocol** with Bangladesh—transferring **111 Indian enclaves** to Bangladesh and integrating **51 Bangladeshi enclaves** into India alongside demarcation of undemarcated stretches and adverse possessions.

### Phase V: Recent Constitutional Amendments (101st to 106th, 2016–2026)

Every single amendment enacted since 2016 occupies centre stage in both UPSC Prelims and Mains GS-2/GS-3:

#### 101st Amendment Act, 2016 (122nd Bill) — Goods and Services Tax (GST)
Ratified by more than half the States and assented on 8 September 2016, the `101st Amendment` launched India's most ambitious cooperative-federal tax reform (**effective 1 July 2017**), replacing a cascading web of central and state indirect taxes (excise, service tax, VAT, CST, octroi, luxury tax) with a unified national market:
- Inserted **`Article 246A`** granting **simultaneous (concurrent) legislative power** to Parliament and State Legislatures to levy GST, while reserving **exclusive Parliamentary power** over **inter-State trade or commerce (IGST)**.
- Inserted **`Article 269A`** governing the levy, collection, and apportionment of IGST between the Union and States (including imports treated as inter-State supply).
- Inserted **`Article 279A`** establishing the **GST Council**—chaired by the Union Finance Minister, with the Union MoS (Finance) and State Finance Ministers as members, taking decisions by a **three-fourths weighted majority** (Centre holding **1/3rd weight**, all States collectively holding **2/3rd weight**)—and entrenched `Article 279A` inside the **proviso to `Article 368(2)`**!
- Omitted `Article 268A` (Service Tax), amended Seventh Schedule entries (leaving petroleum crude, high-speed diesel, motor spirit/petrol, natural gas, aviation turbine fuel, and alcohol for human consumption outside immediate GST levy), and mandated five-year GST revenue compensation to States (2017–2022). *(In Union of India v. Mohit Minerals, May 2022, the Supreme Court clarified that GST Council recommendations carry persuasive, not strictly binding, legislative weight in cooperative federalism.)*

#### 102nd Amendment Act, 2018 (123rd Bill) and 105th Amendment Act, 2021 (127th Bill) — NCBC & State OBC Lists
The **`102nd Amendment`** elevated the **National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC)** from a statutory body (1993 Act) into a full constitutional commission under **`Article 338B`**, inserted **`Article 342A`** empowering the President to notify Socially and Educationally Backward Classes (SEBCs) in consultation with Governors (with subsequent modifications requiring Parliamentary law), and added the definition in **`Article 366(26C)`**.

However, on **5 May 2021**, in ***Dr. Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v. Chief Minister*** (the Maratha Reservation Case), a five-judge Constitution Bench held by a **3–2** majority that the plain text of `Article 342A` vested **exclusive authority** in the President and Parliament to identify SEBCs—stripping States of their historical power to maintain separate State OBC lists! Parliament moved swiftly with near-unanimous bipartisan consensus to pass the **`105th Amendment Act, 2021`**, which amended `Articles 338B(9), 342A, and 366(26C)` and inserted **`Article 342A(3)`** explicitly clarifying that every State and Union Territory retains independent power to prepare and maintain its **own State/UT List of SEBCs** (which may differ from the Central List) without mandatory consultation with the NCBC.

#### 103rd Amendment Act, 2019 (124th Bill) — 10% EWS Reservation
Inserted **`Article 15(6)`** and **`Article 16(6)`** empowering the State to provide up to **10 per cent reservation** in educational institutions (including private unaided **non-minority** institutions) and public employment for **Economically Weaker Sections (EWS)** of citizens *other than* those already covered by SC, ST, and non-creamy-layer OBC quotas—marking the first time **purely economic criteria** were written into constitutional reservations. On **7 November 2022**, in ***Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India***, a five-judge bench upheld the amendment by **3–2** (Justices Dinesh Maheshwari, Bela M. Trivedi, and J.B. Pardiwala in the majority; CJI U.U. Lalit and Justice S. Ravindra Bhat dissenting), ruling that economic affirmative action does not breach the Basic Structure and that *Indra Sawhney*'s 50 per cent cap is not inflexible.

#### 104th Amendment Act, 2019 (126th Bill; Effective 25 January 2020)
Amended **`Article 334`** in two decisive ways: (i) extended the reservation of seats for **Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes** in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies by another ten years—from 70 to **80 years** from commencement (until **25 January 2030**); and (ii) **discontinued the nominated seats for the Anglo-Indian community** (up to 2 in Lok Sabha under `Article 331` and 1 each in State Assemblies under `Article 333`), noting their small enumerated population in the 2011 Census. *(Note: Because Article 334 affects representation, the 104th Amendment was duly ratified by at least half the States!)*

#### 106th Amendment Act, 2023 (128th Bill) — Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (Women's Reservation)
Passed nearly unanimously in September 2023 and assented on **28 September 2023**, the `106th Amendment`—India's most historic gender-justice constitutional reform—was **formally brought into force by the Union Ministry of Law and Justice via Gazette Notification with effect from 16 April 2026**. Its core architecture includes:
- Inserts **`Article 330A`** (reserving **one-third of all seats** in the **Lok Sabha** for women, including one-third of the seats reserved for SCs and STs), **`Article 332A`** (one-third reservation in all **State Legislative Assemblies**), and amends **`Article 239AA`** (one-third reservation in the **NCT of Delhi Legislative Assembly**).
- Inserts **`Article 334A`** establishing two operational locks: (i) the reservation takes operational effect **only after a delimitation exercise** is undertaken based on the **first Census conducted after the Act's commencement** (hence bringing the Act into force on 16 April 2026 did not activate quotas in the existing 18th Lok Sabha); and (ii) the reservation carries a **15-year sunset period** from the date it takes operational effect (extendable by Parliament), with reserved constituencies **rotated** after each subsequent delimitation.
- **Key Prelims Exclusions:** Does **NOT** apply to the **Rajya Sabha** or **State Legislative Councils**; does **NOT** create a separate sub-quota for **OBC women** within the one-third women's quota; and did **NOT** require state ratification (though debates surrounded its passage, it passed under Track 2).

### Recent Constitutional Amendment Bills (2024–2026)

As of **November 2026**, no 107th Constitutional Amendment Act has been enacted, yet **three consequential Constitutional Amendment Bills** introduced between December 2024 and April 2026 have reshaped constitutional debate across Parliament, States, and the judiciary:

#### 129th Amendment Bill, 2024 — One Nation, One Election (Pending in JPC)
Introduced in the Lok Sabha on **17 December 2024** following the **High-Level Committee chaired by former President Ram Nath Kovind** (March 2024), alongside the companion *Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2024* (covering Delhi, Puducherry, and J&K). The Bill proposes inserting **`Article 82A`** (enabling the President to notify an "appointed date" on the first sitting of Lok Sabha after a general election so that all State Assemblies constituted thereafter expire coterminously with that Lok Sabha, while allowing the ECI to recommend deferring a State poll if needed) and amending **`Articles 83, 172, and 327`** (mandating that after premature dissolution, fresh mid-term polls serve only the **unexpired remainder** of the five-year term). Referred to a **Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC) chaired by P.P. Chaudhary**, whose reporting tenure was extended to the **Winter Session of 2026**. Notably, on **1 October 2026**, the Supreme Court cancelled an informal interaction planned by the JPC with sitting judges because the Bill may eventually come before the Court in judicial review. *(Key Mains Debate: Whether curtailing State Assembly tenures triggers Track 3 state ratification and infringes federalism/democratic accountability, versus gains in governance continuity and reduced election expenditure.)*

#### 130th Amendment Bill, 2025 — Removal of Detained Ministers (Pending in JPC)
Introduced in the Lok Sabha on **20 August 2025** (alongside companion J&K and Union Territories Amendment Bills), the Bill amends **`Articles 75, 164, and 239AA`** to require the removal—or automatic vacation of office on the **31st consecutive day**—of a Prime Minister, Chief Minister, or Union/State/UT Minister arrested and detained in custody for **30 consecutive days** on accusation of an offence punishable with **five or more years of imprisonment**. Referred to a **JPC chaired by Aparajita Sarangi**, also extended to the **Winter Session of 2026** (with draft deliberations weighing temporary *suspension* with automatic restoration upon acquittal instead of permanent removal). *(Key Mains Debate: Balances clean-governance ethics against presumption of innocence, federalism, and potential misuse of investigative agencies against opposition Chief Ministers.)*

#### 131st Amendment Bill, 2026 — Delimitation & Women's Quota Fast-Track (Defeated)
Introduced during a Special Session on **16 April 2026** (the very day the 106th Act was notified into force) alongside the *Delimitation Bill, 2026* and *Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026*, this Bill sought to raise maximum Lok Sabha strength from 543 to **850 seats** (up to 815 from States and 35 from UTs), delete the third proviso to **`Article 82`** to permit delimitation on the **2011 Census** without waiting for the post-2026 census, and operationalise the 33% women's quota by 2029. Amid united Opposition resistance warning that 2011-census seat redistribution would penalise southern and north-eastern states that stabilised population early, the Bill was **defeated in the Lok Sabha on 17 April 2026**—garnering **298 Ayes to 230 Noes** out of 528 voting, **54 votes short** of the **352** two-thirds special majority under `Article 368`—forcing withdrawal of both companion Bills.

## 3. Evolution of the Basic Structure Doctrine (1951–1973)

Can a duly elected Parliament, commanding a two-thirds supermajority under `Article 368`, abolish free elections, convert India into a theocratic or unitary dictatorship, or extinguish Fundamental Rights altogether? For twenty-three turbulent years after 1950, Parliament and the Supreme Court fought an epic constitutional duel over precisely that question. The answer finally forged on **24 April 1973** in ***Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala***—the **Doctrine of Basic Structure**—stands as India's most celebrated original contribution to global constitutional jurisprudence.

### The Foundational Constitutional Dilemma: Article 13 vs Article 368

Nowhere does the text of the Indian Constitution mention or define the phrase **"Basic Structure."** It is a purely **judicial innovation** inferred from the architecture of a written Constitution, arising out of a direct textual collision between two seminal provisions:

- **`Article 13(2)` (Shield of Fundamental Rights):** Declares that *"The State shall not make any **law** which takes away or abridges the rights conferred by this Part [Part III] and any law made in contravention of this clause shall, to the extent of the contravention, be void."*
- **`Article 368` (Amending Power):** Empowers Parliament, by special majority (and state ratification where required), to amend the Constitution.

The decisive legal question was deceptively simple: **Does the word "law" in `Article 13(2)` include a Constitutional Amendment Act passed under `Article 368`?** Beneath this statutory puzzle lay a deeper distinction in constitutional theory between **original constituent power** (exercised by the sovereign Constituent Assembly of 1946–49 in authoring the Republic's founding charter) and **derived constituent power** (exercised by Parliament as a creature of that Constitution under `Article 368`). If Parliament possessed unlimited derived power, the creature could destroy its creator; yet if Fundamental Rights were utterly unamendable, social and agrarian reform could stall indefinitely.

```timeline
# The 22-Year Constitutional Duel (1951–1973)
1951 | Shankari Prasad v. Union of India | Unanimous 5-judge bench upheld 1st Amendment: 'law' in Art. 13(2) excludes Art. 368 amendments; FRs amendable.
1965 | Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan | 3–2 majority followed Shankari Prasad on 17th Amendment, but Hidayatullah & Mudholkar JJ. sowed seeds of 'basic features'.
Feb 1965 | Dieter Conrad's BHU Lecture | German jurist lectured on 'Implied Limitations of the Amending Power', citing Weimar collapse & Germany's Art. 79(3).
1967 | I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab | 11-judge bench (6–5) reversed Shankari Prasad: FRs 'transcendental & immutable'; Art. 368 procedural; prospective overruling.
1971 | 24th & 25th Constitutional Amendments | Parliament counter-attacked: inserted Arts. 13(4), 368(1)/(3), mandatory assent, and Art. 31C shielding 39(b)–(c).
24 Apr 1973 | Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala | Historic 13-judge bench (7–6): overruled Golaknath, upheld 24th Amendment, and invented the inviolable Basic Structure Doctrine.
```

### Early Consensus and Seeds of Doubt: Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965)

The constitutional drama opened over land reform and the original Fundamental Right to Property (`Articles 19(1)(f)` and `31`). When the Patna High Court invalidated Bihar's zamindari abolition statute (*Kameshwar Singh*, 1950), the Provisional Parliament swiftly enacted the **`1st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951`**, adding `Articles 31A`, `31B`, and the **Ninth Schedule**. Challenging its validity, landowners petitioned the Supreme Court in ***Shankari Prasad Singh Deo v. Union of India* (1951)**.

A unanimous five-judge bench led by **Justice Patanjali Sastri** upheld the 1st Amendment, applying the rule of harmonious construction. The Court drew a bright demarcation between **ordinary legislative law** (enacted under `Articles 245–246`) and **constituent law** (enacted under `Article 368`), ruling that **"law" in `Article 13(2)` includes only ordinary statutes and NOT constitutional amendments**. Parliament therefore possessed plenary power to amend any part of the Constitution, including Part III Fundamental Rights.

Fourteen years later, Parliament enacted the **`17th Amendment Act, 1964`**, inserting 44 more agrarian statutes into the Ninth Schedule. In ***Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan* (decided 30 October 1964; reported 1965)**, a five-judge bench headed by **Chief Justice P.B. Gajendragadkar** (joined by Justices K.N. Wanchoo and Raghubar Dayal) reaffirmed *Shankari Prasad* by a **3–2** majority on the amendability question, holding that `Article 368` empowered Parliament to amend Fundamental Rights cleanly.

Yet the **two separate opinions** in *Sajjan Singh* cracked the edifice of judicial consensus forever. While concurring in dismissing the writ petitions on technical grounds, both **Justice M. Hidayatullah** and **Justice J.R. Mudholkar** expressed grave doubts about *Shankari Prasad*:

- **Justice M. Hidayatullah** observed that Fundamental Rights were meant to be *"more permanent"* and declared he would require far stronger reasons to accept that the solemn guarantees of Part III were merely **"the play things of a special majority."**
- **Justice J.R. Mudholkar** introduced the exact conceptual vocabulary of **"basic features"** into Indian constitutional law for the very first time—explicitly citing the Pakistan Supreme Court under **Chief Justice A.R. Cornelius** in ***Fazlul Quader Chowdhry v. Mohd. Abdul Haque* (1963)**, which had held that the President could not remove fundamental structural features under a removal-of-difficulties clause:

> "It is also a matter for consideration whether making a change in a basic feature of the Constitution can be regarded merely as an amendment or would it be, in effect, rewriting a part of the Constitution; and if the latter, would it be within the purview of Article 368?" — Justice J.R. Mudholkar, *Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan* (1965)

Crucially, just four months after *Sajjan Singh* was decided, in **February 1965**, German legal scholar **Prof. Dieter Conrad** (of the University of Heidelberg) delivered a prophetic lecture at **Banaras Hindu University (BHU)** titled ***"Implied Limitations of the Amending Power."*** Haunted by how Adolf Hitler had used the Weimar Constitution's own amending clause (Article 76) to pass the 1933 Enabling Act and destroy German democracy legally from within—prompting post-war West Germany to adopt its explicit **Eternity Clause (`Article 79(3)`)**—Conrad asked Indian jurists whether Parliament could lawfully use `Article 368` to divide India into two states, abolish `Article 21`, or make the Prime Minister a dictator for life. Senior Advocate **M.K. Nambiyar** placed Conrad's paper before the Supreme Court in *Golaknath*, and **Nani Palkhivala** later marshalled Conrad's thesis with devastating force in *Kesavananda Bharati*.

### The Pendulum Swings: I.C. Golaknath (1967) and the 24th and 25th Amendments (1971)

In ***I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab* (27 February 1967)**, the Supreme Court convened an unprecedented **11-judge bench** to test the Punjab Security of Land Tenures Act (shielded by the `17th Amendment`). By a razor-thin **6–5 majority**—with **Chief Justice K. Subba Rao** writing for five judges (himself, J.C. Shah, S.M. Sikri, J.M. Shelat, and C.A. Vaidialingam JJ.) and **Justice M. Hidayatullah** writing a separate concurring opinion (Justices Wanchoo, Bhargava, Mitter, Bachawat, and Ramaswami dissenting)—the Court **overruled *Shankari Prasad* and *Sajjan Singh*** and erected an absolute wall around Part III:

1. **Fundamental Rights Declared Immutable:** Fundamental Rights occupy a **"transcendental and immutable"** place in the Constitution; Parliament has **no power** under `Article 368` to abridge or take away any Fundamental Right.
2. **Article 368 Held Purely Procedural:** Relying on its original marginal heading (*"Procedure for amendment"*), CJI Subba Rao held that `Article 368` laid down *only the procedure* to amend—the substantive *power* to amend flowed from Parliament's ordinary residuary legislative power (`Article 248` read with Entry 97, List I). Consequently, a Constitutional Amendment Act **was "law" within the meaning of `Article 13(2)`** and void if it infringed Part III!
3. **Doctrine of Prospective Overruling:** Realising that invalidating the 1st, 4th, and 17th Amendments retrospectively would plunge India's land-reform structure into chaos, CJI Subba Rao imported the American **doctrine of prospective overruling**—keeping all past amendments valid while barring Parliament from abridging Part III *from 27 February 1967 onward*. (The Court suggested that only a fresh **Constituent Assembly** convened via residuary power could amend Fundamental Rights.)

*Golaknath* triggered an explosive political clash. When the Supreme Court subsequently struck down bank nationalisation in ***R.C. Cooper* (1970)** and the executive abolition of privy purses in ***Madhav Rao Scindia* (1970)**, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi fought the **March 1971 general election** on a mandate for constitutional supremacy of Parliament, winning a commanding two-thirds majority. Parliament retaliated with a surgical constitutional counter-offensive:

- **`24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971`:** Systematically dismantled every textual pillar of *Golaknath*. It inserted **`Article 13(4)`** and **`Article 368(3)`** declaring that nothing in `Article 13` applies to any `Article 368` amendment; changed the marginal heading of `Article 368` to *"Power of Parliament to amend the Constitution and procedure therefor"*; inserted **`Article 368(1)`** confirming Parliament acts in exercise of its **"constituent power"** to amend *"by way of addition, variation or repeal"*; and made **Presidential assent mandatory** under `Article 368(2)`.
- **`25th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971`:** Replaced *"compensation"* with *"amount"* in `Article 31(2)` and inserted **`Article 31C`** containing two explosive limbs: (i) no law giving effect to `Article 39(b)` (distribution of material resources) or `Article 39(c)` (preventing concentration of wealth) could be declared void for violating `Articles 14, 19, or 31`; and (ii) **no law containing a legislative declaration** that it gave effect to such policy **could be called in question in any court** on the ground that it did not actually serve that policy. (Parliament also passed the `26th Amendment` abolishing privy purses and the `29th Amendment, 1972` placing two Kerala Land Reform Acts into the Ninth Schedule.)

### The Watershed: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (24 April 1973)

Everything converged when **Swami Kesavananda Bharati**, pontiff of the **Edneer Mutt** in Kasaragod, Kerala, challenged the Kerala Land Reforms Acts placed in the Ninth Schedule via the 29th Amendment, alongside sweeping challenges to the `24th`, `25th`, and `29th Amendments`. Recognising that the fate of Indian constitutional democracy hung in the balance, the Supreme Court constituted its **largest bench ever—13 judges** headed by **Chief Justice S.M. Sikri**.

Oral arguments lasted **68 working days** across five months, pitting legendary constitutional advocate **Nani A. Palkhivala** (for the petitioners) against towering jurist **H.M. Seervai** (for Kerala) and **Attorney General Niren De** (for the Union). On **24 April 1973**—one day before CJI Sikri retired—the bench delivered **11 separate judgments** spanning 703 pages, accompanied by a famous one-page summary titled **"View by the Majority"** signed by 9 judges.

#### The Three Pillar Holdings and Justice H.R. Khanna's Decisive Vote

The genius of *Kesavananda Bharati* lay in forging a middle synthesis between *Shankari Prasad*'s unlimited parliamentary sovereignty and *Golaknath*'s absolute rigidity:

1. **Overruled *Golaknath* & Upheld the `24th Amendment`:** Parliament *can* amend **any part** of the Constitution—including **Fundamental Rights** in Part III—under `Article 368`, and an amendment is NOT "law" under `Article 13(2)`.
2. **Invented the Inviolable Basic Structure Limit (7–6):** While Parliament's amending power is wide, the word **"amend"** itself implies that the Constitution must survive with its core identity intact. Therefore, `Article 368` **does NOT empower Parliament to alter or destroy the "basic structure or framework" of the Constitution**.
3. **Surgical Split on `Article 31C` (`25th Amendment`) & `29th Amendment`:** The Court **upheld the first limb** of `Article 31C` shielding `Articles 39(b)–(c)` laws from `Articles 14, 19, and 31`, **but struck down the second limb** barring judicial review of legislative declarations as destructive of judicial review and the Basic Structure! (The `29th Amendment` was upheld subject to testing the Kerala Acts against basic structure.)

**Justice H.R. Khanna** cast the historic **seventh decisive vote**. Disagreeing with the six Sikri-bloc judges on whether Fundamental Rights per se were unamendable (and expressly holding that the **Right to Property is NOT part of Basic Structure**, clearing the path for the 44th Amendment in 1978!), Justice Khanna nevertheless parted ways with the six Ray-bloc judges by embracing Dieter Conrad's semantic limit on *"amendment"*: Parliament cannot use `Article 368` to abrogate the Constitution or dismantle its foundational framework—such as secularism, democracy, and judicial review.

> [!case]
> ***Kesavananda Bharati Sripadagalvaru v. State of Kerala* (24 April 1973) — 13-Judge Bench (7–6):**
> - **Immediate Political Aftermath:** Within twenty-four hours of the verdict (**25 April 1973**), the Union government shattered seniority conventions by superseding three senior majority judges (**Justices J.M. Shelat, K.S. Hegde, and A.N. Grover**) to appoint dissenting **Justice A.N. Ray** as Chief Justice of India; all three superseded judges resigned in protest.
> - **The Aborted November 1975 Review Attempt:** During the Emergency, CJI A.N. Ray abruptly convened another 13-judge bench in **November 1975** to reconsider and overturn *Kesavananda*. Following two days of electrifying oral argument by **Nani Palkhivala**—demonstrating that no party had even properly petitioned for review and warning against stripping citizens of their constitutional shield during an Emergency—CJI Ray abruptly **dissolved the bench** on 12 November 1975, cementing *Kesavananda* permanently.
> - **The Permanent Cut-off Date:** **24 April 1973** became the constitutional watershed date separating immune pre-1973 amendments and Ninth Schedule laws from reviewable post-1973 ones (*Waman Rao*, 1981; *I.R. Coelho*, 2007).

## 4. Consolidation and Post-Kesavananda Jurisprudence (1975–2026)

What emerged in April 1973 as a fragile 7–6 doctrinal compromise could easily have withered had history turned quieter. Instead, the Emergency of 1975–77 forged the Basic Structure Doctrine in constitutional fire—converting even former *Kesavananda* dissenters into its staunchest defenders—and five subsequent decades expanded it into a sophisticated guardian of democracy, federalism, secularism, and rights.

### Emergency Crucible and Doctrinal Fortification: Raj Narain (1975), Minerva Mills (1980), and Waman Rao (1981)

#### Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (7 November 1975)
When the Allahabad High Court set aside Prime Minister Indira Gandhi's 1971 Rae Bareli election for electoral malpractices in June 1975, Parliament passed the **`39th Amendment Act, 1975`** inserting **`Article 329A(4)`**, which retroactively wiped out the High Court verdict, placed the Prime Minister's election beyond judicial scrutiny, and declared her election valid by constitutional fiat.

This landmark judgment marked the **very first time the Supreme Court struck down a Constitutional Amendment using the Basic Structure Doctrine**. Strikingly, **four of the five judges** on the bench—CJI A.N. Ray and Justices K.K. Mathew, M.H. Beg, and Y.V. Chandrachud (every judge except Justice H.R. Khanna)—had **dissented in *Kesavananda***, yet every one of them accepted *Kesavananda* as binding law! While upholding Indira Gandhi's election on the merits under retroactively amended ordinary election law (RPA 1951), the Court invalidated `Article 329A(4)`: Justices Khanna, Mathew, and Chandrachud struck it down squarely for destroying **democracy**, **free and fair elections**, the **rule of law**, and **separation of powers** (since Parliament had usurped judicial adjudication without applying any legal standard), while CJI Ray voided it for adjudicating without applying law and Justice Beg read it down. Crucially, the bench also articulated an enduring boundary: the Basic Structure test applies to **constitutional amendments**, NOT to ordinary statutes!

#### Minerva Mills v. Union of India (31 July 1980)
Smarting from *Raj Narain*, Parliament deployed the **`42nd Amendment Act, 1976`** to launch a frontal assault on *Kesavananda* itself—inserting **`Articles 368(4)` and `368(5)`** claiming unlimited, unreviewable constituent power, while expanding **`Article 31C`** to let *any* Directive Principle override `Articles 14 and 19`. Because the Congress-majority Rajya Sabha blocked their repeal during the 44th Amendment (1978), it fell to a five-judge Constitution Bench led by **Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud** (a *Kesavananda* dissenter now wholly converted!) to settle the issue in ***Minerva Mills***.

Unanimously (**5–0**), the Court struck down **`Articles 368(4)` and `368(5)`**, enshrining **limited amending power** and **judicial review** as unassailable pillars of the Basic Structure:

> "Parliament cannot, under Article 368, expand its amending power so as to acquire for itself the right to repeal or abrogate the Constitution or to destroy its basic and essential features. The donee of a limited power cannot by the exercise of that power convert the limited power into an unlimited one." — Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud, *Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980)

Simultaneously, by a **4–1 majority** (Justice P.N. Bhagwati dissenting on `Art. 31C`), the bench struck down the 42nd Amendment's expansion of **`Article 31C`**, delivering one of constitutional law's most famous metaphors: the Indian Constitution rests *"on the bedrock of the balance between Parts III and IV"*—giving absolute primacy to one over the other disturbs the **harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles**, which is itself an essential feature of the Basic Structure!

#### Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981)
Evaluating challenges to Maharashtra agricultural ceiling laws sheltered in the Ninth Schedule, a five-judge bench led by CJI Y.V. Chandrachud drew a crystal-clear **prospective bright line** at **24 April 1973** (the date of *Kesavananda Bharati*). All constitutional amendments and Ninth Schedule insertions enacted **before 24 April 1973** remain valid and immune from basic-structure challenge; all amendments and laws placed into the Ninth Schedule **on or after 24 April 1973** are open to judicial scrutiny on whether they damage or destroy the Basic Structure.

### Institutional, Federal, and Rights-Based Expansion (1987–2015)

Across the next three decades, Constitution Benches systematically extended Basic Structure protection across tribunals, anti-defection, federalism, secularism, affirmative action, the Ninth Schedule, and judicial appointments:

```cases

```

- **Tribunals & Judicial Review (*S.P. Sampath Kumar*, 1987 → *L. Chandra Kumar*, 1997):** In *Sampath Kumar* (1987), the Court initially permitted `Article 323A` Administrative Tribunals to exclude High Court jurisdiction so long as they functioned as credible "effective institutional substitutes." A decade later, a **7-judge unanimous bench** in ***L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997)** overruled *Sampath Kumar*, striking down clauses `323A(2)(d)` and `323B(3)(d)` to the extent they ousted High Court jurisdiction. The Court held that **judicial review vested in the Supreme Court (`Article 32`) and High Courts (`Articles 226 and 227`)** is an inviolable basic feature; tribunals can act only as **supplemental** bodies of first instance whose decisions remain subject to scrutiny by a Division Bench of the jurisdictional High Court.
- **Anti-Defection & Democracy (*Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992):** Upheld the core Tenth Schedule (`52nd Amendment`) as strengthening parliamentary democracy and free and fair elections, while striking down **Paragraph 7** for lack of state ratification and declaring the Speaker/Chairman's disqualification decisions open to judicial review on grounds of jurisdictional error, *mala fides*, perversity, or violation of natural justice.
- **Substantive Equality (*Indra Sawhney*, 1992):** A 9-judge bench upholding 27% OBC reservation reaffirmed **rule of law** and **substantive equality** as basic features—embedding the **creamy layer exclusion** and the **50 per cent ceiling** (subject to extraordinary situations) into India's equality jurisprudence.
- **Executive Action Under Article 356 (*S.R. Bommai v. Union of India*, 1994):** A monumental **9-judge bench** elevated **secularism**, **federalism**, **democracy**, **unity and integrity of the nation**, and **social justice** as core Basic Structure pillars. Uniquely, the Court extended Basic Structure beyond constitutional amendments to **executive proclamations under `Article 356` (President's Rule)**—holding that a State government pursuing anti-secular policies cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution and legitimately invites Presidential dismissal, while simultaneously subjecting `Article 356` proclamations themselves to judicial review!
- **Analytical Rigour for Equality Amendments (*M. Nagaraj v. Union of India*, 2006):** Adjudicating the `77th, 81st, 82nd, and 85th Amendments` on SC/ST promotion quotas, a five-judge bench formulated two precision judicial tests for Basic Structure review:
  1. **The Width Test:** Checks whether an amendment obliterates constitutional limitations/ceilings (backwardness, inadequate representation, the 50 per cent current-vacancy ceiling, creamy layer, and `Article 335` administrative efficiency) so broadly that it destroys the overarching equality principle.
  2. **The Identity Test:** Checks whether the amendment alters the **constitutional identity** of the equality code itself. Because all four amendments were merely *enabling* provisions retaining those constitutional guardrails, the Court upheld them all (later refined in *Jarnail Singh*, 2018).
- **Closing the Ninth Schedule "Black Hole" (*I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu*, 11 January 2007):** With Ninth Schedule entries ballooning from 13 in 1951 to **284 statutes**—including non-agrarian statutes like Tamil Nadu's 69% reservation law—a unanimous **9-judge bench** led by **Chief Justice Y.K. Sabharwal** ended blanket Ninth Schedule immunity permanently.

> [!case]
> ***I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu* (11 January 2007) — 9-Judge Unanimous Bench:**
> - **Core Rule:** There is **no blanket immunity** for any law inserted into the **Ninth Schedule (`Article 31B`) on or after 24 April 1973**—even if it was previously struck down by a court before insertion!
> - **Two-Step Judicial Test:** Every post-24 April 1973 Ninth Schedule law must pass:
>   1. **The Rights Test:** Does the law abridge any Fundamental Right forming part of the Basic Structure—specifically the **Golden Triangle (`Articles 14, 19, and 21`)** and **`Article 15`**?
>   2. **The Essence of Rights Test:** Not every minor curtailment invalidates a Ninth Schedule law; rather, courts examine its *direct impact and effect*—if it damages or destroys the **essence of the overarching principles** underlying those rights (equality, liberty, rule of law, separation of powers), it must be struck down!

```flow
# Judicial Review Matrix: Testing Amendments & Ninth Schedule Laws
Measure Challenged in Supreme Court | Constitutional Amendment (Art. 368) OR Post-24 April 1973 Ninth Schedule Law
Procedural Compliance Check | Does it alter an entrenched federal clause under Art. 368(2) proviso (incl. substantive 'change in effect')? → If unratified: VOID (Kihoto Hollohan; Rajendra N. Shah)
Substantive Basic Structure Check | For Amendments: Width Test & Identity Test (M. Nagaraj, 2006) || For Post-1973 Ninth Schedule Laws: Rights Test & Essence of Rights Test against Arts. 14, 15, 19, 21 (I.R. Coelho, 2007)
Constitutional Outcome | Core Identity Intact → UPHELD (e.g. 103rd Amend in Janhit Abhiyan, 2022) || Basic Structure Damaged/Destroyed → STRUCK DOWN (e.g. 39th, 42nd, 99th/NJAC)
```

- **Judicial Independence & Primacy (*Madras Bar Association*, 2014 & *NJAC / SCAORA*, 16 October 2015):** In *Madras Bar Association* (2014), a five-judge bench invalidated the **National Tax Tribunal Act, 2005** for encroaching on High Court adjudication and breaching separation of powers. A year later, in ***Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India* (16 October 2015)**, a five-judge bench struck down both the **`99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014`** and the **NJAC Act** by a **4–1 majority** (Justices J.S. Khehar, Madan B. Lokur, Kurian Joseph, and Adarsh Kumar Goel; **Justice J. Chelameswar dissenting**). The majority held that **independence of the judiciary** requires **primacy of the judiciary in appointing higher-court judges**, which the NJAC's executive/eminent-persons veto fatally compromised. Justice Chelameswar's celebrated dissent warned that judge-made Collegium primacy was not synonymous with judicial independence and criticised insulating the appointment process from parliamentary reform.

### Contemporary Frontiers (2022–2026): Affirmative Action, Federalism, Preamble, and Ordinary Statutes

Six pivotal Supreme Court pronouncements between November 2022 and November 2026 have sharpened the boundaries of the Basic Structure Doctrine for the modern era:

1. ***Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India* (7 November 2022 — 5-Judge Bench, 3–2):** Upholding the **`103rd Amendment` (10% EWS quota)**, Justices Dinesh Maheshwari, Bela M. Trivedi, and J.B. Pardiwala held that (i) affirmative action based solely on **economic criteria** strengthens rather than violates the Basic Structure; (ii) excluding SCs, STs, and non-creamy-layer OBCs from EWS does not breach the equality code since they occupy separate vertical compartments; and (iii) *Indra Sawhney*'s **50 per cent ceiling is not an inflexible part of Basic Structure**. Dissenting, **CJI U.U. Lalit and Justice S. Ravindra Bhat** argued that excluding the poorest SCs, STs, and OBCs from an economic-poverty quota breached the non-discriminatory core of the equality code.
2. ***In Re: Article 370 of the Constitution* (11 December 2023 — 5-Judge Unanimous Bench):** Led by **CJI D.Y. Chandrachud**, the Court unanimously upheld the August 2019 abrogation of Jammu & Kashmir's special status under `Article 370(3)`, holding that J&K retained no internal sovereignty after accession and that `Article 370` was a **temporary feature of asymmetric federalism**. Crucially for amendment jurisprudence, however, the Court **struck down Constitutional Order (CO) 272's indirect amendment of `Article 367`** (which had tried to substitute "Legislative Assembly" for "Constituent Assembly"), affirming that **constitutional amendment procedures cannot be bypassed through interpretive shortcuts**. Declining to decide conclusively whether `Article 3` allows permanent downgrading of a State into a Union Territory (in view of the Solicitor General's assurance), the Court affirmed **federalism and representative democracy** by directing prompt restoration of J&K's statehood and Assembly elections.
3. ***Anoop Baranwal* (2 March 2023) & *ADR / Electoral Bonds* (15 February 2024):** Five-judge Constitution Benches anchored **free and fair elections** and an **independent Election Commission** firmly in the Basic Structure. *Anoop Baranwal* established an interim PM–LoP–CJI selection committee until Parliament enacted the **Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners Act, 2023** (replacing the CJI with a Union Cabinet Minister; on **23 September 2026**, a two-judge bench referred the constitutional challenge to the 2023 Act to the CJI for listing before a Constitution Bench). In *Electoral Bonds*, a unanimous five-judge bench struck down anonymous corporate political funding under `Articles 19(1)(a)` and `14`.
4. **The Ordinary-Statute Boundary — *Anjum Kadari* (5 November 2024) vs *Madras Bar Association* (19 November 2025):** Can an **ordinary statute** (as distinct from a constitutional amendment) be struck down directly for violating the Basic Structure? The orthodox doctrine—laid down in *Raj Narain* (1975), *State of Karnataka v. Union of India* (1977), and a five-judge bench in ***Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India* (2006)** (upholding open-ballot Rajya Sabha voting)—answers **NO**: ordinary legislation fails only for **(i) lack of legislative competence** or **(ii) violation of Part III or a specific constitutional provision**. Two major verdicts in 2024–25 harmonised this boundary with crystal precision:
   - In ***Anjum Kadari v. Union of India* (UP Madarsa Act Case, 5 November 2024)**, CJI D.Y. Chandrachud's three-judge bench overturned the Allahabad High Court and reaffirmed *Kuldip Nayar*: courts **cannot invalidate an ordinary statute merely by invoking an abstract, free-floating Basic Structure concept** like undefined "secularism" without demonstrating breach of a specific constitutional Article or legislative incompetence.
   - A year later, in ***Madras Bar Association v. Union of India* (19 November 2025)**, **CJI B.R. Gavai and Justice K. Vinod Chandran** struck down re-enacted appointment and tenure provisions of the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** for violating **separation of powers** and **judicial independence**—explaining that these principles are *"firmly embedded in the text, structure, and spirit of the Constitution"* and Parliament cannot override judicial invalidation by re-enacting struck-down provisions without curing the underlying defect. Parliament responded by enacting the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026** (assented **13 August 2026**), repealing the 2021 Act and creating the independent **National Tribunals Commission** mandated by the Court.
5. ***Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (5 November 2024 — 9-Judge Bench):** Resolving a three-decade uncertainty left after *Minerva Mills* (1980), the nine-judge bench unanimously (**9–0**) held that once *Minerva Mills* voided the 42nd Amendment's expanded `Article 31C`, the **original, narrower `Article 31C`** (inserted by the 25th Amendment shielding `Articles 39(b)` and `(c)`) **automatically revived and remains valid law**—while holding **8–1** that not all private property qualifies as "material resources of the community" under `Article 39(b)`.
6. ***Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India* (25 November 2024):** A bench led by **CJI Sanjiv Khanna** and Justice Sanjay Kumar dismissed petitions challenging the 42nd Amendment's insertion of **"socialist"** and **"secular"** into the Preamble—ruling that (i) Parliament's amending power under `Article 368` unquestionably extends to the **Preamble** despite its 26 November 1949 adoption date; (ii) **secularism** is an inviolable pillar of the Basic Structure; and (iii) in India's constitutional context, **"socialism"** signifies a commitment to a **welfare state and equality of opportunity**, imposing no straitjacket on elected governments' economic policies and fully harmonising with India's mixed economy and private enterprise under `Article 19(1)(g)`.

> [!news]
> **The 2024–2026 Supreme Court Quartet Refining Basic Structure:**
> - **5 Nov 2024 (*Anjum Kadari*):** Ordinary laws CANNOT be voided on abstract basic structure alone—must tie to constitutional text/competence.
> - **5 Nov 2024 (*Property Owners Association*, 9 JJ):** Narrower `Art. 31C` (25th Amend) automatically revived after *Minerva Mills* (9–0).
> - **25 Nov 2024 (*Dr. Balram Singh*):** Upheld **"socialist"** and **"secular"** in the Preamble; `Art. 368` reaches Preamble; secularism reaffirmed as Basic Structure.
> - **19 Nov 2025 (*Madras Bar Association*):** Voided re-enacted **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** clauses breaching judicial independence → prompted **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026** (13 Aug 2026, setting up National Tribunals Commission).

## 5. Critical Evaluation, Comparative Constitutionalism, and Mains Debate

More than half a century after *Kesavananda Bharati*, the interplay between Parliament's amending authority under `Article 368` and the judiciary's guardianship of the Basic Structure remains the defining structural dialogue of Indian democracy. For UPSC Mains GS-2, aspirants must move beyond partisan slogans to evaluate both the democratic legitimacy of parliamentary majorities and the necessity of constitutional guardrails—anchored in comparative global law and recent institutional exchanges.

### The Core Democratic Tension: Constitutionalism vs Counter-Majoritarian Difficulty

The debate reignited forcefully in **January 2023** and continued through the **November 2025 Tribunals Reforms verdict** and ongoing JPC scrutiny of the **129th Amendment Bill (ONOE)**. Addressing the **83rd All-India Presiding Officers Conference in Jaipur on 11 January 2023**, Vice-President **Jagdeep Dhankhar** questioned *Kesavananda Bharati*'s premise and the 2015 invalidation of the NJAC, contending that in a democratic republic, the sovereignty of Parliament—as the direct expression of the people's electoral mandate—must be *"the basic"* of any basic structure. Ten days later, delivering the **18th Nani Palkhivala Memorial Lecture in Mumbai on 21 January 2023**, Chief Justice **D.Y. Chandrachud** responded with an enduring metaphor defending the doctrine:

> "The basic structure of our Constitution, like the north star, guides and gives certain direction to the interpreters and implementers of the Constitution when the path ahead is convoluted." — Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud, 18th Nani Palkhivala Memorial Lecture, Mumbai, 21 January 2023

> [!debate]
> **Parliamentary Sovereignty vs Constitutionalism — Both Sides Fairly Weighed:**
> - **The Case for Constitutionalism (Defending Basic Structure):**
>   1. *Shield Against Majoritarian Self-Destruction:* Prevents a transient two-thirds majority from dismantling democracy from within—historically proven during the 1975–77 Emergency when *Raj Narain* and *Minerva Mills* thwarted unreviewable elections and unlimited amending power.
>   2. *Protection of Pluralism, Minorities, and Federalism:* Safeguards India's secular, federal, and rights-based compact (*S.R. Bommai*, *I.R. Coelho*, *In Re: Art. 370*) against temporary centralising waves.
>   3. *Hierarchy of Constituent Power:* Enforces the foundational distinction between **original constituent power** (Constituent Assembly) and **derived constituent power** (Parliament under `Art. 368`)—the donee of a limited power cannot expand it into an unlimited one (*Minerva Mills*).
>   4. *Adaptable Living Charter, Not Paralysis:* Over **seventy constitutional amendments** have been enacted since 24 April 1973—including transformative reforms like the `44th, 52nd, 61st, 73rd/74th, 86th, 91st, 101st, 103rd, 105th, and 106th`—while the Court has invalidated only a tiny handful of provisions (`39th, 42nd, 99th`, and partial clauses of `323A/B` and `97th`).
>   5. *Global Credibility:* India's doctrine is recognised worldwide as a vital safety valve in constitutional democracies that lack mandatory popular referendums for constitutional amendments.
> - **The Counter-Majoritarian Critique (Cautions Against Judicial Overreach):**
>   1. *Counter-Majoritarian Difficulty ("Tyranny of the Unelected"):* Unelected judges wielding a final veto over amendments passed by elected supermajorities—and even ratified by half the States—strains democratic accountability.
>   2. *Absence of Express Textual Basis:* Unlike Germany's explicit Article 79(3), India's Constitution contains no textual eternity clause; critics contend the judiciary inferred an unstated power to limit Parliament.
>   3. *Indeterminacy & Elastic Scope:* Refusing to close the list of basic features leaves boundaries fluid—an ambiguity that tempted lower courts to invalidate ordinary statutes on vague notions of secularism until *Anjum Kadari* (Nov 2024) restored textual discipline.
>   4. *Judicial Self-Insulation (The 2015 NJAC Flashpoint):* Voiding the near-unanimous `99th Amendment` by elevating judge-created **Collegium primacy** into Basic Structure drew sharp criticism even from within the Court—notably **Justice J. Chelameswar's** lone dissent warning that transparency and executive participation in judicial appointments do not inherently destroy judicial independence.
>   5. *Fragile 7–6 Origin:* Critics occasionally point out that the doctrine rested originally on a single swing vote (Justice H.R. Khanna) amid eleven sprawling opinions—though five decades of unanimous reaffirmation have decisively settled its legal authority.

### Comparative Constitutional Unamendability: India in Global Perspective

Placing India's Basic Structure Doctrine alongside peer constitutional democracies illuminates *why* India developed implied judicial unamendability—and why some democracies codify it textually while others decline it altogether:

- **Germany (Basic Law, 1949 — Textual Eternity Clause, `Article 79(3)`):** Scarred by Weimar Germany's legal self-destruction via the 1933 Enabling Act, West Germany explicitly codified an **Eternity Clause (*Ewigkeitsklausel*)** prohibiting any amendment affecting the division of the Federation into *Länder*, their participation in legislation, or the core principles laid down in **Article 1** (inviolability of human dignity) and **Article 20** (democratic, federal social state and rule of law). Germany's textual entrenchment directly inspired Prof. Dieter Conrad's 1965 BHU lecture.
- **Bangladesh (Direct Judicial Export → Codification):** In ***Anwar Hossain Chowdhury v. Bangladesh* (2 September 1989 — Eighth Amendment Case)**, the Appellate Division of the Supreme Court of Bangladesh explicitly adopted India's *Kesavananda* doctrine to strike down a military-era amendment that broke up the integrated High Court Division into seven regional permanent benches. Bangladesh later codified basic-structure unamendability textually in **Article 7B** (via the 15th Amendment, 2011).
- **Kenya (Structured Rejection Based on Referendum Lock):** In ***Attorney General v. David Ndii* (Building Bridges Initiative / BBI Case, 31 March 2022)**, Kenya's Supreme Court reversed its High Court and Court of Appeal by **6–1** (Justice Ibrahim dissenting), declining to import India's uncodified Basic Structure Doctrine. Its reasoning offers a profound comparative lesson: Kenya's 2010 Constitution (**Articles 255–257**) already contains a hyper-rigid, gapless amendment lock requiring a **mandatory national referendum** for foundational changes—making an implied judicial doctrine unnecessary where the people themselves vote directly! (Even so, Kenya's Supreme Court struck down the BBI Bill on procedural grounds because the President could not initiate a "popular initiative" amendment.)
- **Pakistan, Malaysia, Belize, USA, and UK:** **Pakistan's** Supreme Court cautiously acknowledged "salient features" in *District Bar Association, Rawalpindi* (2015) without striking down the 21st Amendment; **Belize** adopted implied unamendability; **Malaysia** has oscillated between endorsement and restraint; the **United States** relies on Article V's extreme procedural rigidity without a general substantive basic-structure bar (save equal state representation in the Senate); and the **United Kingdom** operates under uncodified Parliamentary Sovereignty without formal unamendable rules.

#### Balanced Way Forward for Constitutional Harmony

Sustaining India's delicate equilibrium between democratic dynamism and constitutional permanence requires five mutually reinforcing reforms:

1. **Basic Structure as a High-Threshold Safety Valve:** The judiciary should reserve Basic Structure invalidation strictly for genuine existential threats to constitutional identity—deploying *M. Nagaraj*'s **width and identity tests** and *I.R. Coelho*'s **essence of rights test** rather than expanding the doctrine loosely.
2. **Textual Discipline for Ordinary Legislation:** Anchoring statutory review firmly in *Kuldip Nayar* (2006) and *Anjum Kadari* (Nov 2024)—while striking down laws that breach explicit structural mandates like judicial independence (*Madras Bar Association*, Nov 2025)—prevents judicial overreach into everyday policy.
3. **Institutional Reform & Collegium Transparency:** Addressing legitimate counter-majoritarian concerns post-*NJAC* (2015) requires finalising a transparent **Memorandum of Procedure (MoP)** with objective selection criteria, diversity benchmarks, and reasoned disclosures—reconciling judicial independence with public accountability.
4. **Cooperative-Federal Consensus in Transformative Amendments:** Major structural proposals touching State governance—such as the **129th Amendment Bill (ONOE)**, **130th Amendment Bill**, and **post-2026 delimitation**—should prioritise substantive bipartisan and Inter-State Council consensus alongside faithful observance of `Article 368(2)`'s ratification lock (*Rajendra N. Shah*, 2021).
5. **Respecting Deliberative Parliamentary Norms:** Strengthening scrutiny through Joint Parliamentary Committees, public consultations, and recorded supermajority votes—whose vitality was vividly underscored by the Lok Sabha's April 2026 vote on the 131st Bill—ensures that every constitutional amendment embodies enduring national consensus rather than transient partisan advantage.

## 1. Constitutional Amendment Procedure (Part XX, Article 368)

- **Design & Source:** **Part XX, `Article 368`** borrowed from **South Africa** (+ state ratification); **blend of rigidity & flexibility** praised by **K.C. Wheare** & **Granville Austin**; **Nehru (8 Nov 1948)** warned rigidity halts *"a nation's growth."* **106 Amendment Acts** enacted up to **Nov 2026** (latest in force: `106th Act, 2023`, notified **16 Apr 2026**).
- **24th Amendment (1971) Changes:** Renamed `Art. 368` heading (*"Power of Parliament to amend... and procedure therefor"*); added `368(1)` (constituent power: add, vary, repeal), `368(3)` (exempts from `Art. 13`), and **mandatory Presidential assent** (`368(2)`).
- **Three Amendment Tracks (2 inside `Art. 368` + 1 outside):**
  - **Track 1 — Simple Majority (Outside `Art. 368`; ordinary law, Joint Sitting & Art. 111 veto apply):** `Arts. 2–4` (e.g. **Kerala (Alteration of Name) Act, 2026** → **Keralam**, 25 Aug 2026); `Art. 169` (State Councils after Assembly special-majority resolution); **2nd Schedule**; `100(3)` quorum; `106` MP salaries; `118` rules; `105(3)` privileges; `120(2)` English in Parliament; `124(1)` SC puisne judges (**33 + CJI = 34**); `138` enlarging SC jurisdiction; `343(3)` official language; `11` citizenship; `327` elections; `82/170` delimitation commissions; `239A` UT legislatures; **5th Sch. (Para 7)** & **6th Sch. (Para 21)**.
  - **Track 2 — Special Majority (`Art. 368(2)`):** **>50% of total sanctioned strength** (ignoring vacancies/absentees) **AND ≥ 2/3 present & voting**, **each House separately** (FRs, DPSPs, Duties, rest of Constitution).
  - **Track 3 — Special Majority + ≥ Half the States (14/28, Simple Majority, No Deadline; UTs excluded):** Proviso to `Art. 368(2)` **8 federal items (clauses a–e)**: `Arts. 54, 55` (Presidential polls); `Arts. 73, 162` (Union/State executive power); `Art. 241` (UT HCs); **`Art. 279A` (GST Council — `101st Amend, 2016`)**; SC/HC Chapters (Ch. IV Part V, Ch. V Part VI); legislative distribution (Ch. I Part XI) & **any 7th Schedule List**; State representation (**4th Schedule**); **`Art. 368` itself**.
- **7 Strict Procedural Rules & Traps:** Either House (never State Legislature; RS co-equal); **Minister OR Private Member**; **NO prior Presidential permission**; separate special majority; **NO Joint Sitting (`Art. 108`)**; **mandatory Presidential assent** (`24th Amend`); **Bill No. > Act No.** (`122nd=101st`, `128th=106th`).
- **Majorities Cheat-Sheet:** Simple (>50% P&V); Effective (>50% total minus vacancies — VP/Speaker removal); Special A (2/3 P&V only — `Arts. 249, 312`); Special B (`Art. 368(2)` — >50% total + 2/3 P&V — Amendments, Judges/CAG/CEC removal, `Art. 352`); Super-Special (`Art. 61` — 2/3 **total membership** — Presidential impeachment ONLY).
- **Enforceable Ratification Lock:** Incidental effect exempt (*Shankari Prasad*, *Sajjan Singh*); direct ouster of SC/HC jurisdiction void without ratification (***Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992** — Tenth Schedule **Para 7** void, rest severed 3–2); substantive **"change in effect"** on State List Entry 32 void without ratification (***Rajendra N. Shah*, 20 July 2021** — Part IXB struck down for State co-ops 3–0, saved for multi-State co-ops 2–1; `19(1)(c)` & `43B` untouched).
- **6 Criticisms:** No Constitutional Convention; States can't initiate (save `Art. 169`); Parliament alone amends bulk; no ratification deadline/rescission rule; no joint sitting; sketchy text.

## 3. Evolution of the Basic Structure Doctrine (1951–1973)

- **Root Clash:** **Neither mentioned nor defined** in text; **derived** (Parliament) vs **original** (Constituent Assembly) power; does **"law" in `Art. 13(2)`** cover `Art. 368` amendments?
- ***Shankari Prasad* (1951, 5–0):** `Art. 368` ≠ `Art. 13(2)` "law"; FRs amendable.
- ***Sajjan Singh* (1965, 3–2):** Followed *Shankari Prasad*; **Hidayatullah J.** doubted Part III as *"play things of a special majority"*; **Mudholkar J.** coined **"basic features"** (citing Pakistan's *Fazlul Quader Chowdhry*, 1963). **Prof. Dieter Conrad** lectured at **BHU (Feb 1965)** on *"Implied Limitations"* (Weimar / Germany `Art. 79(3)`).
- ***Golaknath* (1967, 11 JJ, 6–5, CJI Subba Rao):** FRs **"transcendental & immutable"**; `Art. 368` purely procedural (power under `248`/Entry 97); **prospective overruling** saved 1st, 4th, 17th Amdts.
- **24th & 25th Amdts (1971):** Reversed *Golaknath* (`13(4), 368(1)/(3)`, mandatory assent) & added `Art. 31C`.
- ***Kesavananda Bharati* (24 April 1973, 13 JJ, 7–6, CJI Sikri, 68 days):** Overruled *Golaknath*, upheld `24th` & `31C` first limb, **voided `31C` review bar**, established **Basic Structure** via **H.R. Khanna J.'s decisive 7th vote** (excluding Property). Followed by **25 Apr 1973** supersession (→ CJI A.N. Ray) & aborted Nov 1975 review; **24 April 1973** = permanent cut-off.

## 4. Consolidation & Post-Kesavananda Jurisprudence (1975–2026)

- ***Raj Narain* (1975):** **First** amendment voided (`39th`, `Art. 329A(4)`); free & fair elections, rule of law, separation of powers; basic structure tests amendments, not ordinary statutes.
- ***Minerva Mills* (1980):** Voided **`368(4)–(5)` (5–0)** & widened **`31C` (4–1)**; *"donee of a limited power"*; **limited amending power, judicial review, FR–DPSP harmony**.
- ***Waman Rao* (1981) & *I.R. Coelho* (2007, 9 JJ):** **24 Apr 1973** cut-off; post-1973 Ninth Schedule laws face **Rights Test** & **Essence of Rights Test** (**Golden Triangle `14, 19, 21`** + `15`).
- ***L. Chandra Kumar* (1997, 7 JJ):** Overruled *Sampath Kumar* (1987); **`Arts. 32, 226, 227`** review inviolable; tribunals supplemental.
- ***S.R. Bommai* (1994, 9 JJ):** Secularism, federalism, democracy, unity; extended to **`Art. 356` executive proclamations**.
- ***M. Nagaraj* (2006):** Upheld `77th/81st/82nd/85th`; created **Width Test** & **Identity Test**.
- ***NJAC / SCAORA* (16 Oct 2015, 4–1):** Voided **`99th Amend`** (judicial primacy in appointments = Basic Structure; Chelameswar J. dissent).
- **2022–2026 Rulings:** ***Janhit Abhiyan* (Nov 2022, 3–2)** upheld `103rd` EWS; ***In Re: Art. 370* (Dec 2023, 5–0)** upheld `370(3)` abrogation (`370` = temporary **asymmetric federalism**), **voided CO 272's indirect `Art. 367` edit**, directed statehood restoration; ***Anoop Baranwal* (2023) & *Electoral Bonds* (2024)** anchored free polls/ECI (CEC Act 2023 challenge referred to Constitution Bench Sept 2026); ***Anjum Kadari* (5 Nov 2024)** barred voiding ordinary laws on abstract basic structure alone (*Kuldip Nayar*), whereas ***Madras Bar Assn* (19 Nov 2025)** voided Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 clauses breaching embedded judicial independence → **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026** (13 Aug 2026, National Tribunals Commission); ***Property Owners Assn* (5 Nov 2024, 9 JJ)** revived narrower `Art. 31C` (9–0); ***Dr. Balram Singh* (25 Nov 2024)** upheld **"socialist"** & **"secular"** in Preamble.
- **Key Exclusions:** Property (`300A`), British Parliamentary Sovereignty, rigid 50% cap, RS secret ballot are **NOT** Basic Structure.

## 5. Critical Evaluation, Comparative Constitutionalism & Mains Synthesis

- **Jan 2023 Exchange:** VP **Jagdeep Dhankhar** (11 Jan 2023, parliamentary mandate) vs CJI **D.Y. Chandrachud** (21 Jan 2023, Basic Structure as **"North Star"**).
- **Balance Sheet:** Shields against majoritarianism (Emergency test), protects pluralism/federalism, non-paralysing (70+ post-1973 amendments survived) **vs** counter-majoritarian difficulty, uncodified text, indeterminacy, *NJAC* self-insulation critique.
- **Comparative Lens:** **Germany** (`Art. 79(3)` textual Eternity Clause); **Bangladesh** (*Anwar Hossain*, 1989 imported → `Art. 7B`); **Kenya** (*BBI Case*, 31 Mar 2022, 6–1 declined due to `Arts. 255–257` mandatory referendum); **Pakistan** (2015 cautious salient features); **USA** (rigid Art. V); **UK** (Parliamentary Sovereignty).
- **Way Forward:** Preserve Basic Structure as high-threshold safety valve with *Nagaraj*/*Anjum Kadari* discipline; transparent **MoP** for judicial appointments; cooperative-federal consensus via Track 3 ratification & JPC scrutiny on ONOE (`129th Bill`) and post-2026 delimitation.
