## 1. The Chief Minister and the state Council of Ministers

Mirroring the Prime Minister at the Centre, the **Chief Minister** is the real (*de facto*) executive head of a state under Part VI (**Articles 163, 164, 166, 167 and 177**), while the Governor remains the nominal (*de jure*) head.

Under **Article 164(1)**, the Governor appoints the Chief Minister and, on the Chief Minister's advice, the other ministers. Conventionally, the leader of the majority party or coalition in the **Legislative Assembly** is invited; only in a hung Assembly does situational discretion arise, culminating in a floor test (*S.R. Bommai*, 1994). In a bicameral state, a Chief Minister may belong to **either the Assembly or the Legislative Council**—as seen with Nitish Kumar (Bihar), Yogi Adityanath in his first term (Uttar Pradesh) and Uddhav Thackeray (Maharashtra). Under **Article 164(4)**, even a **non-legislator** may serve as Chief Minister or minister for up to **six consecutive months**, though ***S.R. Chaudhuri* (2001)** barred repeated back-to-back re-appointments without winning election.

To curb bloated ministries, the **91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003** added **Articles 164(1A) and 164(1B)**: a state Council of Ministers (including the Chief Minister) **cannot exceed 15 per cent** of the Assembly's strength and **cannot be fewer than 12 ministers**. Under **Article 164(1B)**, a legislator disqualified under the Tenth Schedule cannot become a minister until re-elected or until the House term expires, whichever is earlier.

> **Prelims pointer:** Three classic traps sit inside Article 164. First, States have a **minimum floor of 12 ministers** (including the CM) under Article 164(1A), whereas the Union Council of Ministers has **NO minimum** under Article 75(1A)! Second, **NCT of Delhi** has a separate **10 per cent cap** (maximum 7 ministers in its 70-member Assembly) under **Article 239AA(4)**. Third, under the **proviso to Article 164(1)**, a **Tribal Welfare Minister** is mandatory in **Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh and Odisha**—the **94th Amendment Act, 2006** dropped **Bihar** and added Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand.

The ministry rests on **collective responsibility to the Legislative Assembly** (**Article 164(2)**—never to the Legislative Council; an Assembly no-confidence vote ousts the whole ministry) and **individual responsibility** to the Governor (**Article 164(1)**, exercised on the Chief Minister's advice). **Article 163(3)** bars courts from inquiring into ministerial advice, while **Article 166** places formal executive action in the Governor's name. **Article 167** (mirroring Article 78) obliges the Chief Minister to communicate Council decisions on administration and legislation to the Governor, furnish requested information, and place individual ministerial decisions before the full Council if asked. Under **Article 177** (mirroring Article 88), ministers and the Advocate General may speak and participate in **either House**, without voting in a House of which they are not members. Beyond state administration, the Chief Minister represents the state on the **NITI Aayog Governing Council**, the **Inter-State Council** (Article 263) and, by annual rotation as Vice-Chairman, the regional **Zonal Council**.

> **In the news:** Introduced on **20 August 2025**, the **Constitution (130th Amendment) Bill, 2025** proposes inserting a clause in **Article 164** (alongside Articles 75 and 239AA) under which a Chief Minister or minister detained in custody for **30 consecutive days** on offences carrying five or more years' imprisonment must leave office on the 31st day (automatically ceasing to hold office the day after). Pending before a Joint Parliamentary Committee as of **November 2026**, critics caution that pre-conviction arrests could override the Assembly's authority to choose and remove a Chief Minister.

## 2. Structure of the state legislature and Article 169

Part VI (**Articles 168 to 212**) makes bicameralism optional for states. Every state has a **unicameral** legislature—Governor plus **Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha)**—unless it also maintains a **Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad)**.

As of **November 2026**, **22 states** are unicameral and **only 6 states** are bicameral: **Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana and Uttar Pradesh**. No Union Territory has a Council; Jammu and Kashmir's 36-member Council was abolished by the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019**.

### Creating or abolishing a Legislative Council (Article 169)

Under **Article 169**, Parliament may create or abolish a state's Legislative Council by **simple majority**—but **only after** the State Legislative Assembly passes a resolution by **special majority** (a majority of the Assembly's **total membership** AND at least **two-thirds of members present and voting**).

```text
   State Legislative Assembly (Art 169(1))
   Resolution passed by SPECIAL MAJORITY:
   • > 50% of total membership of Assembly, AND
   • ≥ 2/3rd of members present and voting
                      │
                      ▼
      Parliament (NOT bound to accept)
      Passes an Act by SIMPLE MAJORITY
                      │
                      ▼
    Council Created or Abolished (Art 168 updated)
    Note: NOT an Article 368 Constitutional Amendment!
```

Two Prelims traps stand out. First, under **Article 169(3)**, Parliament's Act is **NOT an Article 368 constitutional amendment**, though it alters Article 168. Second, Parliament is **not bound** to act on a state's resolution: proposals from **Assam** (2005, 2010; 2013 Bill), **Rajasthan** (2012; 2013 Bill), **Odisha** (2018) and **West Bengal** (July 2021, seeking revival after 1969 abolition) remained pending as of **November 2026**. **Andhra Pradesh's** Council was created in 1958, abolished in 1985, revived in 2007 (2005 Act), marked for abolition by a January 2020 resolution, and saved when the Assembly withdrew that resolution in November 2021. **Tamil Nadu** abolished its Council in 1986 (a 2010 revival Act was never notified), while **Madhya Pradesh's** Council (added in Article 168 by the 7th Amendment) was never constituted.

## 3. Composition of the Assembly and the Council

### Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha — Article 170)

Directly elected on universal adult suffrage, an Assembly must have **60 to 500** members (**Article 170(1)**; Uttar Pradesh is largest at **403**).

> **Prelims pointer:** Five states have Part XXI constitutional exceptions below 60: **Sikkim** (Art 371F, min 30; actual **32**), **Arunachal Pradesh** (Art 371H, min 30; actual **60**), **Goa** (Art 371I, min 30; actual **40**), **Mizoram** (Art 371G, min 40; actual **40**) and **Nagaland** (Art 371A, min 46; actual **60**). Among UTs with Assemblies, **Puducherry** has 30 elected seats (plus up to 3 Centre-nominated), **Delhi** 70, and **Jammu and Kashmir** 90 elected seats.

State seat totals remain frozen at the **1971 Census** until the first census after **2026** (84th Amendment, 2001), with internal boundaries based on the **2001 Census** (87th Amendment, 2003). **Article 332** reserves SC/ST seats (extended to 2030), while the **104th Amendment Act, 2019** ended the Governor's nomination of one Anglo-Indian member (**Article 333**). Under **Article 172(1)**, the normal term is **five years**; during a **National Emergency (Article 352)**, Parliament may extend it **one year at a time**, never beyond **six months** after the Emergency ends.

### Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad — Article 171)

A **permanent chamber** not subject to dissolution, **one-third of its members retire every two years** on a **six-year term** (**Article 172(2)**). Under **Article 171(1)**, its strength cannot exceed **one-third of the Assembly** and cannot be **below 40** (Telangana sits at 40); Parliament fixes exact numbers under the Representation of the People Act, 1950.

Unless Parliament alters the scheme, **Article 171(3)** fills the Council through a **five-channel indirect formula** using **proportional representation by single transferable vote (PR-STV)**:
1. **1/3rd** elected by **local bodies** (municipalities, district boards, etc.);
2. **1/12th** elected by **graduates** of three years' standing residing in the state;
3. **1/12th** elected by **teachers** of three years' standing (secondary school and above);
4. **1/3rd** elected by **MLAs** from among **non-members** of the Assembly;
5. **1/6th** **nominated by the Governor** from **literature, science, art, cooperative movement and social service** (**Article 171(5)**).

> **Remember:** Spot the 5th nomination field! Under Article 171(5), Governors nominate MLCs from five fields—adding **"cooperative movement"**—whereas under Article 80(3) the President nominates Rajya Sabha MPs from only **four** fields (no cooperative movement!).

## 4. Qualifications, disqualifications, sessions, presiding officers and privileges

**Qualifications (Article 173 & RPA 1951):** Citizen of India; subscribed oath; minimum age **25 years** (Assembly) or **30 years** (Council); elector in an assembly constituency of that state (Governor's Council nominees must reside in the state).

**Disqualifications (Articles 190–192 & Tenth Schedule):** **Article 191** disqualifies for office of profit, unsound mind, undischarged insolvency, non-citizenship, or statutory RPA 1951 grounds (e.g., two-year conviction under Section 8(3)). **Article 190** prohibits simultaneous membership across Houses and vacates a seat after **60 days** of unpermitted absence. Crucially, **Article 191** disqualifications are decided by the **Governor** acting on the **binding opinion of the Election Commission of India** (**Article 192**), whereas **Tenth Schedule** anti-defection disqualifications are decided by the **Speaker** or **Chairman**, subject to judicial review (*Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992).

**Sessions, quorum and presiding officers:** Under **Article 174**, the gap between sessions cannot exceed **six months** (at least two sessions yearly). Quorum under **Article 189(3)** is **ten members or one-tenth of total membership, whichever is greater** (protecting small Assemblies like Sikkim). The Assembly elects a **Speaker** (who continues past dissolution until the new Assembly's first sitting, certifies **Money Bills**, casts a tie-breaking vote, and is removable by **effective majority** after 14 days' notice) and **Deputy Speaker** (**Article 178**). Unlike the Rajya Sabha's ex-officio Vice-President, a Council's **Chairman** and **Deputy Chairman** are elected from **among the Council's own members** (**Article 182**).

**Privileges (Article 194):** Mirroring Article 105, Article 194 protects legislative speech, votes and House procedure. In ***Sita Soren v. Union of India* (March 2024)**, a seven-judge bench overruled *P.V. Narasimha Rao* (1998), holding that **Articles 105(2) and 194(2) do NOT protect legislators from prosecution for bribery** tied to legislative votes or speeches.

## 5. Legislative procedure: why the Council cannot match the Rajya Sabha

Unlike Parliament, **state legislatures have NO provision for a Joint Sitting**. Under **Articles 196–197**, if the Assembly passes an **Ordinary Bill** and the Council rejects it, amends it unacceptably, or delays for **three months**, the Assembly may pass it a **second time**. If the Council again rejects, alters or holds it for **one month**, the Bill is **deemed passed by both Houses** as approved by the Assembly the second time—capping the Council's delaying power at **four months (3 + 1)**. Worse, a Council-originated Ordinary Bill rejected by the Assembly dies instantly. For **Money Bills (Articles 198–199)**, introduction occurs only in the Assembly with the Governor's prior recommendation, the Speaker's certificate is final, and the Council can only recommend changes within **14 days**.

```text
            Ordinary Bill passed by Assembly
                           │
                           ▼
             Sent to Legislative Council
     ┌─────────────────────┼─────────────────────┐
     ▼                     ▼                     ▼
  Passes             Rejects / Amends /      Delays > 3
(To Governor)        unacceptable changes     months
                           └──────────┬──────────┘
                                      ▼
                     Assembly passes Bill 2nd time
                                      │
                                      ▼
                     Sent back to Council (max 1 mo)
                                      │
                                      ▼
              Deemed PASSED by both Houses as passed
              by Assembly 2nd time (NO Joint Sitting)
              ── Max Council delay: 3 + 1 = 4 months ──
```

The Council also has **no vote** on Demands for Grants, **cannot pass a no-confidence motion**, has **no vote in Presidential elections** (Article 54) or Rajya Sabha polls, and has **no role in ratifying Constitutional Amendments** under Article 368(2) (Assemblies only).

| Dimension | Rajya Sabha (Union) | State Legislative Council |
|---|---|---|
| **Existence** | Permanent, mandatory; cannot be abolished | Optional (6 states); created/abolished via Art 169 |
| **Presiding officer** | Vice-President of India (ex-officio) | Chairman elected from Council members (Art 182) |
| **Nominations** | 12 members from **4 fields** (Art 80(3)) | 1/6th from **5 fields**—adds **cooperative movement** |
| **Ordinary Bill deadlock** | Co-equal; resolved via **Joint Sitting** (Art 108) | Subordinate; max **4-month delay** (3 + 1); **NO Joint Sitting** |
| **Constitutional amendments** | Co-equal passage required (Art 368) | **No role** in ratification (Assemblies only) |
| **Presidential election** | Elected MPs vote (Art 54) | **No role** |

## 6. Legislative Councils and the health of state legislatures: Mains analysis

### Legislative Councils: revising chamber or costly anachronism?

Proponents argue the Vidhan Parishad acts as a **revising chamber** checking hasty legislation, brings **domain experts, local-body leaders, teachers, graduates and cooperators** into lawmaking, and broadens regional representation in large states. Critics counter that its weak four-month delay makes it an expensive **"dilatory chamber"** often misused as a **"rehabilitation home for defeated politicians"**, while partisan creation-and-abolition swings (as in Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu) undermine institutional stability and outdated teachers'/graduates' electorates need rethinking. Addressing this inconsistency, the **Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice** (63rd Report, Dec 2013 on Rajasthan; 70th Report, Feb 2014 on Assam) urged a **National Policy on Legislative Councils**, alongside reviewing teachers'/graduates' seats and strengthening Panchayati Raj representation.

### Declining health of state legislatures

Even more pressing is the erosion of deliberation inside primary Assemblies. **PRS Legislative Research's *Annual Review of State Laws 2025* (released May 2026)**, covering 27 states and 3 UTs (Manipur did not sit under President's Rule from Feb 2025 to Feb 2026), documents five troubling trends:

- **Shrinking sitting days:** Assemblies averaged just **24 sitting days in 2025** (133 hours; up slightly from 21 in 2024 and 23 in 2023), ranging from **43 days in Odisha** to **7 days in Nagaland** (2021–25 five-year averages: Kerala highest at 41, Odisha 39, Karnataka 37; Nagaland 8, Tripura 9). Except **Himachal Pradesh**, no state met its own statutory or rule-based minimum sitting target.
- **Rushed lawmaking:** Over **600 Bills** passed in 2025 (Karnataka 84 in 34 days; Assam 60 in 21 days), with **30 per cent passed on the day of introduction**; seven legislatures (Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Gujarat, Jharkhand, Mizoram, Puducherry, Punjab) passed **all** Bills within a day.
- **Weak committee and budget scrutiny:** Across six states with published records, only **5 per cent of Bills** went to committees (Kerala, with its Subject Committees, led), and budgets are passed after only a few days of debate.
- **Ordinance overuse:** States issued **127 ordinances in 2025** (up 27 per cent from 100 in 2024), led by local governance (31 per cent) and education (13 per cent).
- **Vacant Deputy Speakers:** Despite **Article 178**, several legislatures have no Deputy Speaker; Jharkhand has gone more than **21 years** without one.

Revitalising state legislatures demands five concrete reforms: mandating **60–90 minimum sitting days yearly** (echoing **NCRWC 2002**—50 for small states, 90 for large—and Presiding Officers' Conferences); establishing permanent **Kerala-style Subject Committees** with mandatory Bill and budget referral; enforcing *Krishna Kumar Singh* (2017) against ordinance abuse; filling Deputy Speaker posts promptly (conventionally from the Opposition); and expanding **live-streaming, National e-Vidhan Application (NeVA) digitisation, and dedicated research staff** for MLAs.

> **Mains angle:** Anchor GS-2 answers in **PRS 2025–26 metrics** (24 sitting days, 30% of Bills passed the day they were introduced, about 5% sent to committees, 127 ordinances). Pair critique with balanced reforms—a **National Policy on Legislative Councils** (Standing Committee, 2013–14) for bicameralism, and NCRWC sitting-day floors plus Subject Committees for Assemblies—concluding that vigorous state legislatures are essential to democratic accountability and cooperative federalism.
