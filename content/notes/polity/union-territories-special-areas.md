## 1. Union Territories: Creation and Administration

A textbook reading of `Article 1` reveals an architectural nuance that goes to the heart of India's constitutional design. While `Article 1(1)` declares India to be a **"Union of States"**, `Article 1(3)` defines the broader **"Territory of India"** across three distinct constitutional categories: the territories of the States, the **Union Territories** specified in the `First Schedule`, and such other territories as may be acquired. This deliberate distinction means that whereas India's twenty-eight States share sovereign legislative and executive powers with the Centre through a federal division under the `Seventh Schedule`, the **eight Union Territories** stand in a fundamentally different constitutional relationship with New Delhi—governed directly by the Union Government under `Part VIII` (`Articles 239 to 241`).

Viewed alongside the protective regimes for **Scheduled Areas** (`Fifth Schedule`, `Article 244(1)`), **Tribal Areas** (`Sixth Schedule`, `Articles 244(2)` and `275(1)`), and the **Special Provisions for Twelve States** (`Part XXI`, `Articles 371 to 371J`), Union Territories represent one pillar of India's wider architecture of **asymmetric federalism**—a calibrated constitutional toolkit designed to reconcile national unity, administrative efficiency, cultural distinctiveness, strategic security, and tribal self-determination within a single republican framework.

```keyfacts
12 Part XXI States | Articles 371 to 371J | Asymmetric shields from Maharashtra & Gujarat (371) to Karnataka (371J, 98th Amendment 2012)
```

### Evolution of Union Territories from Colonial Provinces to Part VIII

The institutional ancestry of India's Union Territories traces directly to the **Scheduled Districts Act of 1874** and the **Chief Commissioners' Provinces** established during British rule under the Government of India Acts of 1919 and 1935. Territories such as Delhi, Ajmer-Merwara, Coorg, British Baluchistan, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and Panth Piploda were considered too small, strategically sensitive, or administratively distinct to function as full Governors' Provinces and were therefore placed under centrally appointed Chief Commissioners responsible directly to the Governor-General.

When the Constitution commenced on **26 January 1950**, the framers retained this transitional asymmetry through a **four-fold territorial classification** in the `First Schedule`:
- **Part A States (9):** Erstwhile Governors' Provinces of British India (Assam, Bihar, Bombay, Madhya Pradesh, Madras, Orissa, Punjab, United Provinces, and West Bengal).
- **Part B States (9):** Erstwhile major princely states and princely unions headed by Rajpramukhs (Hyderabad, Jammu & Kashmir, Madhya Bharat, Mysore, PEPSU, Rajasthan, Saurashtra, Travancore-Cochin, and Vindhya Pradesh initially before shifting to Part C).
- **Part C States (10):** Centrally administered territories combining erstwhile Chief Commissioners' Provinces and smaller princely states (Ajmer, Bhopal, Bilaspur, Cooch Behar initially, Coorg, Delhi, Himachal Pradesh, Kutch, Manipur, Tripura, and Vindhya Pradesh).
- **Part D Territory (1):** Exclusively the **Andaman and Nicobar Islands**, administered by the President through a Chief Commissioner with plenary Presidential regulation-making authority.

This four-tier hierarchy quickly proved unwieldy and politically contentious. Part C States lacked financial viability and uniform democratic structures, while their citizens resented second-class status relative to Part A States. In 1953, the Union Government appointed the **States Reorganisation Commission (SRC)** under **Justice Fazl Ali** (with **H.N. Kunzru** and **K.M. Panikkar**). Submitting its landmark report in **September 1955**, the Commission recommended abolishing the colonial A–B–C–D hierarchy altogether and replacing it with a crisp two-tier framework: **States** (forming the primary federal units) and **Centrally Administered Territories** (designated as **Union Territories**) for enclaves that could neither be merged into neighbouring States nor sustain full statehood independently.

Acting on these recommendations, Parliament enacted the **Seventh Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956** alongside the **States Reorganisation Act, 1956** (both effective **1 November 1956**). Part VII (governing Part B States) was repealed, `Part VIII` was comprehensively recast to govern **Union Territories**, and India was reorganised into **14 States and 6 Union Territories**: **Delhi**, **Himachal Pradesh**, **Manipur**, **Tripura**, the **Andaman and Nicobar Islands**, and the **Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands** (renamed **Lakshadweep** by a 1973 Act).

Over the seven decades since 1956, the map of Union Territories has acted both as an **incubator for future full statehood** and as a **flexible administrative instrument** for absorbing former colonial enclaves, resolving inter-state capital disputes, and managing sensitive border territories. Six erstwhile Union Territories eventually graduated to full statehood, while new territories were inducted via constitutional amendments, reorganisation statutes, and territorial mergers:

Meanwhile, several territories entered the Union Territory fold through distinct historical pathways: **Dadra and Nagar Haveli** (liberated from Portuguese rule in 1954; made a UT via the **`10th Amendment Act, 1961`**), **Puducherry** (former French establishments of Pondicherry, Karaikal, Mahe, and Yanam handed over in 1954, formally ceded in 1962, and constituted as a UT via the **`14th Amendment Act, 1962`**; renamed from Pondicherry to Puducherry in 2006), **Chandigarh** (created in **1966** via the Punjab Reorganisation Act as the shared capital of Punjab and Haryana), **Jammu and Kashmir** and **Ladakh** (created on **31 October 2019** via the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019**—the first instance in independent India of a full State being converted into Union Territories), and the merged UT of **Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu** (created on **26 January 2020** by Parliament's **Merger of Union Territories Act, 2019**, reducing the total number of UTs from nine back to **eight**).

### Four Rationales Behind Creating Union Territories

Why does a federal democracy maintain centrally administered territories at all rather than converting every parcel of land into a full State? Constitutional history and the debates of the Fazl Ali Commission reveal **four distinct rationales**, each anchored in real geographical, political, and cultural imperatives:

1. **Political and Administrative Considerations (`Delhi` and `Chandigarh`):** Federal capitals—like Washington, D.C. and Canberra—are kept under national supervision so central diplomacy, security, federal installations, and policing never depend on a single State government. **Delhi** reflects this capital rationale, while **Chandigarh** reflects inter-state arbitration: following Punjab's 1966 linguistic bifurcation, both Punjab and Haryana claimed Le Corbusier's planned city, leading Parliament to designate Chandigarh as a neutral centrally administered UT serving as their joint capital.
2. **Cultural Distinctiveness (`Puducherry` and `Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu`):** Former French and Portuguese enclaves inherited distinct legal codes, architecture, and administrative traditions markedly different from British-Indian provinces. Merging **Puducherry** (Pondicherry, Karaikal, Mahe, Yanam) or **Daman, Diu, Dadra, and Nagar Haveli** immediately into neighbouring States risked submerging their Franco-Indian and Indo-Portuguese cultural identities; Union stewardship preserved their distinctiveness during democratic integration.
3. **Strategic and Maritime Importance (`Andaman & Nicobar Islands` and `Lakshadweep`):** Lying far off India's mainland across vital maritime chokepoints—the **Six Degree and Ten Degree Channels** near the **Malacca Strait** (hosting India's sole Tri-Services Command at Sri Vijaya Puram) and western Indian Ocean trade lanes passing **Lakshadweep**—both archipelagos combine tiny populations, fragile coral/rainforest ecologies, fiscal dependence, and critical defence imperatives requiring unified central command.
4. **Tribal Welfare, Backwardness, and Border Security (`Erstwhile NE UTs`, `Himachal Pradesh`, and `Jammu & Kashmir` and `Ladakh`):** Historically, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, Manipur, Tripura, and Himachal Pradesh received Union administration to channel concentrated development funds and protect vulnerable indigenous communities until achieving maturity for full statehood. In 2019, Parliament invoked national security, cross-border terrorism, developmental integration, and Ladakh's distinct high-altitude identity to bifurcate Jammu and Kashmir into two UTs—with the Union assuring Parliament and the Supreme Court that J&K's UT status is temporary pending full statehood restoration.

### The Eight Current Union Territories: Complete Architectural Profile

Following the bifurcation of Jammu and Kashmir (31 October 2019) and the administrative merger of Dadra & Nagar Haveli with Daman & Diu (26 January 2020), India currently comprises **28 States and 8 Union Territories**. Although all eight UTs share the legal label of Union Territory under `Part VIII`, there is **no constitutional uniformity** in their internal governance—three possess popularly elected Legislative Assemblies and Councils of Ministers, while five are governed directly by the President through an Administrator without a legislature.

> [!prelims]
> **Five High-Yield Parliamentary & Electoral College Traps on Union Territories:**
> 1. **Lok Sabha Representation (`Art. 81`):** All 8 Union Territories are represented in the Lok Sabha with a combined total of **19 elected MPs** (against the constitutional ceiling of 20 UT seats under `Article 81(1)(b)`). Note that **Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu** retains **2 Lok Sabha seats** (one each for DNH and Daman & Diu) even after the 2020 merger.
> 2. **Rajya Sabha Representation (`Art. 80` & `Fourth Schedule`):** ONLY the **three UTs with Legislative Assemblies** have Rajya Sabha seats—**Delhi (3)**, **Puducherry (1)**, and **Jammu & Kashmir (4)** (totalling **8 seats**; J&K's 4 vacant seats were filled via indirect elections by the new J&K Assembly on **24 October 2025**, with JKNC winning 3 and BJP winning 1). The other five UTs have **zero** Rajya Sabha representation.
> 3. **Presidential Electoral College (`Article 54`) Trap:** The **70th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992** inserted an Explanation in `Article 54` expressly naming ONLY the **NCT of Delhi** and the **UT of Puducherry** so their elected MLAs vote in Presidential elections. Because `Article 54` has **not yet been amended** (which requires a special majority plus ratification by at least half the States under `Article 368(2)`), the elected MLAs of the **UT of Jammu and Kashmir** are **NOT yet part of the Presidential Electoral College**!
> 4. **Dedicated High Court:** **Delhi (1966)** is the **ONLY Union Territory** with its own exclusive High Court; J&K and Ladakh share a common High Court (`High Court of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh`, renamed July 2021).
> 5. **Capital Renaming:** Port Blair, capital of the Andaman & Nicobar Islands, was officially renamed **Sri Vijaya Puram** on **13 September 2024**.

### Constitutional Machinery of Administration: Part VIII (Articles 239 to 241)

`Part VIII` of the Constitution (`Articles 239` to `241`—note that `Article 242` relating to Coorg was repealed by the 7th Amendment Act, 1956) lays down the compact yet powerful constitutional framework governing Union Territories. Every clause carries sharp distinctions that UPSC Prelims tests relentlessly.

#### Article 239: The President and the Administrator as an Agent

`Article 239(1)` establishes the foundational principle of Union Territory governance: *"Save as otherwise provided by Parliament by law, every Union territory shall be administered by the President acting, to such extent as he thinks fit, through an administrator to be appointed by him with such designation as he may specify."*

This single sentence creates a structural gulf between a **State Governor** and a **UT Administrator**. A Governor under `Part VI` (`Articles 153–163`) is a **Constitutional Head of State** who holds an independent constitutional office and is ordinarily bound by the aid and advice of the State Council of Ministers. By contrast, an Administrator of a Union Territory—regardless of whether the President designates him or her as **Lieutenant Governor**, **Chief Commissioner**, or **Administrator**—is legally an **agent (delegate) of the President**, not a Constitutional Head of State. Because the President acts on the aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers under `Article 74`, real administrative superintendence over all Union Territories rests with the **Union Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA)**.

Currently, the President uses two designations across the eight UTs:
- **Lieutenant Governor (5 UTs):** NCT of Delhi, Puducherry, Andaman & Nicobar Islands, Jammu & Kashmir, and Ladakh.
- **Administrator (3 UTs):** Chandigarh, Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu, and Lakshadweep.

Equally crucial for Prelims is `Article 239(2)`, which empowers the President to appoint the **Governor of an adjoining State** as the **Administrator of a Union Territory** (a power continuously exercised since **1984** by designating the **Governor of Punjab** as ex-officio **Administrator of Chandigarh**). Whenever a State Governor serves concurrently in this dual role, `Article 239(2)` explicitly mandates that the Governor *"shall exercise his functions as such administrator independently of his Council of Ministers."* Thus, the Governor of Punjab does **not** take advice from the Chief Minister or Cabinet of Punjab when administering Chandigarh, acting instead directly under the Union Government's instructions.

> [!remember]
> **State Governor vs UT Administrator / Lieutenant Governor (Prelims Anchor):**
> - **Status:** Governor = **Constitutional Head of State** (`Art. 153`); UT Administrator/LG = **Agent of the President** (`Art. 239(1)`).
> - **Dual-Hat Rule (`Art. 239(2)`):** When the Governor of Punjab administers Chandigarh, he acts **independently of the Punjab Council of Ministers**.
> - **Discretionary Latitude:** Even in UTs with Legislative Assemblies (Delhi, Puducherry, J&K), the LG enjoys substantially wider statutory and constitutional reference powers (e.g., referring any difference of opinion to the President) than a State Governor enjoys under `Article 163`.

#### Plenary Legislative Power of Parliament (Article 246(4))

In India's federal division of legislative powers (`Part XI`), Parliament ordinarily cannot legislate on matters enumerated in the **State List (List II)** for a State except under specific extraordinary provisions (`Articles 249, 250, 252, 253, 356`). Union Territories, however, are deliberately exempted from this federal barrier.

Under **`Article 246(4)`**, *"Parliament has power to make laws with respect to any matter for any part of the territory of India not included in a State notwithstanding that such matter is a matter enumerated in the State List."* This confers **plenary, overriding legislative competence** upon Parliament across **all three Lists**—Union List, State List, and Concurrent List—for **every single Union Territory**.

Crucially, **creating a Legislative Assembly for a Union Territory does NOT extinguish Parliament's plenary power over List II for that UT**. Whether in Puducherry (`Art. 239A` and the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963), the NCT of Delhi (`Art. 239AA(3)(b)`), or Jammu & Kashmir (`Section 32(2)` of the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019), Parliament retains concurrent authority to enact laws on every State List subject—even those ordinarily allotted to the UT Assembly—and in the event of any repugnancy between a law made by the UT Assembly and a law made by Parliament, **Parliament's law invariably prevails** (unless, in the narrow constitutional exception of `Art. 239AA(3)(c)` for Delhi, a subsequent Delhi law is reserved for and receives the **assent of the President**).

#### President's Regulation-Making Power (Article 240) and Classic Prelims Traps

Because five Union Territories have no Legislative Assemblies of their own—and because Parliament cannot devote scarce floor time to routine local legislation for small islands or frontier enclaves—`Article 240` empowers the **President of India** to make **Regulations** *"for the peace, progress and good government"* of specified Union Territories.

Under **`Article 240(2)`**, a Presidential Regulation carries extraordinary legislative potency: it **has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament** applying to that territory, and may expressly **repeal or amend any Act made by Parliament or any other law** currently in force in that Union Territory. Unlike Ordinances under `Article 123`, which are temporary measures that lapse six weeks after Parliament reassembles unless approved by both Houses, **Regulations under `Article 240` are permanent legislation** that require no subsequent parliamentary ratification.

The **territorial scope** of `Article 240(1)` is one of UPSC Prelims' most frequently tested precision points:
1. **`Article 240(1)(a)`:** The **Andaman and Nicobar Islands** — unconditional Presidential regulation power.
2. **`Article 240(1)(b)`:** **Lakshadweep** *(renamed from Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands in 1973)* — unconditional.
3. **`Article 240(1)(c)`:** **Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu** *(consolidated into clause (c) by the Merger Act, 2019 w.e.f. 26 January 2020; clause (d), which formerly listed Daman and Diu, was omitted)* — unconditional.
4. **`Article 240(1)(e)`:** **Puducherry** *(subject to two vital provisos inserted by the `14th Amendment, 1962` and `27th Amendment, 1971`)* — once Puducherry's Legislative Assembly met under `Article 239A`, the President **cannot** issue Regulations for Puducherry while its Assembly is functioning; **HOWEVER**, whenever Puducherry's Assembly is **dissolved or suspended**, the President's `Article 240` regulation-making power revives for the duration of such dissolution or suspension!
5. **UT of Ladakh (Statutory Extension via `Section 58(2)` of the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019):** Although Parliament did not directly add a new sub-clause `(f)` into the bare alphabetical text of `Article 240(1)`, **`Section 58(2)`** of the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** explicitly vested the President with power to make Regulations *"for the peace, progress and good government of the Union territory of Ladakh under article 240 of the Constitution."* Exercising this exact joint power (`Article 240` read with `Section 58(2)`), the President promulgated **four landmark Regulations for Ladakh on 2 June 2025** covering reservation, domicile, official languages, and women's quota in Hill Councils!

> [!prelims]
> **The Classic `Article 240` Exclusion Trap:**
> Which Union Territories are **OUTSIDE** the President's Regulation-making power under `Article 240`?
> - **NCT of Delhi:** **NO** `Article 240` power (laws are made by Delhi Assembly or Parliament; even when `Art. 239AB` President's Rule operates, Parliament—not `Art. 240`—legislates).
> - **Chandigarh:** **NO** `Article 240` power! Despite having **no Legislative Assembly**, Chandigarh is conspicuously **NOT** listed in `Article 240(1)`. Laws for Chandigarh are enacted by Parliament or extended by the Central Government under the Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966 (`Section 87`, extending existing laws of any State to Chandigarh). *(UPSC loves pairing Chandigarh with A&N/Lakshadweep as a trap!)*
> - **UT of Jammu & Kashmir:** **NO** `Article 240` power (Section 58(2) applied `Art. 240` strictly to **Ladakh**, NOT to J&K!).

#### Ordinance-Making Power of Administrators (Article 239B)

Inserted by the **27th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971**, `Article 239B` empowers the **Administrator** of a Union Territory having a Legislature under `Article 239A` (presently **Puducherry**) to promulgate **Ordinances** during the recess of the Legislative Assembly when immediate legislative action is required. Unlike a State Governor under `Article 213`, however, a UT Administrator **cannot** promulgate an Ordinance on his own initiative—he requires the **prior instructions of the President** in every single case! Furthermore, no Ordinance may be promulgated when the Assembly is dissolved or suspended (for Puducherry, `Article 240` Presidential Regulations take over in that contingency). Every Ordinance must be laid before the Assembly upon reassembly and ceases to operate **six weeks** from reassembly unless approved earlier, and may be withdrawn at any time by the Administrator under Presidential instructions. Analogous statutory Ordinance-making powers (likewise requiring prior Presidential instructions) govern the **LG of Delhi** under the GNCTD Act, 1991 (`Section 239B`-style framework in `Sec. 44`/`Sec. 239AA`) and the **LG of Jammu & Kashmir** under `Section 52` of the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019.

#### High Courts for Union Territories (Article 241)

Under **`Article 241`**, Parliament may by law constitute a separate High Court for a Union Territory, declare any court in such territory to be a High Court, or extend or exclude the jurisdiction of a State High Court over any Union Territory or part thereof. Until Parliament alters the arrangement, every High Court exercising jurisdiction over a territory prior to the Constitution's commencement continues to do so (`Art. 241(3)`).

Exercising `Article 241`, Parliament established the **Delhi High Court** in **1966** (the sole exclusive High Court for a single UT). When Jammu and Kashmir was reorganised in 2019, the pre-existing J&K High Court was designated as the **common High Court** for both the UT of Jammu & Kashmir and the UT of Ladakh (officially renamed the **High Court of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh** in July 2021). All remaining five UTs are attached to geographically proximate State High Courts: **Calcutta High Court** for Andaman & Nicobar Islands, **Kerala High Court** for Lakshadweep, **Bombay High Court** for Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu, **Madras High Court** for Puducherry, and the **Punjab and Haryana High Court** for Chandigarh.

#### Advisory Committees of the Ministry of Home Affairs

Under the **Government of India (Allocation of Business) Rules, 1961**, the **Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA)** serves as the nodal ministry for all Union Territories regarding legislation, finance and budget, cadre/services, and appointments of Lieutenant Governors and Administrators. Because the **five Union Territories without Legislative Assemblies** (Andaman & Nicobar Islands, Chandigarh, Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu, Lakshadweep, and Ladakh) lack an elected UT-level legislature to voice local socio-economic concerns, the Union Government instituted a two-tier consultative mechanism:

- **Home Minister's Advisory Committee (HMAC):** Chaired by the **Union Home Minister** at the national level; meets periodically to deliberate on policy priorities, budgetary needs, infrastructure, and socio-economic development of each UT without a legislature.
- **Administrator's Advisory Committee (AAC):** Chaired locally by the **Administrator / Lieutenant Governor** of the concerned UT; provides a regular local forum for administrative feedback and grievance redressal.

Members of both HMAC and AAC include the UT's elected **Member(s) of Parliament**, elected chairpersons and representatives from grassroots **Panchayati Raj Institutions (District Panchayats)** and **Urban Local Bodies (Municipal Councils/Corporations)**, and nominated representatives from civil society, trade, and vulnerable sections. While valuable as consultative channels, critics note that their recommendations are purely **advisory and non-binding**, leaving a persistent democratic deficit in non-legislature UTs—a tension vividly illustrated by recent civil-society protests in Lakshadweep (2021) and Ladakh (2020–2026).

## 2. Special Status of NCT of Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu and Kashmir, and Ladakh

Among India's eight Union Territories, no two illustrate the constitutional complexities of shared sovereignty, representative democracy, and central oversight more vividly than the **National Capital Territory (NCT) of Delhi** and the bifurcated territories of **Jammu and Kashmir** and **Ladakh**—with **Puducherry** furnishing the statutory baseline against which both are constitutionally measured. Understanding the exact textual distinctions across `Article 239A`, `Article 239AA`, and the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** is indispensable for both UPSC Prelims and Mains GS Paper II.

### Special Constitutional Status of NCT of Delhi (69th Amendment Act, 1991)

Delhi's post-Independence constitutional trajectory reflects an enduring tension between two competing democratic ideals: the right of over twenty million residents of the national capital to an accountable, elected local government, and the imperative that the Union Government retain seamless control over national security, diplomacy, policing, and federal land in the seat of the Republic.

Originally placed in **Part C** in 1950 with a Legislative Assembly (1951–1956), Delhi lost its legislature in **1956** under the States Reorganisation Act and became a centrally administered Union Territory. In **1966**, Parliament enacted the **Delhi Administration Act** establishing an elected **Metropolitan Council** (56 elected + 5 nominated members) and a four-member Executive Council—yet this body was purely **advisory**, possessing neither legislative nor financial authority. Growing popular demand for full statehood prompted the Union Government in December 1987 to appoint the **S. Balakrishnan Committee** on the Reorganisation of Delhi Set-up. Submitting its landmark report in **December 1989**, the Committee rejected full statehood (warning that dual sovereign control over a national capital would endanger national administration) and instead recommended a **constitutional *sui generis* framework**: a directly elected Assembly and responsible Council of Ministers with constitutional protection, while reserving police, public order, and land exclusively for the Union.

Acting on the Balakrishnan blueprint, Parliament enacted the **69th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1991** (effective **1 February 1992**), inserting **`Article 239AA`** and **`Article 239AB`** into `Part VIII`, complemented by the statutory **Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi (GNCTD) Act, 1991**.

#### Core Architectural Provisions of Article 239AA

Unlike Puducherry and Jammu & Kashmir—whose legislatures exist by virtue of ordinary Parliamentary statutes enacted under `Article 239A`—the Legislative Assembly and Council of Ministers of Delhi derive their existence **directly from the text of the Constitution (`Article 239AA`)**, giving Delhi a uniquely entrenched constitutional stature among Union Territories:

- **Nomenclature and Administrator (`Art. 239AA(1)`):** The Union Territory of Delhi was redesignated as the **National Capital Territory of Delhi (NCTD)**, and its Administrator appointed under `Article 239` was constitutionally designated as the **Lieutenant Governor (LG)**.
- **Legislative Assembly (`Art. 239AA(2)`):** Fixed at **70 directly elected members** chosen from territorial constituencies across Delhi, with elections conducted by the Election Commission of India. (Crucially, there are **no nominated MLAs** in the Delhi Assembly—unlike Puducherry and J&K.)
- **Council of Ministers — The 10% Cap (`Art. 239AA(4)`):** There shall be a Council of Ministers consisting of **not more than ten per cent (10%)** of the total number of members in the Legislative Assembly—i.e., a maximum of **7 Ministers including the Chief Minister**—to aid and advise the Lieutenant Governor. *(Classic Prelims Trap: States enjoy a **15% cap** under `Article 164(1A)` inserted by the `91st Amendment`, whereas Delhi—and J&K under its 2019 Act—is capped at **10%**!)*
- **Appointment and Collective Responsibility (`Art. 239AA(5)–(6)`):** A signature Prelims trap lies in **who appoints Delhi's Chief Minister**. Unlike a State Chief Minister (appointed by the Governor under `Art. 164`), the **Chief Minister of Delhi is appointed by the PRESIDENT of India** (NOT the Lieutenant Governor!), and the other Ministers are appointed by the **President on the advice of the Chief Minister**. Ministers hold office during the **pleasure of the President**, while remaining **collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly**.
- **Legislative Competence and the Three Excluded Entries (`Art. 239AA(3)(a)`):** The Delhi Legislative Assembly may make laws for the whole or any part of the NCTD on all matters in the **State List (List II)** and the **Concurrent List (List III)** insofar as applicable to Union Territories, **EXCEPT three explicitly excluded entries of the State List**:
  1. **Entry 1:** **Public Order**;
  2. **Entry 2:** **Police**;
  3. **Entry 18:** **Land** *(plus Entries 64, 65, and 66 of List II insofar as they relate to offences, court jurisdiction, and fees connected with Entries 1, 2, and 18)*.
- **Parliament's Overriding Supremacy (`Art. 239AA(3)(b)–(c)`):** Nothing in `Art. 239AA(3)(a)` derogates from Parliament's plenary power under `Art. 246(4)` to legislate on **any** List II or List III matter for Delhi. If a Delhi Assembly law is repugnant to a Parliamentary law (whether earlier or later), **Parliament's law prevails** unless the Delhi Bill was reserved for and received the **assent of the President** (and even then, Parliament may subsequently amend, vary, or repeal it).

#### The Difference of Opinion Proviso and Article 239AB

Two further constitutional provisions have proved decisive in shaping executive governance in the capital:

- **Proviso to `Article 239AA(4)` ("Difference of Opinion"):** While the LG ordinarily acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers in matters within the Assembly's legislative competence, the proviso stipulates that **in the case of a difference of opinion between the Lieutenant Governor and his Ministers on *"any matter"***, the Lieutenant Governor **shall refer it to the President for decision** and act according to the President's decision. Pending the President's decision, if the matter is too urgent to brook delay, the LG is empowered to take such immediate action or give such direction as he deems necessary.
- **`Article 239AB` (Failure of Constitutional Machinery):** Modeled functionally on `Article 356` (President's Rule in States), `Article 239AB` empowers the **President**, upon receiving a report from the Lieutenant Governor or otherwise, if satisfied that a situation has arisen in which the administration of the NCTD cannot be carried on in accordance with `Article 239AA` or any law made thereunder—or that it is necessary or expedient for the proper administration of the NCTD—to **by order suspend the operation of any provision of `Article 239AA`** for such period and subject to such conditions as specified, and make incidental/consequential provisions (including bringing Delhi under direct Presidential administration). Notably, `Article 239AB` is **independent of `Article 356`** (which applies strictly to States); President's Rule was imposed in Delhi under `Article 239AB` between February 2014 and February 2015.

#### The Constitutional Litigation and Statutory Arc (2018–2025)

For over two decades after 1993, political alignment between New Delhi and the Delhi Secretariat—or quiet conventions of restraint—kept the latent ambiguities of `Article 239AA(4)` dormant. Once ideologically rival parties took charge at the Centre (BJP, May 2014) and in Delhi (AAP, February 2015), a protracted six-phase constitutional struggle unfolded across the Supreme Court and Parliament:

1. **Phase I — *GNCTD v. Union of India* (4 July 2018, 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** Triggered by a May 2015 MHA notification reserving "Services" and the Anti-Corruption Branch for the LG, a Constitution Bench of **CJI Dipak Misra** and **Justices A.K. Sikri, A.M. Khanwilkar, D.Y. Chandrachud, and Ashok Bhushan** delivered a foundational verdict anchoring `Article 239AA` in **constitutional morality**, **pragmatic federalism**, and **representative democracy**. While confirming that Delhi is **not a full State** and remains a *sui generis* Union Territory, the Court held decisively that **executive power is co-extensive with legislative power**: outside the three excluded entries (Public Order, Police, Land), the **LG has no independent decision-making power** and is **bound by the aid and advice** of the elected Council of Ministers. Crucially, the Bench narrowed the `Art. 239AA(4)` proviso, ruling that *"any matter"* **cannot mean *"every matter"***—the LG cannot act as an obstructionist or seek prior concurrence for routine cabinet decisions, and must invoke the Presidential reference power only in **exceptional, constitutionally substantive** situations after genuine dialogue.

> "The Lieutenant Governor has not been entrusted with any independent decision-making power. He has to either act on the 'aid and advice' of Council of Ministers or he is bound to implement the decision taken by the President on a reference being made by him... 'Any matter' cannot be construed to mean 'every matter'." — *Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India*, 5-Judge Constitution Bench, Supreme Court of India, 4 July 2018

2. **Phase II — GNCTD (Amendment) Act, 2021:** Enacted by Parliament in March 2021, this statute tilted administrative leverage back toward the LG through four changes: (i) defining **"Government"** in any law enacted by the Delhi Assembly to mean the **"Lieutenant Governor"**; (ii) requiring the Council of Ministers to obtain the LG's opinion before taking executive action on specified classes of decisions; (iii) barring the Assembly or its Committees from framing rules to inquire into day-to-day administrative matters; and (iv) directing the LG to reserve for the President any Bill that incidentally touches matters outside the Assembly's remit.
3. **Phase III — *GNCTD v. Union of India* (11 May 2023, 5-Judge Unanimous Bench — The "Services" Judgment):** Because a split two-judge bench (February 2019) had left unresolved which government controls **Entry 41 of List II ("State Public Services")**—transfers, postings, and vigilance over IAS/DANICS bureaucrats working in Delhi departments—a unanimous 5-Judge Constitution Bench (**CJI D.Y. Chandrachud**, **Justices M.R. Shah, Krishna Murari, Hima Kohli, and P.S. Narasimha**) ruled decisively in favour of the elected Delhi Government. Reasoning from the **triple chain of democratic accountability** *(permanent civil servants accountable to elected Ministers → Ministers collectively accountable to the Assembly → Assembly accountable to the electorate)*, the Court held that **Entry 41 is NOT excluded by `Article 239AA(3)(a)`**. Therefore, the elected NCTD Government possesses full legislative and executive control over all services (including All-India Services posted to Delhi) **except** officers working specifically in the three excluded fields of Public Order, Police, and Land.
4. **Phase IV — GNCTD (Amendment) Ordinance (19 May 2023) and GNCTD (Amendment) Act, 2023 (Enacted August 2023):** Just eight days after the May 2023 verdict, the President promulgated an Ordinance—subsequently replaced by Parliament's **GNCTD (Amendment) Act, 2023**—invoking Parliament's overriding legislative power under `Article 239AA(3)(b)` and `239AA(7)(a)` to construct an entirely new statutory architecture for Delhi's bureaucracy:
   - **National Capital Civil Service Authority (NCCSA):** Created a three-member permanent statutory authority to recommend transfers, postings, vigilance, and disciplinary matters for Group-A (IAS) and DANICS officers serving in Delhi, comprising: (a) the **Chief Minister** (ex-officio Chairperson), (b) the **Chief Secretary of Delhi** (Member), and (c) the **Principal Secretary (Home)** (Member-Secretary).
   - **Majority-Vote Reversal of Ministerial Primacy:** All NCCSA decisions are taken by **majority vote of members present and voting** (with a quorum of two)—meaning the **two centrally appointed IAS bureaucrats can legally outvote the elected Chief Minister**!
   - **LG as Final Arbiter:** Recommendations of the NCCSA go to the Lieutenant Governor, who may return them for reconsideration; if a difference of opinion persists, the **decision of the Lieutenant Governor is final** (`Section 45H`).
   - **Central Control Over Statutory Bodies (`Section 45D`):** Empowered the **President** (not the Delhi Government) to constitute and appoint chairpersons/members of any authority, board, commission, or statutory body established under a Parliamentary law operating in Delhi (such as DERC).
   - **Subtle Shift from Ordinance to Act:** Whereas the May 2023 Ordinance had inserted `Section 3A` expressly stripping Entry 41 from the Delhi Assembly (raising acute doubts whether an ordinary statute could amend `Article 239AA`), the August 2023 Act **omitted `Section 3A`** while functionally re-centralising control over services through the NCCSA and LG override provisions. On **20 July 2023**, a three-judge Supreme Court bench referred the Delhi Government's challenge to the 2023 law to a **5-Judge Constitution Bench**.
5. **Phase V — *GNCTD v. Office of Lieutenant Governor of Delhi* (5 August 2024, 2024 INSC 578 — The MCD Aldermen Verdict):** A three-judge Bench (**CJI D.Y. Chandrachud, Justices P.S. Narasimha [author], and J.B. Pardiwala**) upheld the Delhi LG's January 2023 unilateral nomination of **10 expert members ("aldermen")** to the Municipal Corporation of Delhi (MCD) without seeking the aid and advice of the elected Council of Ministers. The Bench drew a razor-sharp constitutional distinction: where a **Parliamentary statute** (here, `Section 3(3)(b)(i)` of the **Delhi Municipal Corporation Act, 1957**, as amended in 1993) explicitly confers a specific statutory power upon the Lieutenant Governor by name, the LG exercises it as a **statutory duty attached to his office**, falling squarely within the *"except in so far as he is, by or under any law, required to act in his discretion"* carve-out of `Article 239AA(4)`!

### Puducherry: The Article 239A Blueprint and Nominated MLAs

Originally a cluster of four geographically non-contiguous former French enclaves—**Pondicherry** and **Karaikal** (enclaved in Tamil Nadu), **Mahe** (enclaved in Kerala), and **Yanam** (enclaved in Andhra Pradesh)—Puducherry was integrated into the Indian Union through the **14th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1962**, which inserted **`Article 239A`**. Unlike `Article 239AA` for Delhi, `Article 239A` does **not** directly establish a legislature in the Constitution; rather, it is an **enabling provision** empowering Parliament by ordinary law to create a body (elected, partly nominated, or wholly elected) to function as a legislature and Council of Ministers for Puducherry—and expressly declares that **no such Parliamentary law shall be deemed a constitutional amendment under `Article 368`**.

Exercising `Article 239A`, Parliament enacted the **Government of Union Territories (GoUT) Act, 1963**, establishing for Puducherry a **30-member directly elected Legislative Assembly** alongside power for the **Central Government** (`Section 3(3)`) to **nominate up to three additional MLAs** (persons not in government service). Unlike Delhi, the Puducherry Assembly is **not barred from legislating on Police, Public Order, or Land**, though Parliament retains plenary overriding power under `Article 246(4)`.

A fierce constitutional clash between the Congress-led Puducherry Government (CM V. Narayanasamy) and LG Kiran Bedi over three Central nominations reached the Supreme Court in ***K. Lakshminarayanan v. Union of India*** (**6 December 2018**, 3-Judge Bench: Justices A.K. Sikri, Ashok Bhushan [author], and S. Abdul Nazeer). Affirming the Madras High Court, the Supreme Court held two far-reaching propositions:
1. **No Mandatory Consultation with Puducherry Government:** Under `Section 3(3)` of the GoUT Act, 1963, the Central Government exercises an independent statutory power to nominate up to three MLAs **without any legal obligation to consult or obtain the concurrence of the Chief Minister or Council of Ministers** of Puducherry; past instances of consultation had not crystallised into a binding constitutional convention.
2. **Full Voting Rights for Nominated MLAs:** Unlike nominated members of Parliament or State Legislative Assemblies in certain contexts, MLAs nominated under `Section 3(3)` of the GoUT Act, 1963 stand on an **equal statutory footing with elected MLAs**, enjoying **full voting rights** on the floor of the House—including on budgets and no-confidence motions! *(This precedent later became the flashpoint in the 2024 Jammu & Kashmir Assembly elections over 5 LG-nominated MLAs!)*

### Jammu and Kashmir: From Article 370 to Reorganisation and Statehood Debates

No chapter of Indian constitutional history has generated deeper legal, political, and strategic debate than the constitutional integration of **Jammu and Kashmir**—spanning its accession in October 1947, seven decades of asymmetric autonomy under `Article 370`, the dramatic August 2019 abrogation and bifurcation, the unanimous Supreme Court verdict of December 2023, the return of an elected Assembly in October 2024, and the continuing contestation over Business Rules and full statehood through 2026.

#### Historical Genesis of Article 370 and Article 35A (1947–2019)

Upon the lapse of British paramountcy on 15 August 1947, **Maharaja Hari Singh**, ruler of the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir, initially sought standstill agreements with both India and Pakistan. When Pakistan-backed tribal raiders invaded the Kashmir Valley in October 1947, the Maharaja signed the **Instrument of Accession** on **26 October 1947** (accepted by Governor-General Lord Mountbatten on 27 October 1947), acceding to the Dominion of India on **three subjects**: **Defence, External Affairs, and Communications** (identical to the standard Instrument signed by over 500 other princely states).

Unlike other princely states—whose rulers subsequently signed Instruments of Merger accepting the full Indian Constitution—J&K's ongoing military conflict, United Nations reference, and impending convening of its own Constituent Assembly persuaded the framers to craft a bespoke transitional bridge. Piloted in the Constituent Assembly by **N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar** (as Draft `Article 306A`) on **17 October 1949**, **`Article 370`** was deliberately placed in **`Part XXI`** (*"Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions"*) with the marginal heading **"Temporary provisions with respect to the State of Jammu and Kashmir"**. Its architecture rested on four pillars:
1. **Exemption from Standard State Framework:** Only **`Article 1`** and **`Article 370`** applied to J&K automatically; `Article 238` (governing Part B States) did not apply.
2. **Restricted Parliamentary Competence (`Art. 370(1)(b)`):** Parliament could legislate for J&K on Union and Concurrent List matters corresponding to the Instrument of Accession after **consultation** with the State Government, and on other matters only with the **concurrence** of the State Government.
3. **Presidential Application Orders (`Art. 370(1)(d)`):** Other provisions of the Indian Constitution could be applied to J&K—with such *"exceptions and modifications"* as the President specified—by Presidential Order issued in **consultation** (for IoA matters) or **concurrence** (for non-IoA matters) with the State Government, subject to ratification by J&K's Constituent Assembly once convened.
4. **Abrogation / Modification Clause (`Art. 370(3)`):** Empowered the **President** by public notification to declare that `Article 370` **shall cease to be operative** (or operate only with specified exceptions/modifications), provided that the **recommendation of the Constituent Assembly of the State** was necessary before issuing such a notification.

Following the **1952 Delhi Agreement** between Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and J&K Prime Minister Sheikh Abdullah, President Dr Rajendra Prasad promulgated the landmark **Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order, 1954 (`C.O. 48`, 14 May 1954)** under `Article 370(1)(d)`. Extending Indian citizenship, Supreme Court jurisdiction, and Fundamental Rights to J&K with tailored modifications, `C.O. 48` inserted **`Article 35A`** (added exclusively to the Constitution as applied to J&K, never appearing in the main body text—hence printed in an Appendix!). **`Article 35A`** empowered the J&K State Legislature to define **"permanent residents"** of the State and confer upon them exclusive rights and privileges regarding **state public employment, acquisition of immovable property, permanent settlement, and state scholarships/aid**, shielding those laws from judicial invalidation under Fundamental Rights (`Part III`).

On **17 November 1956**, the **J&K Constituent Assembly** adopted the separate **Constitution of Jammu and Kashmir** (effective **26 January 1957**)—which explicitly affirmed in Section 3 that *"The State of Jammu and Kashmir is and shall be an integral part of the Union of India"*—and then **dissolved itself** without recommending the abrogation of `Article 370`. Over the next six decades, more than **forty Presidential Orders** progressively extended over 90% of the Indian Constitution, 94 of 97 Union List entries, the Election Commission, the CAG, and central statutes to J&K, creating a legal paradox: critics argued `Article 370` had become a hollow shell whose `Art. 370(3)` trigger vanished in 1957, while defenders insisted the dissolution of J&K's Constituent Assembly rendered `Article 370` constitutionally permanent!

#### The August 2019 Constitutional Transformation (C.O. 272, C.O. 273 and Reorganisation Act)

Following the collapse of the PDP–BJP coalition in June 2018, J&K came under Governor's Rule (under Section 92 of the J&K Constitution) and subsequently **President's Rule under `Article 356`** (December 2018), vesting the powers of the J&K Legislature in Parliament and the executive authority of the State Government in the Governor acting under central control. Between **5 and 6 August 2019**, the Union Government executed a tightly choreographed three-step constitutional transformation:

1. **Step 1 — Presidential Order `C.O. 272` (5 August 2019):** Issued under `Article 370(1)(d)` with the concurrence of the Governor of J&K (acting as the "Government of the State" during President's Rule), **`C.O. 272`** **superseded the 1954 Order (`C.O. 48`)**—thereby **automatically terminating `Article 35A`**—extended **all provisions of the Constitution of India** to Jammu and Kashmir, and added a new clause **`Article 367(4)(d)`** (the interpretation clause) declaring that references to *"Constituent Assembly of the State"* in the proviso to `Article 370(3)` shall be read as **"Legislative Assembly of the State"** (whose powers were currently exercised by Parliament under `Article 356`).
2. **Step 2 — Statutory Resolution & `C.O. 273` (5–6 August 2019):** Both Houses of Parliament passed a statutory resolution recommending that the President abrogate `Article 370`. Acting on this recommendation on **6 August 2019**, the President issued **`C.O. 273`** under `Article 370(3)`, declaring that all clauses of `Article 370` **shall cease to be operative** and substituting a single operative text stating that all provisions of the Constitution of India shall apply to Jammu and Kashmir without any modification or exception.
3. **Step 3 — The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 (Effective 31 October 2019):** Invoking `Article 3`, Parliament bifurcated the erstwhile State of Jammu and Kashmir into **two Union Territories** effective **31 October 2019** (National Unity Day, Sardar Patel's birth anniversary):
   - **Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir** *(with a Legislative Assembly and Council of Ministers, modeled on Puducherry under `Article 239A`)*;
   - **Union Territory of Ladakh** *(without a Legislative Assembly, comprising Leh and Kargil districts)*.

#### Architecture of the UT of Jammu and Kashmir (2019 Act and 2023 Amendments)

Structurally, the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** blends Puducherry's `Article 239A` blueprint (`Section 13`) with Delhi-style central security carve-outs, creating a uniquely potent office of Lieutenant Governor:
- **Delimitation Commission (2020–2022) & Expanded Assembly:** Headed by retired Supreme Court Judge **Justice Ranjana Prakash Desai**, the Delimitation Commission submitted its final order in **May 2022**, raising J&K's directly elected Assembly seats from 83 (after subtracting Ladakh's 4 seats from the erstwhile 87) to **90 seats** (**43 in Jammu division**, up from 37; **47 in Kashmir division**, up from 46), while maintaining **24 vacant seats for Pakistan-Occupied Jammu & Kashmir (PoJK)**—taking nominal strength from 107 to **114**. Crucially, for the **first time in J&K's history**, **9 seats were reserved for Scheduled Tribes (STs)** (Gujjars, Bakerwals, Paharis/Gaddis) alongside **7 seats for Scheduled Castes (SCs)**.
- **Five Nominated MLAs (`J&K Reorganisation (Amendment) Act, 2023`, eff. 26 Dec 2023):** Beyond the 2 women nominees permitted under the original 2019 Act (`Section 15`), Parliament inserted **`Sections 15A` and `15B`** empowering the **Lieutenant Governor** to nominate up to **three additional MLAs**: **two members from the Kashmiri Migrant community** (at least one being a woman) and **one member representing Displaced Persons from PoJK**, bringing maximum nominated MLAs to **five** (raising effective voting strength from 90 to **95** if exercised—a flashpoint ahead of the October 2024 verdict when opposition parties warned that five unilateral nominations could alter a wafer-thin majority, though the Supreme Court on 14 October 2024 declined a direct `Art. 32` plea and asked petitioners to approach the High Court).
- **Legislative Carve-Out — Crucial Prelims Contrast with Delhi:** Under **`Section 32(1)`**, the J&K Assembly can legislate on all List II and List III subjects **EXCEPT Entry 1 (Public Order) and Entry 2 (Police)**. Notice the sharp UPSC Prelims trap: **Entry 18 (Land) is NOT excluded** from J&K's legislative competence (unlike Delhi's `Art. 239AA(3)(a)`!). However, **`Section 53(2)`** grants the **Lieutenant Governor exclusive discretionary authority** over Public Order, Police, **All-India Services (IAS/IPS/IFoS)**, and the **Anti-Corruption Bureau (ACB)**, while **`Section 53(1)`** caps the Council of Ministers at **10%** of Assembly strength.

#### In Re: Article 370 (11 December 2023) — The Landmark Constitution Bench Verdict

> [!case]
> ***In Re: Article 370 of the Constitution*** (**11 December 2023** — 5-Judge Unanimous Constitution Bench: **CJI D.Y. Chandrachud** [majority opinion for himself, Justices B.R. Gavai & Surya Kant], with concurring opinions by **Justice Sanjay Kishan Kaul** and **Justice Sanjiv Khanna**):
> 1. **No Residual "Internal Sovereignty" in J&K:** Rejecting the argument that J&K retained sovereign independence outside Defence, External Affairs, and Communications, the Bench held that J&K **completely surrendered sovereignty** upon signing the 1947 Instrument of Accession and Maharaja Yuvraj Karan Singh's November 1949 Proclamation adopting the Indian Constitution. `Article 370` represented **asymmetric federalism**, NOT dual sovereignty!
> 2. **`Article 370` Was Explicitly Temporary and Transitional:** Reading its placement in `Part XXI`, historical wartime context, and Constituent Assembly Debates, the Court held that `Article 370` was designed to facilitate gradual constitutional integration, not eternal constitutional separateness.
> 3. **`C.O. 272`'s `Article 367` Route Held ULTRA VIRES / INVALID:** In a critical victory for constitutional textualism, the Bench **struck down Paragraph 2 of `C.O. 272`** (which had used `Article 367`—the interpretation clause—to rewrite "Constituent Assembly" in `Art. 370(3)` as "Legislative Assembly"). A substantive constitutional provision cannot be amended covertly via `Article 367`!
> 4. **Yet `C.O. 273` Upheld as VALID Under `Article 370(3)`:** Despite invalidating the `Article 367` shortcut, the Court upheld the abrogation itself under **`C.O. 273`**! It reasoned that once the J&K Constituent Assembly dissolved in 1957, the proviso requiring its recommendation became redundant—leaving the **President's unilateral power under `Article 370(3)`** intact to complete constitutional integration (and validating the Centre's exercise of powers during `Article 356` President's Rule under the *S.R. Bommai* test of reasonableness, rejecting any strict ban on irreversible actions during President's Rule).
> 5. **Ladakh Bifurcation Upheld; J&K State-to-UT Question Left Open; Elections & Statehood Directed:** The Bench upheld carving out the **UT of Ladakh** under `Article 3(a)` read with Explanation I (since a part of a State can be separated into a UT), **declined to adjudicate** the far-reaching federal question of whether `Article 3` permits downgrading an *entire* State into Union Territories (relying on Solicitor General Tushar Mehta's solemn undertaking that J&K's statehood would be restored), and issued two binding directions: (i) the ECI must conduct **J&K Assembly elections by 30 September 2024**, and (ii) **Statehood shall be restored "at the earliest and as soon as possible"**. *(Justice S.K. Kaul additionally proposed an impartial **Truth and Reconciliation Commission** to heal decades of human rights trauma in the region.)*

#### J&K Assembly Elections (Oct 2024), Business Rules Friction and Statehood Debate (2024–2026)

Compliance with the Supreme Court's mandate produced J&K's first Assembly elections in a decade (and first as a UT) in **September–October 2024**, with results declared on **8 October 2024**:
- **Mandate (90 Elected Seats):** The **JKNC–INC–CPI(M)** alliance secured a decisive majority of **49 seats** (**JKNC 42, INC 6, CPI(M) 1**), ahead of **BJP (29)**, **PDP (3)**, **Independents (7)**, and others (**2**). **Omar Abdullah** took oath as the UT's first Chief Minister on **16 October 2024**; on **24 October 2025**, the Assembly filled J&K's **four vacant Rajya Sabha seats** (JKNC 3, BJP 1).
- **Business Rules & "Dual Governance" Friction:** Weeks before polling (**12 July 2024**), MHA amended the **Transaction of Business Rules (TBR)** under `Section 55` of the 2019 Act, routing proposals on **Police, Public Order, All-India Services (AIS), Anti-Corruption Bureau (ACB), Advocate General/law officers, and prosecution sanctions** exclusively through the Chief Secretary to the LG. Two years on, fresh departmental Business Rules under `Section 55(1)` remained unfinalised into late 2026, leaving J&K without an appointed Advocate General. Pressing its mandate, the Assembly passed resolutions on **6 November 2024** (special status/constitutional guarantees) and **28 September 2026** (demanding immediate restoration of full Statehood).
- **Supreme Court Monitoring (*Zahoor Ahmad Bhat v. Union of India*, Oct 2025):** Hearing applications seeking a fixed statehood timeline, a Bench of **CJI B.R. Gavai and Justice K. Vinod Chandran** (10 October 2025) sought the Centre's reply while noting Solicitor General Tushar Mehta's plea that security realities—specifically the **22 April 2025 Pahalgam terror attack** (26 tourists killed, triggering *Operation Sindoor*)—had to be weighed carefully before taking the final decision.

### Union Territory of Ladakh: Districts, Article 240 Regulations and the Sui Generis Talks (2019–2026)

Initially welcomed in Buddhist-majority **Leh** yet met with anxiety in Shia Muslim-majority **Kargil**, Ladakh's creation as a **UT without Legislature** in 2019 stripped the region of its four J&K MLAs, two MLCs, and erstwhile `Article 35A` domicile/land shields. Although Leh (**1995**) and Kargil (**2003**) operate 30-member (26 elected + 4 nominated) **Ladakh Autonomous Hill Development Councils (LAHDCs)** under the **LAHDC Act, 1997**, these remain **statutory administrative bodies** without law-making authority or constitutional entrenchment.

Uniting across regions, the **Leh Apex Body (LAB)** and **Kargil Democratic Alliance (KDA)**—amplified by activist **Sonam Wangchuk**—advanced a **four-point charter**: (1) **Full Statehood**; (2) **Sixth Schedule** inclusion (citing Ladakh's **>90% ST population**, earlier endorsed by the **NCST** in September 2019); (3) a dedicated **Public Service Commission** and local job quota; and (4) **two Lok Sabha seats** (Leh and Kargil separately) plus **one Rajya Sabha seat**. Between **August 2024 and October 2026**, the Centre responded through three measures short of Statehood or Sixth Schedule:

1. **Five New Districts (Announced 26 August 2024; Notified 27 April 2026):** Following MHA's in-principle nod (26 Aug 2024), the Ladakh Administration issued Gazette Notification **`S.O. 180` on 27 April 2026** (under `Sec. 5` of the J&K Land Revenue Act, 1996) expanding Ladakh from **2 to 7 districts**—**Sham (Khaltse), Nubra (Diskit), Changthang (Nyoma)** from Leh, and **Zanskar (Padum), Drass (Drass-Ranbirpura)** from Kargil.
2. **Four `Article 240` Presidential Regulations (2 June 2025):** Issued under `Article 240` read with **`Section 58(2)` of the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019**: (i) **Reservation Regulation (`No. 1 of 2025`)** — capped total reservation at **85%** (excluding 10% EWS); (ii) **LAHDC Regulation (`No. 2 of 2025`)** — mandated **1/3rd rotational women's reservation** in Leh and Kargil Hill Councils; (iii) **Official Languages Regulation (`No. 3 of 2025`)** — recognised **five official languages** (**English, Hindi, Urdu, Bhoti, Purgi**); and (iv) **Recruitment Regulation (`No. 4 of 2025`)** — introduced a **15-year domicile requirement** for Ladakh UT government posts.
3. **MHA High-Powered Committee (HPC) & Proposed `Article 371K` *Sui Generis* Model (May–October 2026):** Declining Statehood (citing strategic LAC/LoC frontiers) and Sixth Schedule (citing its North-East focus), the HPC sub-committee (chaired by MoS Home Nityanand Rai) recorded an initial consensus on **22 May / 3 July 2026** for a customised ***sui generis* UT-level elected legislative body** under a new **`Article 371K`** Constitutional Amendment (`Art. 368` special majority). However, talks **stalled on 22 October 2026** after MHA's **15 October 2026** consultation draft limited the body to making **"regulations"** requiring **LG assent**, empowered the LG to remove members, anchored the institution in a revocable **`Article 240` Presidential Regulation**, and omitted binding **land-alienation shields**—leading LAB and KDA to reject the draft and publish five negotiation documents on **23 October 2026**.

> [!debate]
> **Ladakh's Governance Dilemma — Strategic Security vs Constitutional Self-Rule:**
> - **Union's Position:** Bordering both the LAC (China) and LoC (Pakistan) with under three lakh residents, Ladakh needs unified defence command and fiscal support; `Art. 240` Regulations (85% quota, 15-yr domicile, 5 languages, 7 districts) plus a proposed `Art. 371K` *sui generis* council protect identity without statehood risks.
> - **Civil Society's Position:** `Article 240` Regulations remain unilaterally revocable executive decrees and leave **land ownership** unprotected against commercial extraction in a fragile cold desert; without entrenched legislative and land shields, frontier alienation itself weakens security.

## 3. Scheduled Areas — The Fifth Schedule

India is home to over 10.4 crore Scheduled Tribe citizens (8.6% of India's population per Census 2011) spread across over 700 distinct tribal communities. Recognising that applying uniform colonial-era property, contract, forest, and debt statutes blindly to forest-dwelling and agrarian tribal belts had historically triggered massive land alienation, bonded labour, and cultural erasure, the Constituent Assembly embedded a special protective chapter—**`Part X` (`Article 244`)**—creating two distinct constitutional regimes: the **Fifth Schedule** (`Article 244(1)`) for **Scheduled Areas** across ten mainland States, and the **Sixth Schedule** (`Articles 244(2)` and `275(1)`) for **Tribal Areas** across four North-Eastern States.

### Constitutional Philosophy and Colonial Lineage

The administrative roots of the Fifth Schedule stretch back to the **Scheduled Districts Act of 1874**, which empowered colonial governors to insulate notified tribal tracts from ordinary civil and revenue codes, later reclassified as **"Backward Tracts"** under the **Government of India Act, 1919** and as **"Excluded Areas"** and **"Partially Excluded Areas"** under the **Government of India Act, 1935**. While British rule pursued a paternalistic policy of isolation (*"keep the tribals in anthropological reserves"*), India's Constituent Assembly—guided by the **A.V. Thakkar Sub-Committee** (*Excluded and Partially Excluded Areas other than Assam*)—forged a distinctly democratic middle path: **protective integration**.

This philosophy found its classic expression in **Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru's Tribal Panchsheel (1958)** (penned in the foreword to anthropologist Verrier Elwin's *A Philosophy for NEFA*): (1) People should develop along the lines of their own genius without imposition; (2) Tribal rights in land and forests should be respected; (3) Tribal teams should be trained for administration and development without flooding their areas with outsiders; (4) Tribal areas should not be over-administered or overwhelmed with competing schemes; and (5) Results should be judged not by statistical expenditure, but by the quality of human character evolved. Within this vision, the **Fifth Schedule** keeps Scheduled Areas firmly integrated inside regular State administration while arming the **President**, the **Governor**, and the **Tribes Advisory Council** with extraordinary constitutional shields against exploitative land transfers, usurious moneylending, and ill-suited general statutes.

### Declaration, Alteration and Criteria of Scheduled Areas (Paragraph 6)

Under **`Paragraph 6(1)`** of the Fifth Schedule, the power to declare an area to be a **Scheduled Area** rests exclusively with the **President of India** by Presidential Order—NOT with Parliament and NOT with the State Governor acting alone. Once notified, **`Paragraph 6(2)`** empowers the **President**, **after consultation with the Governor of the State**, to:
- Direct that the whole or any specified part of a Scheduled Area shall **cease to be a Scheduled Area** (`Para 6(2)(a)`);
- **Increase the area** of any Scheduled Area in a State (`Para 6(2)(aa)` — inserted by the *Fifth Schedule to the Constitution (Amendment) Act, 1976*, filling an earlier lacuna);
- **Alter**, but only by way of rectification of boundaries, any Scheduled Area (`Para 6(2)(b)`);
- On any alteration of State boundaries or admission of a new State, declare any territory not previously included in any State to be a Scheduled Area (`Para 6(2)(c)`); and
- **Rescind**, in relation to any State or States, any previous Order and make fresh orders redefining the areas which are to be Scheduled Areas (`Para 6(2)(d)`).

Crucially for Prelims, **`Paragraph 7`** empowers **Parliament** by **ordinary law** (simple majority) to **amend by way of addition, variation, or repeal any of the provisions of the Fifth Schedule**, and expressly declares that **no such law shall be deemed to be an amendment of the Constitution for the purposes of `Article 368`**!

Although the Constitution itself does **not** enumerate the eligibility criteria for notifying a Scheduled Area, four well-established operational norms—codified by the **Dhebar Commission (1961)** and consistently applied by the Union Government—govern Presidential declaration:
1. **Preponderance of tribal population** (typically a substantial tribal concentration in the contiguous tract);
2. **Compactness and reasonable size** of the area (forming a viable administrative entity such as a district, taluk, or block);
3. **Under-developed nature** of the area relative to the rest of the State; and
4. **Marked disparity in the economic standard** of the tribal inhabitants compared to neighbouring non-tribal populations.

### Administrative Machinery and Governor's Special Powers

Unlike the Sixth Schedule—which creates self-governing legislative councils—the Fifth Schedule vests protective guardianship directly in the **Union Executive**, the **State Governor**, and a consultative **Tribes Advisory Council**.

#### Centre's Executive Directions and Governor's Annual Report (Paragraph 3)

Under `Paragraph 2`, the executive power of a State extends to its Scheduled Areas—meaning Scheduled Areas remain integral parts of the State's administrative machinery rather than enclaves outside State jurisdiction. However, **`Paragraph 3`** subjects this State authority to two vital central oversight mechanisms:
1. **Binding Executive Directions from the Centre:** The executive power of the Union extends to giving **directions to the State** as to the administration of Scheduled Areas (reinforced by **`Article 339(2)`**, empowering the Union to direct a State on the drawing up and execution of schemes essential for the welfare of Scheduled Tribes).
2. **Governor's Annual Report to the President:** The **Governor** of every State having Scheduled Areas must **annually**, or **whenever so required by the President**, submit a report to the President regarding the administration of Scheduled Areas in that State. In practice, however, parliamentary committees and the **Bhuria Commission (2004)** have repeatedly criticised State Governors for treating this constitutional mandate perfunctorily—submitting delayed, bureaucratic summaries drafted by State Tribal Welfare Departments rather than independent assessments of land alienation, police excesses, or PESA non-compliance.

#### Tribes Advisory Council (Paragraph 4)

`Paragraph 4` mandates the constitution of a **Tribes Advisory Council (TAC)** in **every State having Scheduled Areas**, and—in an oft-missed Prelims detail—also permits the **President to direct** the establishment of a TAC in **any State having Scheduled Tribes but NO Scheduled Areas**! *(Exercising this exact clause, **Tamil Nadu** and **West Bengal** have constituted Tribes Advisory Councils despite having zero Fifth Schedule areas!)*

- **Composition:** Not more than **20 members**, of whom **as nearly as may be three-fourths (3/4th — i.e., 15 out of 20)** shall be representatives of the **Scheduled Tribes in the Legislative Assembly** of the State. If the number of ST MLAs is fewer than the seats to be filled, the remaining seats are filled by other members of Scheduled Tribes.
- **Function and Rules:** The TAC's duty is to **advise on such matters pertaining to the welfare and advancement of Scheduled Tribes** in the State as may be **referred to it by the Governor** (`Para 4(2)`). The Governor frames rules prescribing the number of members, mode of appointment (including its Chairperson—often the Chief Minister or Tribal Welfare Minister in State practice), conduct of meetings, and incidental procedures (`Para 4(3)`). Notably, because the TAC can only advise on matters *referred* to it by the Governor and its recommendations are non-binding, critics view it as a weak consultative body compared to the elected Autonomous District Councils of the Sixth Schedule.

#### Governor's Law-Making and Regulation Powers (Paragraph 5)

`Paragraph 5` is the **heart of the Fifth Schedule**, vesting the State Governor with sweeping quasi-legislative authority that overrides ordinary federal law-making norms:

1. **Filtering Parliamentary and State Statutes (`Paragraph 5(1)`):** *"Notwithstanding anything in this Constitution,"* the **Governor** may by **public notification** direct that **any particular Act of Parliament or of the Legislature of the State** shall **not apply** to a Scheduled Area (or any part thereof), or shall apply subject to such **exceptions and modifications** as the Governor specifies—and **any such direction may be given retrospective effect**! *(Crucial Prelims Distinction: Under Fifth Schedule `Para 5(1)`, the **GOVERNOR alone** exercises this power for **BOTH** Parliamentary Acts and State Acts—unlike the Sixth Schedule where the President handles Parliamentary Acts in Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram!)*
2. **Making Regulations for Peace and Good Government (`Paragraph 5(2)`):** The **Governor** may make **Regulations** for the peace and good government of any Scheduled Area, specifically empowering the Governor to:
   - **(a) Prohibit or restrict the transfer of land** by or among members of the Scheduled Tribes in such area;
   - **(b) Regulate the allotment of land** to members of the Scheduled Tribes in such area; and
   - **(c) Regulate the carrying on of business as money-lender** by persons who lend money to members of the Scheduled Tribes in such area.
   In enacting such a Regulation, **`Paragraph 5(3)`** expressly allows the Governor to **repeal or amend any Act of Parliament or of the State Legislature** or any existing law currently applicable to that Scheduled Area!
3. **Two Mandatory Constitutional Checks (`Paragraphs 5(4) & 5(5)`):** To prevent arbitrary executive decree, no Regulation made under `Para 5(2)` takes effect unless two strict conditions are fulfilled:
   - **Mandatory Consultation with the TAC (`Para 5(5)`):** If a Tribes Advisory Council exists in the State, the Governor **must consult** it before making any Regulation; and
   - **Mandatory Presidential Assent (`Para 5(4)`):** Every Regulation must be submitted forthwith to the **President** and **shall have NO effect until assented to by the President**! *(Contrast this with Sixth Schedule ADC laws under `Para 3(3)`, which require **Governor's assent**, NOT President's assent!)*

#### Commissions Under Article 339 and PESA Linkage

Under **`Article 339(1)`**, the **President** may at any time—and **was constitutionally mandated at the expiration of ten years from the commencement of the Constitution (1960)**—to appoint a **Commission** to report on the administration of Scheduled Areas and the welfare of Scheduled Tribes in the States. Only **two Commissions** have been constituted under `Article 339(1)` since 1950:
- **First Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes Commission (1960–1961):** Chaired by **U.N. Dhebar** (submitted report October 1961). Codified the **four criteria** for Scheduled Areas, recommended an integrated **Tribal Development Block** strategy (precursor to the Fifth Five-Year Plan's **Tribal Sub-Plan [TSP]**), and urged proactive use of `Para 5` Governor Regulations to halt tribal land dispossession.
- **Second Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes Commission (2002–2004):** Chaired by **Dileep Singh Bhuria** (constituted July 2002; submitted report **July 2004**—over four decades after Dhebar!). Recommended empowering Gram Sabhas as the true fulcrum of tribal self-governance, harmonising forest and mining statutes with PESA, strengthening tribal land alienation laws, and shifting state policy from top-down welfare to rights-based autonomy.

Because `Part IX` (Panchayats, 73rd Amendment) was exempted from Scheduled Areas under `Article 243M(4)(b)`, Parliament enacted the **Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA)** based on the **1995 Bhuria Committee** report—creating a vital grassroots democratic layer in Fifth Schedule villages by empowering **Gram Sabhas** over minor forest produce, minor minerals, land acquisition consultation, village markets, intoxicants, and moneylending (examined exhaustively in Chapter 19). Alongside the **Forest Rights Act, 2006**, PESA bridges the Fifth Schedule's historic deficit of grassroots self-rule—yet weak state rules and executive reluctance continue to blunt its promise on the ground.

#### Landmark Fifth Schedule Jurisprudence: Samatha (1997) and Chebrolu Leela Prasad Rao (2020)

Two landmark Supreme Court verdicts define the outer constitutional boundaries—both expansive and limiting—of Fifth Schedule protections:

> [!case]
> ***Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh*** (**11 July 1997** — 3-Judge Bench, 2:1 Majority: **Justice K. Ramaswamy** [author] and **Justice S. Saghir Ahmad**; Justice G.B. Pattanaik dissenting):
> - **Issue:** Whether the Andhra Pradesh State Government could grant mining leases over government/forest lands located inside Fifth Schedule areas to **non-tribal private mining companies**, despite the **AP Scheduled Areas Land Transfer Regulation, 1959** (made under `Para 5(2)`) prohibiting transfer of immovable property in Scheduled Areas to non-tribals.
> - **Core Holding ("Person" Includes the State):** Interpreting the Fifth Schedule purposively alongside `Articles 38, 39(b)-(c), and 46`, the majority ruled that the statutory word **"person"** in the Land Transfer Regulation **includes the State Government**! Consequently, the **State cannot lease government or forest land in Fifth Schedule areas to non-tribal private individuals or private corporations** for mining or industrial operations, declaring all such leases **null and void**.
> - **The *Samatha* Framework:** Mining in Scheduled Areas may lawfully be undertaken **only** by the **State Mineral Development Corporation** or a **cooperative society composed exclusively of Scheduled Tribes**, with **at least 20% of net profits** mandatorily earmarked as a permanent fund for tribal health, education, water, and ecological restoration. *(Note: In ***BALCO Employees' Union v. Union of India* (2001)**, a later 3-judge bench expressed reservations about extending *Samatha* automatically to States whose own land-transfer statutes lacked Andhra's exact wording—making State-specific statutory framing critical for Mains analysis!)*

Two decades later, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** in ***Chebrolu Leela Prasad Rao v. State of Andhra Pradesh*** (**22 April 2020** — Justices Arun Mishra [author], Indira Banerjee, Vineet Saran, M.R. Shah, and Aniruddha Bose) drew a decisive constitutional boundary around the Governor's `Para 5(1)` authority. Striking down a January 2000 Andhra Pradesh Government Order issued under `Para 5(1)` providing **100% reservation exclusively to local Scheduled Tribe candidates** for school teacher posts in Scheduled Areas, the Bench held unanimously that: (1) the non-obstante phrase *"notwithstanding anything in this Constitution"* in `Para 5(1)` **does NOT empower the Governor to override Fundamental Rights (`Part III`, `Articles 14, 15, and 16`)** or breach the 50% *Indra Sawhney* ceiling arbitrarily—excluding SCs, OBCs, and unreserved candidates totally was unconstitutional; (2) `Para 5(1)` empowers the Governor only to **modify or exclude existing Parliamentary or State Acts**, NOT to enact an altogether new law creating 100% reservation; and (3) Fifth Schedule powers must operate harmoniously with `Article 371D` Presidential Orders rather than subverting them.

## 4. Tribal Areas — The Sixth Schedule

Whereas the Fifth Schedule relies upon top-down gubernatorial protection inside regular State governance, the **Sixth Schedule** (`Articles 244(2)` and `275(1)`) constructs India's boldest experiment in **constitutionalised sub-state tribal self-government**—establishing elected **"mini-parliaments"** with genuine legislative, executive, judicial, and fiscal powers inside **four North-Eastern States**.

### Genesis, Philosophy and the Four AMTM States

During Constituent Assembly deliberations, the **Gopinath Bordoloi Sub-Committee** (*North-East Frontier [Assam] Tribal and Excluded Areas Sub-Committee*, chaired by Assam Premier Gopinath Bordoloi alongside Rev. J.J.M. Nichols-Roy, Rup Nath Brahma, A.V. Thakkar, and Mayang Nokcha) observed a decisive sociological contrast between central-Indian tribes and the hill tribes of undivided Assam. Unlike mainland tribal groups, who had lived for centuries in porous agrarian contact with surrounding plains communities, the Khasi, Jaintia, Garo, Mizo, Karbi, and Dimasa hill tribes possessed intact customary legal codes, village democracies (*dorbars*, *nokmas*, *chieftaincies*), communal land tenure, and distinct Tibeto-Burman or Austro-Asiatic cultural identities. Forced overnight assimilation into plains-dominated provincial administration risked triggering insurgency and alienation along India's sensitive eastern frontier.

Defending the Sixth Schedule in the Constituent Assembly on **6 September 1949**, **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** explained that the Assam hill tribes had *"not yet assimilated into the rest of the population"* and therefore required an autonomous constitutional enclave akin to self-governing tribal reservations—ensuring self-rule over land, forests, and customs while keeping them anchored firmly within the State and the Indian Union.

**Strict Territorial Scope — AMTM Only:** Under `Article 244(2)`, the Sixth Schedule applies **EXCLUSIVELY to four North-Eastern States** (remember the classic UPSC acronym **AMTM**): **Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram**. It does **NOT** apply to Nagaland (`Art. 371A`), Arunachal Pradesh (`Art. 371H`), Manipur (`Art. 371C`, which has statutory Hill Area Autonomous District Councils created under a 1971 Parliamentary Act—NOT Sixth Schedule councils!), Sikkim (`Art. 371F`), or Ladakh (statutory LAHDCs under 1997 Act). Furthermore, mirroring `Para 7` of the Fifth Schedule, **`Paragraph 21`** empowers Parliament to **amend the Sixth Schedule by ordinary legislation** (simple majority), **NOT deemed an `Article 368` amendment**.

Across these four AMTM States, the Table appended to **`Paragraph 20`** of the Sixth Schedule presently enumerates **ten Autonomous Districts** (each governed by an **Autonomous District Council [ADC]**):

| State (AMTM) | Part of Para 20 Table | Autonomous District Councils (Total = 10 ADCs) | Key Constitutional & Contemporary Milestones |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Assam (3)** | **Part I** | 1. **North Cachar Hills Autonomous Council** *(Dima Hasao)*<br>2. **Karbi Anglong Autonomous Council (KAAC)**<br>3. **Bodoland Territorial Council (BTC)** | **BTC** added via **Sixth Schedule (Amendment) Act, 2003** (up to **46 members**); renamed **Bodoland Territorial Region (BTR)** under **27 Jan 2020 Bodo Peace Accord** (proposing up to 60 seats); **Karbi Anglong Accord** signed **4 Sept 2021**. Special expanded legislative powers under `Paras 3A & 3B`. |
| **Meghalaya (3)** | **Part II** | 4. **Khasi Hills ADC (KHADC)**<br>5. **Jaintia Hills ADC (JHADC)**<br>6. **Garo Hills ADC (GHADC)** | Covers almost the **entire geographical territory of Meghalaya** (except Shillong Municipal/Cantonment pockets). | **Tripura (1)** | **Part IIA** | 7. **Tripura Tribal Areas ADC (TTAADC)** | Inducted into the Sixth Schedule by the **49th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1984** (effective 1 April 1985); covers nearly **two-thirds (~68%)** of Tripura's land area. |

### Constitution and Structure of Autonomous District and Regional Councils

Although tribal areas remain constitutionally part of their respective States (and do not fall outside the State's executive authority), the Sixth Schedule establishes an intricate autonomous district architecture governed primarily by the **Governor** and locally elected Councils:

- **Governor's Power to Organise and Re-organise Autonomous Districts (`Paragraph 1(3)`):** Unlike Fifth Schedule areas (declared/altered by the **President**), the power to **reorganise autonomous districts** under the Sixth Schedule vests with the **GOVERNOR of the State**! By public notification, the Governor may: (a) include or exclude any area from the Para 20 Table; (b) create a new autonomous district; (c) increase or diminish the area of any autonomous district; (d) unite two or more autonomous districts; (e) alter the name of any autonomous district; and (f) define boundaries. **Mandatory Constitutional Check:** Before exercising powers (c)–(f) (increasing, diminishing, uniting, or redefining), the Governor **must first consider the report of a Commission appointed under `Paragraph 14`** (affirmed by a 5-Judge Constitution Bench in ***Edwingson Bareh v. State of Assam*, 1966**).
- **Autonomous Regions (`Paragraph 1(2)`):** If there are **different Scheduled Tribes** inhabiting a single autonomous district, the **Governor** may divide the district into **Autonomous Regions**, each possessing its own **Autonomous Regional Council (ARC)** (e.g., historically the Pawi-Lakher Regional Council in Assam/Mizoram before upgrading into three ADCs in 1972).
- **Composition and Tenure of ADCs (`Paragraph 2`):** Each standard Autonomous District Council consists of **not more than 30 members**:
  - **Up to 26 elected members**, chosen directly on the basis of **universal adult suffrage** for a **five-year term** (unless dissolved earlier; extendable by the Governor during a National Emergency or if elections are impracticable); and
  - **Not more than 4 nominated members**, appointed by the **Governor** (holding office **during the pleasure of the Governor**). *(In ***Pu Myllai Hlychho v. State of Mizoram*, 2005**, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench held that in Mizoram the Governor acts on ministerial aid and advice when nominating/terminating these 4 members under `Para 20BB` unless constitutional discretion specifically applies!)*
- **The Bodoland Territorial Council (BTC) Exception:** Inserted by the **Sixth Schedule to the Constitution (Amendment) Act, 2003** following the Second Bodo Accord, the **BTC** in Assam has a special enhanced ceiling of **up to 46 members**: **40 elected** (**30 reserved for Scheduled Tribes**, **5 for non-tribal communities**, and **5 open/unreserved** to accommodate non-Bodo residents) plus **6 nominated by the Governor** from unrepresented communities (at least two of whom must be women).
- **Executive Leadership:** Each ADC elects a Chairman and forms an **Executive Committee** led by a **Chief Executive Member (CEM)**, who functions effectively as a district-level chief minister managing devolved departments and funds.

### Legislative, Judicial, Financial and Regulatory Powers of ADCs

What truly separates Sixth Schedule ADCs from both Panchayati Raj Institutions (`Part IX`) and Fifth Schedule TACs is their **four-dimensional fusion of legislative, judicial, administrative, and fiscal powers**—often described by constitutional scholars as a **"state within a state"**.

#### Legislative Powers (Paragraph 3, plus Paragraphs 3A & 3B)

Under **`Paragraph 3(1)`**, District and Regional Councils possess direct **law-making power** over ten enumerated local domains:
1. Allotment, occupation, use, or setting apart of **land** (other than reserved forests) for agriculture, grazing, residential, or non-agricultural purposes;
2. Management of **any forest NOT being a reserved forest** *(Classic UPSC Prelims Trap: **Reserved Forests** remain under the State Government, NOT ADCs!)*;
3. Use of any **canal or water-course** for agriculture;
4. Regulation of the practice of ***jhum*** (shifting cultivation);
5. Establishment of **village or town committees or councils** and their powers;
6. Any other matter relating to **village or town administration**, including village or town police, public health, and sanitation;
7. Appointment or succession of **Chiefs or Headmen**;
8. **Inheritance of property**;
9. **Marriage and divorce**; and
10. **Social customs**.

**Mandatory Assent Requirement (`Paragraph 3(3)`):** Every law made by a District Council or Regional Council under `Para 3` must be submitted forthwith to the **Governor** and **shall have NO effect until assented to by the Governor**! Additionally, **`Paragraphs 3A` and `3B`** confer substantially expanded legislative jurisdiction (spanning over 30–40 developmental subjects including agriculture, minor irrigation, fisheries, public works, tourism, and cooperatives) specifically upon **Karbi Anglong**, **North Cachar Hills (Dima Hasao)**, and **Bodoland Territorial Council** in Assam.

#### Judicial Powers (Paragraphs 4 and 5)

Under **`Paragraph 4`**, District and Regional Councils may constitute **Village Councils or Courts** for the **trial of suits and cases between parties ALL of whom belong to Scheduled Tribes** within the autonomous district/region—and may themselves sit as **District Council Courts** hearing appeals from village courts. Crucially, however, **`Paragraph 4(1)`** and **`Paragraph 5`** impose two tight boundaries:
- Village/District Council courts **cannot try offences punishable with death, imprisonment for life, or imprisonment for a term of five years or more** under the Indian Penal Code / Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, **unless** the **Governor** specifically invests the Council or its courts with such enhanced criminal/civil powers under `Paragraph 5`; and
- **High Court Jurisdiction (`Paragraph 4(3)`):** The jurisdiction of the State **High Court** over suits and cases decided by ADC/village courts is **only as specified by the Governor** from time to time!

#### Administrative, Financial and Regulatory Powers (Paragraphs 6, 7, 8, 9, 10)

- **Social Infrastructure (`Paragraph 6`):** District Councils may establish, construct, and manage **primary schools, dispensaries, markets, cattle pounds, ferries, fisheries, roads, road transport, and waterways**, and prescribe the **language and manner** of primary school instruction.
- **District/Regional Fund & Taxation (`Paragraphs 7 & 8`):** Every District and Regional Council maintains its own constitutional **District Fund / Regional Fund** (`Para 7`; audited by the **Comptroller and Auditor General of India [CAG]** under `Para 7(4)`!). Under **`Paragraph 8`**, ADCs possess independent power to **assess and collect land revenue** and levy specified **taxes**—on professions, trades, callings and employments; animals, vehicles, and boats; entry of goods into markets for sale; tolls on passengers and goods carried in ferries; and taxes for the maintenance of schools, dispensaries, or roads.
- **Mineral Royalties (`Paragraph 9`):** A share of the **royalties accruing each year from licences or leases for prospecting or extracting minerals** granted by the State Government within an autonomous district must be paid to the District Council, in proportions agreed upon between the State and the Council—and **in case of any dispute over the share, the decision of the GOVERNOR in his discretion is final**!
- **Control of Moneylending and Trading by Non-Tribals (`Paragraph 10`):** A District Council may make **Regulations** restricting and licensing moneylending and wholesale/retail trading by **non-tribals** within the district, including capping maximum interest rates. **Double Constitutional Safeguard:** No regulation under `Para 10` may be made unless passed by **a majority of not less than three-fourths (3/4th) of the total membership** of the District Council **AND assented to by the Governor** (`Para 10(2)–(3)`), nor may a licence be refused to a pre-existing trader or moneylender operating before the regulation came into force.
- **Governor's Supervisory & Emergency Powers (`Paragraphs 14, 15, 16`):** The Governor may appoint a **Commission** to examine ADC administration (`Para 14`), **annul or suspend** any ADC act or resolution likely to endanger the **safety of India or public order** (`Para 15`), and **dissolve** an ADC or assume its direct administration on Commission recommendation for up to **6 months** (extendable up to **12 months** with State Legislature approval — `Para 16`). Note also that **`Article 275(1)`** mandates statutory **grants-in-aid from the Consolidated Fund of India** to Assam (and the other Sixth Schedule States) to meet the cost of developmental schemes for raising the level of administration of Sixth Schedule tribal areas.

### Applicability of Acts of Parliament and State Legislatures (The Classic Prelims Trap!)

One of the single most treacherous—and frequently misunderstood—constitutional rules in all of Indian Polity lies in **Paragraphs `12`, `12A`, `12AA`, and `12B`** of the Sixth Schedule, which govern how **Acts of Parliament** and **Acts of State Legislatures** apply to Autonomous Districts across the four AMTM States. Many summaries carelessly invert Assam and the other three States; below is the **exact bare-act constitutional matrix**:

| Sixth Schedule State | Bare-Act Paragraph | Applicability of STATE LEGISLATURE Acts | Applicability of ACTS OF PARLIAMENT |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Assam** | **`Paragraph 12`** | • **Para 3 matters & non-distilled liquor:** Do **NOT** apply unless the **District Council** opts in by public notification.<br>• **All other Assam State Acts:** **GOVERNOR** may direct non-application or application with exceptions/modifications. | **GOVERNOR** *(NOT the President!)* may by public notification direct that **any Act of Parliament** shall not apply or shall apply with exceptions/modifications (`Para 12(1)(b)`). |
| **Meghalaya** | **`Paragraph 12A`** | • **State Acts APPLY automatically & PREVAIL!** If any ADC law (`Para 3`) or regulation (`Para 8/10`) is repugnant to a Meghalaya State Act, the **Meghalaya State Act prevails** (`Para 12A(a)`). *(No Governor exclusion power for State Acts!)* | **PRESIDENT** may by notification direct that **any Act of Parliament** shall not apply or shall apply with exceptions/modifications (`Para 12A(b)`). |
| **Tripura** | **`Paragraph 12AA`** *(49th Amend 1984 / 1988 Act)* | • **Para 3 matters & non-distilled liquor:** Do **NOT** apply unless the **District Council** opts in.<br>• **All other Tripura State Acts:** **GOVERNOR** may direct non-application or modification (`Para 12AA(b)`). | **PRESIDENT** may by notification direct that **any Act of Parliament** shall not apply or shall apply with exceptions/modifications (`Para 12AA(c)`). |
| **Mizoram** | **`Paragraph 12B`** *(1988 Act)* | • **Para 3 matters & non-distilled liquor:** Do **NOT** apply unless the **District Council** opts in.<br>• **All other Mizoram State Acts:** **GOVERNOR** may direct non-application or modification (`Para 12B(b)`). | **PRESIDENT** may by notification direct that **any Act of Parliament** shall not apply or shall apply with exceptions/modifications (`Para 12B(c)`). |

> [!prelims]
> **Crack the Sixth Schedule Law-Applicability Trap in 3 Seconds:**
> - **Who filters ACTS OF PARLIAMENT in Sixth Schedule Areas?**
>   - **ASSAM (`Para 12(1)(b)`):** **GOVERNOR**! *(Assam is the ONLY Sixth Schedule State where the **Governor**—and NOT the President—can exclude or modify Acts of Parliament!)*
>   - **MEGHALAYA (`12A`), TRIPURA (`12AA`), MIZORAM (`12B`):** **PRESIDENT**!
> - **Why is Meghalaya (`Para 12A`) uniquely different for STATE Acts?** Because Meghalaya is an overwhelmingly tribal-majority State almost wholly covered by its 3 ADCs, the framers of `Para 12A` (inserted when Meghalaya became a full State in 1971–72) gave **Meghalaya State Acts supremacy over repugnant ADC laws**, eliminating the District Council opt-in veto and Governor exclusion power for State laws!
> - **Contrast with Fifth Schedule (`Para 5(1)`):** In **ALL 10 Fifth Schedule States**, the **GOVERNOR** filters **BOTH** Acts of Parliament AND State Acts!

### Article 244A (Autonomous State Within Assam) and the 125th Constitutional Amendment Bill

Two critical institutional extensions complete the constitutional picture of North-Eastern tribal autonomy:

1. **`Article 244A` — Formation of an "Autonomous State" Within Assam:** Inserted by the **22nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1969**, `Article 244A` empowers **Parliament by ordinary law** (explicitly NOT deemed an `Article 368` amendment under `Art. 244A(4)`) to form an **"Autonomous State"** comprising all or any of the tribal areas specified in Part I of the Para 20 Table of Assam, complete with its own **Legislature** (elected or partly nominated) and **Council of Ministers** wielding powers over specified State and Concurrent List entries (even including Police/Public Order if Parliament provides!). Originally enacted to create **Meghalaya** as an autonomous sub-state within Assam in **1970** (prior to its elevation to full statehood in 1972), `Article 244A` remains alive on the statute book and continues to fuel demands for an Autonomous State covering **Karbi Anglong** and **Dima Hasao** districts in Assam.
2. **Structural Deficits in Sixth Schedule Councils and the 125th Constitutional Amendment Bill, 2019:** Over seven decades, Sixth Schedule ADCs have succeeded remarkably in preserving tribal land and ethnic peace—yet they suffer severe **structural pathologies**: (i) **acute fiscal dependence** on State Governments (which routinely delay release of `Art. 275(1)` and devolved funds because ADCs sit outside `Article 280` Finance Commission devolution); (ii) **democratic deficit at the village tier** (power concentrates in district headquarters without constitutionally mandated Village or Municipal Councils); (iii) **severe gender exclusion** (zero mandatory women's reservation—in several ADCs women constitute under 5% of elected councillors); (iv) **non-tribal minority grievances** within multi-ethnic districts; and (v) **lack of independent election machinery** (elections conducted by State executive rather than State Election Commissions).

> [!news]
> **The Constitution (125th Amendment) Bill, 2019 — Status Through 2026:**
> - **Parliamentary Trajectory:** Introduced in the Rajya Sabha on **6 February 2019** to modernise all 10 Sixth Schedule ADCs; examined by the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Home Affairs, which submitted its **223rd Report on 5 March 2020**. Because the Rajya Sabha is a permanent House, the Bill **has NOT lapsed** and remains pending as of **November 2026**.
> - **Five Core Proposed Reforms:**
>   1. **Amend `Article 280` (Central Finance Commission):** Empower the **Central Finance Commission** (alongside State Finance Commissions) to recommend direct, formula-based grants augmenting the Consolidated Fund of States specifically for Sixth Schedule ADCs, Village Councils, and Municipal Councils!
>   2. **Elected Village & Municipal Councils:** Mandate grassroots **Village Councils** (rural) and **Municipal Councils** (urban) across Sixth Schedule districts, with devolved powers over economic planning, land reforms, and urban planning.
>   3. **One-Third (1/3rd) Women's Reservation:** Reserve at least **1/3rd of seats for women** in Village and Municipal Councils (with anti-defection rules framed by the Governor).
>   4. **State Election Commission (SEC) Oversight:** Entrust conduct of ADC, Village Council, and Municipal Council elections to the independent **State Election Commission**.
>   5. **Renaming & Expansion:** Rename ADCs as **"Autonomous Territorial Councils"** (e.g., KAATC, DHATC, GHATC) and enhance seat ceilings (aligning with the 2020 Bodo and 2021 Karbi Anglong Accords).
> - **Why It Remains Stalled (2024–2026):** While strongly championed by the **10 ADC Chief Executive Members** (who petitioned the Union Home Minister in July 2024), Assam CM Himanta Biswa Sarma (Jan 2025), and Tripura's Tipra Motha leader Pradyot Debbarma (reiterated at a New Delhi national seminar in **November 2026**), passage has been slowed by concerns from traditional institutions in **Meghalaya** (such as the Khasi *Dorbar Shnong* and traditional *Syiemships*) fearing that statutory Village/Municipal Councils could dilute customary headmen and expand State Government interference.

## 5. Special Provisions for Some States — Part XXI

While `Part VI` establishes a broadly uniform parliamentary framework across India's twenty-eight States, the framers and successive Parliaments recognized that rigid constitutional uniformity could shatter a sub-continental federation marked by acute sub-regional backwardness, sensitive border insurgencies, historic princely integrations, and distinct indigenous legal traditions. Accordingly, **`Part XXI`**—titled **"Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions"**—houses eleven calibrated provisions (**`Articles 371 to 371J`**) granting tailored constitutional safeguards to **twelve States**.

Remarkably, **none of the present `Articles 371 to 371J` existed in this form in the original 1950 Constitution**. Original `Article 371` merely provided ten-year temporary Union supervisory power over Part B States; every current special provision was subsequently inserted or substituted through constitutional amendments between **1956 and 2012** to meet concrete historical challenges—transforming `Part XXI` into the living engine of India's **constitutional asymmetric federalism**. Analytically, these twelve-State provisions cluster around **three functional objectives**:
1. **Intra-State Developmental Equity (5 States):** Preventing regional neglect and separatist resentment inside large States through Governor-monitored Development Boards and local employment/education quotas (**Maharashtra, Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, and Karnataka**).
2. **Cultural, Customary and Tribal Accommodation (5 States):** Protecting indigenous customary laws, communal land tenure, and hill-area political representation in sensitive border States (**Nagaland, Assam, Manipur, Mizoram, and Sikkim**).
3. **Border Security, Peace and Small-State Viability (4 States):** Empowering Governors with special law-and-order or peace responsibilities and relaxing `Article 170(1)`'s minimum 60-seat Assembly floor for small States (**Arunachal Pradesh, Sikkim, Goa, Mizoram, and transitional Nagaland**).

| **`371G`** | **Mizoram** | `53rd Amendment Act, 1986` | 20 Feb 1987 | Parliamentary Acts on **4 Mizo matters** (religious/social practices, customary law, customary justice, **ownership & transfer of land**) need **Assembly resolution**; min **40 MLAs**. | **OMITS "and its resources"** & exempts pre-existing Central Acts! |
| **`371H`** | **Arunachal Pradesh** | `55th Amendment Act, 1986` | 20 Feb 1987 | **Governor's special responsibility for law and order** (individual judgment after consulting CoM); min **30 MLAs**. | **NO customary-law or land shield** (unlike Nagaland `371A` & Mizoram `371G`)! |

### Intra-State Regional Equity Provisions (Maharashtra, Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Karnataka)

Five States across western and southern India enjoy `Part XXI` safeguards engineered specifically to redress historic sub-regional developmental backlogs without fragmenting existing linguistic States.

#### Article 371 — Maharashtra and Gujarat (7th Amendment Act, 1956)

Substituted during the 1956 linguistic reorganisation of Bombay State (and adapted upon the 1960 bifurcation into Maharashtra and Gujarat), **`Article 371(2)`** empowers the **President** to confer **"special responsibility"** upon the Governors of Maharashtra and Gujarat to establish:
1. **Separate Development Boards** for **Vidarbha, Marathwada, and the rest of Maharashtra**, and for **Saurashtra, Kutch, and the rest of Gujarat**, with a constitutional mandate that a report on the working of each Board be placed **annually before the State Legislative Assembly**;
2. **Equitable allocation of funds** for developmental expenditure across the listed regions, subject to the needs of the State as a whole; and
3. **Equitable arrangements** providing adequate facilities for **technical education and vocational training**, and equitable opportunities for **employment in services** under State Government control.

> [!example]
> **Operational Reality of Article 371(2) Boards in Maharashtra and Gujarat:**
> While **Gujarat** has never formally invoked `Article 371(2)` to set up statutory Development Boards (relying instead on general state planning), **Maharashtra** constituted three Statutory Development Boards (Vidarbha, Marathwada, and Rest of Maharashtra) on **1 May 1994** following Presidential Order, repeatedly extending their 5-year tenure until **30 April 2020**. After their term lapsed in April 2020, the Maharashtra Cabinet resolved in September 2022 to reconstitute them—yet as Chief Minister Devendra Fadnavis informed the Maharashtra Assembly in **June 2026**, despite five semi-official letters sent by the Governor to the Union Home Ministry between October 2022 and May 2026, formal Presidential reconstitution remains **pending with the Central Government**, illustrating how even entrenched constitutional equity mechanisms can stall without proactive Centre–State follow-through!

#### Articles 371D and 371E — Andhra Pradesh and Telangana (32nd Amendment Act, 1973)

Following intense agitations over the erstwhile **Mulki Rules** (Nizam-era domicile job safeguards in Telangana) and the **Jai Andhra** movement, Parliament enacted the **32nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1973** (effective **1 July 1974**) implementing the historic **Six-Point Formula**, inserting **`Articles 371D` and `371E`**. Upon the bifurcation of Andhra Pradesh in 2014, Section 97 of the **Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014** expressly extended **`Article 371D`** to **both Andhra Pradesh and Telangana** (notably, the Act's ten-year arrangement of **Hyderabad as common capital** expired on **2 June 2024**, leaving Hyderabad solely as Telangana's capital while `Art. 371D` continues to govern local cadres inside both States).

- **Equitable Employment and Education (`Article 371D(1)–(2)`):** Empowers the **President** (having regard to the requirements of each State as a whole) to issue Orders ensuring **equitable opportunities and facilities** for people belonging to different parts of the State in **public employment** and **education**. Specifically, the President may direct the State Government to organise civil posts into **different local cadres** for different districts/zones and specify preferential direct recruitment and admissions in educational institutions (except Central universities) for local candidates.
- **Administrative Tribunal & the *P. Sambamurthy* Ruling (`Article 371D(3)–(5)`):** `Article 371D(3)` empowered the President to establish a State **Administrative Tribunal** to adjudicate service disputes of State civil servants (excluding High Court jurisdiction while retaining Supreme Court `Art. 136` review). However, **`Article 371D(5)`** contained a startling executive-override proviso allowing the **State Government to modify or annul any order of the Administrative Tribunal**! In ***P. Sambamurthy v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (1987)**, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench **struck down `Article 371D(5)` and its proviso as unconstitutional**, holding that empowering a litigant (the State Government) to overturn judicial orders rendered the tribunal an illusory sham, violating the **Rule of Law** and the **Basic Structure** of the Constitution.
- **Central University (`Article 371E`):** Simply empowers **Parliament** by law to provide for the establishment of a **University in Andhra Pradesh**—exercised immediately in 1974 to create the **University of Hyderabad**. *(Crucial Prelims check: `Art. 371E` applies to Andhra Pradesh/Telangana's University provision and creates NO special Governor role.)*

#### Article 371J — Karnataka (98th Amendment Act, 2012)

Inserted by the **98th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2012** (effective **1 January 2013**)—the most recent addition to `Part XXI`—**`Article 371J`** addressed decades of acute human-development backwardness in northern Karnataka's erstwhile Nizam-ruled **Hyderabad-Karnataka region** (officially renamed **Kalyana-Karnataka** in September 2019, today comprising seven districts: **Bidar, Kalaburagi, Yadgir, Raichur, Koppal, Ballari, and Vijayanagara**). Mirroring `Article 371` and `371D`, `Article 371J` empowers the **President** to vest **special responsibility in the Governor of Karnataka** for:
1. Establishing a **separate Development Board** for the region (the Kalyana Karnataka Region Development Board [KKRDB]), whose annual report must be placed before the Karnataka Legislative Assembly;
2. Ensuring **equitable allocation of funds** for developmental expenditure across the region;
3. Providing **equitable opportunities and facilities** for local residents in **public employment** and **education/vocational training**—including creating **local cadres** and reserving a specified proportion of posts in State Government services and seats in educational institutions for persons belonging to the region (presently implemented through dedicated local-cadre and statewide-cadre reservations).

### North-Eastern Customary Autonomy and Hill-Area Safeguards (Nagaland, Assam, Manipur, Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh)

Five North-Eastern States hold bespoke `Part XXI` guarantees forged directly out of historic peace accords and post-colonial tribal accommodation.

#### Article 371A — Nagaland (13th Amendment Act, 1962)

Enacted via the **13th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1962** (effective **1 December 1963**, when Nagaland became the 16th State following the **1960 16-Point Agreement** between the Government of India and the Naga People's Convention), **`Article 371A`** grants India's most far-reaching customary-law and resource shield to any State:

1. **Four-Subject Parliamentary Shield (`Article 371A(1)(a)`):** *"Notwithstanding anything in this Constitution,"* **no Act of Parliament** in respect of four specified domains shall apply to the State of Nagaland **unless the Legislative Assembly of Nagaland by a resolution so decides**:
   - **(i)** Religious or social practices of the Nagas;
   - **(ii)** Naga customary law and procedure;
   - **(iii)** Administration of civil and criminal justice involving decisions according to Naga customary law; and
   - **(iv)** **Ownership and transfer of land AND ITS RESOURCES**. *(This final three-word phrase—**"and its resources"**—has been at the heart of persistent Centre–Nagaland legal friction over oil and petroleum exploration royalties, since Nagaland claims ownership over subsurface hydrocarbons whereas the Union cites Entry 53 of List I!)*
2. **Governor's Special Responsibility for Law and Order (`Article 371A(1)(b)`):** So long as internal disturbances arising immediately before statehood persist, the **Governor of Nagaland** carries **special responsibility with respect to law and order**, exercising his **individual judgment** after consulting the Council of Ministers (his decision in discretion is final, though the President may terminate this responsibility once normalcy returns).
3. **Equitable Central Grants (`Article 371A(1)(c)`):** The Governor must ensure that any Central grant made for a specific purpose is properly entered in the State's Demand for Grants solely for that purpose.
4. **Tuensang District Transitional Architecture (`Article 371A(1)(d) & 371A(2)`):** To protect the socio-economically less developed **Tuensang district** (eastern Nagaland, home to six major tribes today demanding a separate **Frontier Nagaland Territorial Authority [FNTA]**), `Article 371A` established a **35-member Regional Council for Tuensang** and laid down a ten-year transitional regime (1963–1973, extendable by Governor): (a) Governor directly administered Tuensang and ensured equitable division of central funds between Tuensang and the rest of Nagaland; (b) State Acts applied to Tuensang only if the Governor directed so on the Regional Council's recommendation; (c) Governor could make **Regulations for Tuensang** capable of repealing or amending even Acts of Parliament retrospectively; (d) a **Minister for Tuensang Affairs** had to be appointed from among Tuensang MLAs (with direct access to the Governor); and (e) during this transitional decade, `Article 170(1)`'s 60-seat Assembly floor was lowered to **46 members** (including 6 chosen indirectly by the Tuensang Regional Council). Since 1974, Tuensang participates directly in elections to Nagaland's **60-member Assembly**.

#### Article 371B — Assam (22nd Amendment Act, 1969)

Inserted alongside `Article 244A` by the **22nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1969** (effective 25 September 1969), **`Article 371B`** empowers the **President** by order to provide for the **constitution and functions of a Committee of the Assam Legislative Assembly** consisting of MLAs elected from the **tribal areas of Assam** (the Sixth Schedule autonomous districts listed in Part I of the Para 20 Table) and such other Assembly members as the President specifies, along with any modifications to the Assembly's Rules of Procedure necessary for that Committee's effective functioning.

#### Article 371C — Manipur (27th Amendment Act, 1971)

Inserted by the **27th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971** (effective 15 February 1972, shortly after Manipur attained full statehood), **`Article 371C`** creates a distinct constitutional safeguard for Manipur's surrounding **Hill Areas** (inhabited predominantly by Naga and Kuki-Zo Scheduled Tribes, accounting for ~90% of Manipur's geographical area, while the Imphal Valley comprises ~10% of area yet elects 40 of the 60 MLAs!):
- **Hill Areas Committee (HAC):** Empowers the **President** to provide for the constitution and functions of a **Committee of the Manipur Legislative Assembly** consisting solely of the **MLAs elected from the Hill Areas** (20 MLAs), along with special modifications in the Assembly's rules and Government's rules of business.
- **Governor's Special Responsibility & Annual Report:** Entrusts the **Governor of Manipur** with **special responsibility** to secure the proper functioning of the Hill Areas Committee, and mandates that the **Governor submit an annual report (or whenever requested) to the President** regarding the administration of the Hill Areas (`Art. 371C(2)`).
- **Central Executive Directions:** Extends the executive power of the Union to giving **directions to the State of Manipur** concerning the administration of the Hill Areas!

> [!example]
> **Why `Article 371C` Sits at the Heart of Manipur's Governance Debate (2023–2026):**
> Unlike Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram, **Manipur is NOT covered by the Sixth Schedule**—its six Hill Area Autonomous District Councils exist only under an ordinary Parliamentary statute (the **Manipur (Hill Areas) District Councils Act, 1971**) with minimal legislative or judicial power. Following the devastating **May 2023 Meitei–Kuki-Zo ethnic violence**, tribal organisations intensified demands for a **"Separate Administration"** or Sixth Schedule elevation, citing chronic bypassing of the `Article 371C` Hill Areas Committee by the valley-majority Assembly and stalled ADC elections since 2020—underscoring why paper safeguards without robust gubernatorial vigilance and statutory devolution fail under acute ethnic polarisation.

#### Article 371G — Mizoram (53rd Amendment Act, 1986)

Enacted via the **53rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1986** (effective **20 February 1987**, sealing the landmark **30 June 1986 Mizo Peace Accord** that ended a twenty-year insurgency and elevated Mizoram to India's 23rd State), **`Article 371G`** grants two constitutional protections:
1. **Four-Subject Parliamentary Shield (`Article 371G(a)`):** No Act of Parliament in respect of **(i) religious or social practices of the Mizos**, **(ii) Mizo customary law and procedure**, **(iii) administration of civil and criminal justice involving decisions according to Mizo customary law**, and **(iv) ownership and transfer of land** shall apply to Mizoram **unless the Mizoram Legislative Assembly by a resolution so decides**—**PROVIDED** that nothing in this clause applies to **any Central Act already in force in the Union Territory of Mizoram immediately before the commencement of the 53rd Amendment Act**!
2. **Minimum Assembly Strength (`Article 371G(b)`):** The Mizoram Legislative Assembly shall consist of **not less than 40 members** (currently exactly 40).

#### Article 371H — Arunachal Pradesh (55th Amendment Act, 1986)

Inserted by the **55th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1986** (effective **20 February 1987**, elevating the former UT / NEFA to India's 24th State), **`Article 371H`** contains strictly **two provisions**:
1. **Governor's Special Responsibility for Law and Order (`Art. 371H(a)`):** Entrusts the **Governor of Arunachal Pradesh** with special responsibility for **law and order**, exercising his **individual judgment** after consulting the Council of Ministers (his decision in discretion is final; President may direct cessation when no longer required); and
2. **Minimum Assembly Strength (`Art. 371H(b)`):** Fixes the Legislative Assembly minimum at **not less than 30 members** (currently 60).

*(Crucial Prelims Warning: Despite Arunachal Pradesh's large tribal population and strategic Sino-Indian frontier—and its colonial **Inner Line Permit [ILP]** under the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation, 1873—`Article 371H` contains **NO parliamentary veto over customary law or land** like `371A` or `371G`, nor is Arunachal Pradesh covered by the Sixth Schedule!)*

### Integration and Small-State Provisions (Sikkim and Goa)

#### Article 371F — Sikkim (36th Amendment Act, 1975)

Previously an Indian protectorate ruled by the Chogyal, Sikkim first entered the Indian constitutional orbit as an **"Associate State"** via the **35th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1974** (which inserted `Article 2A` and an earlier Tenth Schedule). Following an overwhelming popular referendum abolishing the Chogyal institution, Parliament enacted the **36th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975** (effective **26 April 1975**), repealing `Article 2A`, admitting Sikkim as India's **22nd full State**, and inserting the comprehensive **`Article 371F`**:
- **Legislative & Parliamentary Representation (`Art. 371F(a)–(e)`):** Minimum **30-member Legislative Assembly** (currently 32); **1 Lok Sabha seat** (the entire State constituting a single Parliamentary constituency); the pre-merger 1974 Assembly was deemed Sikkim's first State Assembly.
- **Sectional Reservation Including the Unique *Sangha* Seat (`Art. 371F(f)`):** To protect the rights and interests of different sections of Sikkim's population (indigenous **Bhutias and Lepchas** alongside the Nepali-origin majority), Parliament was empowered to reserve Assembly seats for specific sections—currently allocating **12 seats for Bhutias and Lepchas (BL)**, **2 seats for SCs**, and **1 seat exclusively for the *Sangha*** (elected strictly by registered Buddhist monks of recognised monasteries across Sikkim!). In ***R.C. Poudyal v. Union of India*** (**10 February 1993**, 5-Judge Bench, 3:2 majority), the Supreme Court **upheld both the BL and *Sangha* reservations**, holding that `Article 2` permits Parliament to admit new States on asymmetric *"terms and conditions"* tailored to historic realities, and that the *Sangha* seat recognised a historic socio-cultural institution rather than violating secularism or the Basic Structure!
- **Governor's Discretionary Responsibility (`Art. 371F(g)`):** Confers upon the **Governor of Sikkim** special responsibility for **peace** and for an **equitable arrangement for ensuring the social and economic advancement of different sections** of Sikkim's population—exercised in the **Governor's discretion**, subject strictly to directions issued by the **President**!
- **Continuance of Existing Laws & Judicial Immunity (`Art. 371F(k)–(p)`):** All pre-merger laws of Sikkim continued in force unless amended/repealed; President could extend any Indian enactment with modifications; and courts were barred from questioning pre-merger agreements or treaties (`371F(m)`).

#### Article 371I — Goa (56th Amendment Act, 1987)

Inserted by the **56th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1987** (effective **30 May 1987**, when Goa separated from Daman & Diu to become India's 25th State), **`Article 371I`** is the shortest and simplest article in the entire `371` cluster: it provides **solely** that *"the Legislative Assembly of the State of Goa shall consist of not less than thirty members"* (currently 40)—an exception to `Article 170(1)`'s general 60-member minimum. Unlike every other Article from `371` to `371J` (save `371E`), Goa's `Article 371I` confers **no special Governor responsibility, no Development Board, and no customary-law or land veto**.

### Mains Synthesis: Balancing Asymmetric Federalism, National Integration and Democratic Self-Rule

For UPSC Mains GS Paper II, Union Territories, Scheduled & Tribal Areas, and `Part XXI` Special Provisions should never be treated as disconnected silos—together, they embody the central genius and live constitutional dilemmas of India's **"holding-together" asymmetric federation** (a concept articulated by **Alfred Stepan** and empirically studied in India by **Louise Tillin**).

**Why Asymmetric Accommodation Has Strengthened Indian Unity:** Rather than encouraging separatism, calibrated asymmetry has repeatedly transformed violent peripheral alienation into loyal constitutional citizenship. The **Mizo Peace Accord (`Art. 371G`, 1986)** converted one of South Asia's fiercest insurgencies into one of India's most peaceful, literate States; the **Sixth Schedule** enabled Khasi, Garo, Jaintia, Karbi, Dimasa, and Bodo communities to preserve customary identity while integrating economically with the Union; and **`Articles 371D` and `371J`** channelled regional deprivation in Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, and Kalyana-Karnataka into institutional dialogue rather than prolonged violent conflict.

**Five Contemporary Structural Faultlines Demanding Reform:** Yet developments across **2018–2026** expose severe governance stresses across all three tiers of special-area administration:
1. **Erosion of Representative Accountability in UTs with Legislatures:** Both **Delhi** (under the **GNCTD Amendment Act, 2023**, where two IAS officers can outvote the CM inside the NCCSA, and whose constitutional validity remains judicially untested following the May 2025 petition withdrawal) and **Jammu & Kashmir** (where unfinalised Business Rules and LG control over AIS/ACB/Police create "hybrid governance" deadlock even two years after peaceful elections) highlight the danger of creating elected Assemblies stripped of effective executive control over their civil servants.
2. **Democratic Deficit and Unresolved Statehood Commitments:** Keeping **Jammu & Kashmir** in prolonged UT limbo despite the Supreme Court's December 2023 directive (*"at the earliest"*), while negotiating **Ladakh's** proposed **`Article 371K`** *sui generis* body without entrenched land safeguards or clear legislative autonomy (deadlocked in October 2026), strains federal trust along India's most sensitive geopolitical borders.
3. **Weakness of Fifth Schedule & PESA Implementation:** State Governors rarely exercise their independent `Para 5(1)/(2)` powers proactively; Tribes Advisory Councils act as executive extensions of Chief Ministers; and industrial/mining pressures continually collide with the ***Samatha* (1997)** principle and **PESA Gram Sabha** consent norms.
4. **Fiscal Starvation & Gender Exclusion in Sixth Schedule ADCs:** With the **125th Constitutional Amendment Bill, 2019** still languishing in the Rajya Sabha in late 2026, Sixth Schedule councils remain starved of formula-based `Article 280` Finance Commission devolution, lack elected village/municipal tiers, and exhibit near-zero women's representation.
5. **Dormant Equity Boards & Ethnic Polarisation in Part XXI States:** Non-reconstitution of Maharashtra's `Art. 371(2)` Boards since April 2020 and institutional paralysis of Manipur's `Art. 371C` Hill Areas Committee demonstrate that asymmetric constitutional text delivers justice only when backed by political goodwill and institutional accountability.

> **High-Scoring GS-2 Mains Framing (Committees & Constitutional Principles):**
> - **Cite Core Reports:** **S. Balakrishnan Committee (1989)** on Delhi's *sui generis* balance; **Dhebar (1961)** and **Bhuria (2004)** Commissions on shifting Fifth Schedule governance from top-down paternalism to Gram Sabha empowerment; **2nd ARC (6th Report)** and **Punchhi Commission (2010)** advocating clear demarcation of LG–CM powers, direct Finance Commission transfers to Sixth Schedule ADCs, and proactive, non-partisan gubernatorial reporting under `Fifth Schedule Para 3` and `Art. 371C`.

## 1. Union Territories: Creation and Administration

- **Evolution (`Part VIII, Arts. 239–241`):** Chief Commissioners' Provinces → 1950 **Part C & Part D** States → **Fazl Ali SRC (1953–55)** → **`7th Amendment Act, 1956`** & SRA 1956 (14 States + 6 UTs). **6 former UTs elevated to States:** **HP (1971)**, **Manipur & Tripura (1972)**, **Mizoram, Arunachal & Goa (1987)**.
- **4 Rationales:** Political/administrative (**Delhi, Chandigarh**); cultural (**Puducherry, DNH&DD**); strategic (**A&N, Lakshadweep**); tribal/backward/security (erstwhile NE UTs, **J&K & Ladakh**).
- **8 Current UTs (Post-31 Oct 2019 J&K/Ladakh & 26 Jan 2020 DNH–DD Merger):**
  - **3 WITH Assemblies:** **Delhi** (70 elected; own **Delhi HC 1966**; 7 LS / 3 RS), **Puducherry** (30 elected + up to 3 Central-nominated; Madras HC; 1 LS / 1 RS), **J&K** (90 elected + 24 PoJK vacant + up to 5 LG-nominated; shared **HC of J&K & Ladakh**; 5 LS / 4 RS — filled 24 Oct 2025).
  - **5 WITHOUT Assemblies:** **A&N Islands** (**Sri Vijaya Puram**, renamed 13 Sep 2024; Calcutta HC; LG; 1 LS), **Lakshadweep** (Kerala HC; Admin; 1 LS), **DNH & DD** (Bombay HC; Admin; **2 LS**), **Chandigarh** (P&H HC; **Punjab Governor ex-officio Admin** acting **independently of Punjab CoM** under `Art. 239(2)`; 1 LS), **Ladakh** (shared HC; LG; 1 LS).
- **Administrator = Agent of President (`Art. 239`):** NOT a Constitutional Head of State! **LG** in 5 UTs (Delhi, Puducherry, A&N, J&K, Ladakh); **Administrator** in 3 UTs (Chandigarh, DNH&DD, Lakshadweep). Nodal ministry: **MHA**.
- **Plenary Parliamentary Power (`Art. 246(4)`):** Parliament legislates across **ALL 3 LISTS** (incl. State List) for EVERY UT—even UTs with Assemblies; Parliamentary law prevails!
- **President's Regulation Power (`Art. 240`) Trap:** Permanent laws (force of Parliamentary Acts) apply to **(a) A&N, (b) Lakshadweep, (c) DNH&DD, (e) Puducherry (ONLY when Assembly suspended/dissolved)** + **Ladakh** (via **`Sec. 58(2)` J&K Reorg Act 2019** r/w `Art. 240`). **NOT Delhi, NOT Chandigarh, NOT J&K!**
- **Ordinances (`Art. 239B`, 27th Amend 1971):** Need **prior Presidential instructions**; lapse 6 weeks post-reassembly. **HMAC** & **AAC** serve all 5 non-legislature UTs.
- **`Art. 54` Electoral College (`70th Amend 1992`):** Only **Delhi & Puducherry** MLAs vote; **J&K UT MLAs NOT YET included**!

## 2. Special Status of NCT of Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu and Kashmir, and Ladakh

- **NCT of Delhi (`69th Amend 1991`, `Arts. 239AA–239AB`; Balakrishnan Committee 1989):** **70 elected MLAs** (0 nominated); **10% CoM cap** (**max 7 Ministers**, NOT 15%!); **CM appointed by PRESIDENT** (not LG!); **3 Excluded List II Entries:** **Entry 1 Public Order, Entry 2 Police, Entry 18 Land**; `239AA(4)` proviso = LG refers 'difference of opinion' to **President**; `239AB` = President's Rule in NCTD.
- **Delhi Judicial & Statutory Arc (2018–2025):**
  - ***GNCTD* (4 Jul 2018, 5-Judge):** *Sui generis* (not State); LG bound by CoM aid & advice outside Entries 1, 2, 18; *"any matter"* ≠ *"every matter"*.
  - **GNCTD (Amend) Act, 2021:** Defined "Government" = LG; barred Assembly day-to-day inquiries.
  - ***GNCTD* (11 May 2023, 5-Judge Unanimous):** Awarded **Entry 41 ("Services")** to elected Govt via **triple chain of accountability**.
  - **GNCTD (Amend) Act, 2023 (Aug 2023):** Created **3-member NCCSA** (**CM + Chief Secretary + Principal Home Secretary**, majority vote, quorum 2); **LG final arbiter**; President appoints statutory boards (`Sec. 45D`).
  - ***GNCTD v. Office of LG* (5 Aug 2024, 3-Judge):** Upheld LG's unilateral nomination of **10 MCD aldermen** under `Sec. 3(3)(b)(i)` DMC Act, 1957 without CoM advice.
  - **Feb–May 2025:** BJP won **48/70** (8 Feb 2025; **Rekha Gupta** CM, 20 Feb 2025); on **23 May 2025**, SC (CJI Gavai) allowed withdrawal of **7 AAP-era petitions incl. GNCTD Act 2023 plea** (Act stays operative, validity unadjudicated).
- **Puducherry (`14th Amend 1962`, `Art. 239A`, GoUT Act 1963):** **30 elected + up to 3 Central-nominated MLAs**. ***K. Lakshminarayanan* (6 Dec 2018):** Unilateral Centre nomination without CM consultation; **full voting rights** for nominees.
- **Jammu & Kashmir (1947–2026):** IoA (26 Oct 1947); `Art. 370` (Part XXI temporary); `C.O. 48` (1954) inserted `Art. 35A`; J&K CA dissolved 1957. **5–6 Aug 2019:** `C.O. 272` ended `Art. 35A` & amended `Art. 367`; `C.O. 273` made `Art. 370` inoperative; **J&K Reorg Act 2019** (eff. 31 Oct 2019) created **UT of J&K** (`Art. 239A` model) + **UT of Ladakh**. Delimitation (May 2022) → **90 elected** (43 Jammu, 47 Kashmir; **9 ST, 7 SC**) + 24 PoJK + **up to 5 LG-nominated** (2023 Amend: 2 women, 2 Kashmiri migrants, 1 PoJK displaced). Excluded List II (`Sec. 32(1)`): **Public Order & Police ONLY** (**Land NOT excluded!**); AIS & ACB under **LG discretion** (`Sec. 53(2)` & 12 Jul 2024 TBR); **10% CoM cap**.
- ***In Re: Article 370* (11 Dec 2023, 5-Judge Unanimous):** No internal sovereignty; `Art. 370` temporary; **struck down `C.O. 272`'s `Art. 367` route**, **upheld `C.O. 273`** under `370(3)`; upheld Ladakh UT; left open State-to-UT conversion; directed polls by Sept 2024 & statehood **"at the earliest"**.
- **J&K (2024–2026):** Oct 2024 polls → **JKNC-led alliance 49/90** (JKNC 42, INC 6, CPI(M) 1; BJP 29); **Omar Abdullah** CM (16 Oct 2024); Assembly resolutions for special status (6 Nov 2024) & **full Statehood (28 Sept 2026)** amid Business Rules deadlock; SC (*Zahoor Ahmad Bhat*, Oct 2025) weighed security post-**22 Apr 2025 Pahalgam attack**.
- **Ladakh (2019–2026):** 2 statutory **LAHDCs** (Leh, Kargil — NOT Sixth Schedule); LAB/KDA seek Statehood + Sixth Schedule. **5 New Districts** notified **27 Apr 2026 (`S.O. 180`)** → **7 districts**: **Sham, Nubra, Changthang** (from Leh) & **Zanskar, Drass** (from Kargil). **2 June 2025:** **4 `Art. 240` r/w `Sec. 58(2)` Regulations** (**85% quota** excl. EWS; **15-yr domicile**; **5 languages** — English, Hindi, Urdu, Bhoti, Purgi; **1/3rd women's quota** in LAHDCs). **May–Oct 2026:** MHA HPC proposed ***sui generis* body via new `Art. 371K`**, stalled **22 Oct 2026** over LG assent, `Art. 240` basis & missing land shields.

## 3. Scheduled Areas — The Fifth Schedule

- **Anchor (`Part X, Art. 244(1)`):** **A.V. Thakkar Sub-Committee** & Nehru's **Tribal Panchsheel (1958)**. **10 Mainland States** (excludes AMTM): **AP, Telangana, CG, GJ, HP, JH, MP, MH, OD, RJ** *(NOT KA, KL, TN, WB, BR, UP, UK!)*. Amended by **ordinary law** (`Para 7`, NOT `Art. 368`).
- **Declaration (`Para 6`):** **PRESIDENT** declares/alters/increases Scheduled Areas **after consulting Governor**. **4 Criteria (Dhebar 1961):** Tribal preponderance, compactness/size, under-development, economic disparity.
- **Machinery (`Paras 3–4`):** Union binding executive directions; **Governor** sends **annual/requested report to President**; **TAC** = **max 20 members**, **3/4th (15/20) ST MLAs** (mandatory in 10 States; optional by Presidential order in non-Scheduled ST States like **TN & WB**).
- **Governor's Powers (`Para 5`):** `5(1)` excludes/modifies **BOTH Parliamentary & State Acts** (even retrospectively); `5(2)` makes **Regulations** (restricting tribal **land transfer**, **land allotment**, **moneylending**; can repeal/amend Acts) needing **TAC consultation (`5(5)`)** + **PRESIDENTIAL ASSENT (`5(4)`)**!
- **`Art. 339` Commissions & Cases:** **Dhebar (1960–61)** & **Bhuria (2002–04)**; PESA Act 1996. ***Samatha* (1997, 2:1):** State = "person"; bars private non-tribal mining leases in Schedule V areas; min 20% net profits for tribals. ***Chebrolu Leela Prasad Rao* (Apr 2020, 5-Judge):** Struck down 100% ST quota under `Para 5(1)` — **cannot override Part III FRs** or 50% cap!

## 4. Tribal Areas — The Sixth Schedule

- **Anchor (`Arts. 244(2) & 275(1)`):** **Bordoloi Sub-Committee**. Strictly **4 NE States — AMTM: Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram** (NOT Manipur, Nagaland, Arunachal, Sikkim, Ladakh!). Amended by **ordinary law** (`Para 21`, NOT `Art. 368`).
- **10 ADCs (`Para 20`):** **Assam (3)** — North Cachar Hills (Dima Hasao), Karbi Anglong, **Bodoland (BTC)** (2003 Act; BTR Accord Jan 2020); **Meghalaya (3)** — Khasi, Jaintia, Garo Hills; **Tripura (1)** — TTAADC (`49th Amend 1984`); **Mizoram (3)** — **Chakma** *(Mizoram, NOT Tripura!)*, **Mara, Lai**.
- **Structure (`Paras 1–2`):** **GOVERNOR** alters districts (after `Para 14` Commission — *Edwingson Bareh* 1966) & creates Autonomous Regions. Standard ADC = **30 members** (**26 elected** for 5 yrs + **4 nominated by Governor**; *Pu Myllai Hlychho* 2005). **BTC = up to 46** (40 elected: 30 ST, 5 non-tribal, 5 open + 6 nominated incl. 2 women).
- **Powers (`Paras 3–10`):** `Para 3` laws (land, **non-reserved forests**, canal water, *jhum*, village councils, headmen, inheritance, marriage/divorce, customs) need **GOVERNOR'S ASSENT**. `Paras 4–5` village/ADC courts try inter-tribal disputes (excl. death/life/5+ yr offences unless Governor empowers); **HC jurisdiction specified by Governor**. `Paras 7–10`: CAG-audited Fund, local taxes, **mineral royalties** (Governor's final discretion), **non-tribal moneylending/trade rules** need **3/4th total ADC membership + Governor's assent**.
- **Law Applicability Bare-Act Trap (`Paras 12–12B`):**
  - **Assam (`12`):** Para 3/liquor State Acts need **ADC opt-in**; **GOVERNOR** filters other State Acts & **ALL Acts of Parliament**!
  - **Meghalaya (`12A`):** **State Acts apply & PREVAIL** over ADC laws; **PRESIDENT** filters **Acts of Parliament**!
  - **Tripura (`12AA`) & Mizoram (`12B`):** Para 3/liquor State Acts need **ADC opt-in**; **GOVERNOR** filters other State Acts; **PRESIDENT** filters **Acts of Parliament**!
- **`Art. 244A` (`22nd Amend 1969`) & 125th Amendment Bill (2019):** `244A` = "Autonomous State" inside Assam by ordinary law. **125th Bill** (pending in RS as of Nov 2026; Standing Comm. Report Mar 2020) proposes **`Art. 280` FC grants**, elected **Village/Municipal Councils**, **1/3rd women's quota**, and **SEC elections**.

## 5. Special Provisions for Some States — Part XXI

- **11 Articles (`371–371J`), 12 States** — ALL inserted post-1950 (**MaG–NAM–AA–SMAGK**):
  - **`371` MH & GJ (`7th Amend 1956`):** Governor **Development Boards** (**Vidarbha, Marathwada**, rest of MH; **Saurashtra, Kutch**, rest of GJ), equitable funds/jobs/tech-ed *(MH Boards expired Apr 2020; reconstitution pending June 2026)*.
  - **`371A` Nagaland (`13th Amend 1962`):** Assembly resolution needed for Parliamentary Acts on 4 Naga subjects incl. **land AND ITS RESOURCES**; Governor law & order individual judgment; **35-member Tuensang Regional Council**; transitional **46-seat** floor (now 60).
  - **`371B` Assam (`22nd Amend 1969`):** Assembly Committee of tribal-area MLAs.
  - **`371C` Manipur (`27th Amend 1971`):** **Hill Areas Committee (HAC)**; Governor annual report to President; Centre directions.
  - **`371D–371E` AP & Telangana (`32nd Amend 1973`; ext. 2014):** Equitable local-cadre jobs/education (`371D(5)` struck down in ***P. Sambamurthy* 1987**); Central University (`371E` — Univ. of Hyderabad). *(Hyderabad ceased being common capital **2 June 2024**.)*
  - **`371F` Sikkim (`36th Amend 1975`):** Min **30 MLAs**, 1 LS; **12 Bhutia-Lepcha + 1 *Sangha* monastery seat** (upheld ***R.C. Poudyal* 1993**); Governor **discretionary** peace/equity responsibility.
  - **`371G` Mizoram (`53rd Amend 1986`):** 4 Mizo matters need Assembly resolution — **OMITS "and its resources"** & exempts pre-1987 Central Acts; min **40 MLAs** *(has BOTH `371G` + 3 Sixth Schedule ADCs!)*.
  - **`371H` Arunachal (`55th Amend 1986`):** Governor law & order individual judgment; min **30 MLAs**; **NO customary/land veto**.
  - **`371I` Goa (`56th Amend 1987`):** **ONLY** min **30 MLAs**.
  - **`371J` Karnataka (`98th Amend 2012`, eff. 1 Jan 2013):** Governor Development Board & quota for **Hyderabad-Karnataka (Kalyana-Karnataka, 2019 — 7 districts)**.
- **Assembly Minimum Floors (`Art. 170` Exceptions):** **SAG = 30** (Sikkim, Arunachal, Goa); **Mizoram = 40**; **Nagaland = 46** (transitional, now 60).
